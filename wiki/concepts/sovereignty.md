# Sovereignty

## Definition

Sovereignty in GA names the structural position of the [[center]] that concentrates and legitimates the direction of collective attention and action within a community. It is not primarily a juridical predicate attached to a person or institution but an anthropological function emergent from the [[originary scene]]: the capacity to issue, embody, or defer the imperative that organizes a group around a shared object. Sovereignty is therefore inseparable from the scene's attentional structure — whoever or whatever holds the center holds sovereignty.

---

## Originary Derivation

On the [[originary scene]], a group of proto-humans simultaneously abort a gesture of appropriation toward a central object, converting that gesture into the first sign. This aborted imperative constitutes a rudimentary center: the object is, for the moment, untouchable by any individual will, and the community coheres precisely around that prohibition. [[Sovereignty]] names the force — initially anonymous, located in the object itself — that commands this deferral. The scene thus contains sovereignty before any individual exercises it; the center precedes the sovereign.

As the scene elaborates into ritual, myth, and eventually language, the center's imperative function is progressively personalized. The [[imperative]] speech act remains closest to this originary structure: it replicates the scene's command-compliance dyad. [[Declarative]] culture, by contrast, gradually distances itself from the imperative, as Bouvard develops in [[Programming, Power and Declarative Culture]]. The history of sovereignty is therefore also a history of the tension between declarative orders (law, constitution, norm) and the irreducible imperative core that underlies them.

Sovereignty's attentional character is foregrounded in [[The Attentional Structure of Sovereignty]], which draws on Michael Tomasello's account of [[joint attention]] alongside Gans: language presupposes the capacity to attend to the same thing, to know we are doing so, and to mark that mutuality. The sovereign is whoever can reliably convoke that shared attention — commanding the scene rather than merely participating in it.

---

## Development in the Archive

Katz and Bouvard consistently resist liberal-constitutional reductions of sovereignty to procedural legitimacy. [[The Modernity of Absolutism]] traces how the explicit theorization of absolute sovereignty emerges only in early modernity precisely because the [[center]] had become contested — absolutism is a symptom of modernity, not its antithesis. [[Sovereignty, Difference, Reciprocity, Nature, Value]] refutes the charge that absolutism means arbitrary tyranny by embedding it in differentiated hierarchical reciprocities: the sovereign is sovereign insofar as the order of differences the sovereign sustains is intelligible and productive, not merely willful.

[[Absolutist Economies]] extends this by following Graeber's account of markets: markets are created and maintained by sovereigns; money originates in imperial bureaucratic accounting and military payment. This grounds sovereignty materially — it is not a floating political predicate but the organizing condition of economic exchange itself. Sovereignty is therefore prior to the market logics liberal theory takes as foundational.

[[Sovereign Selection]] addresses the problem of succession and legitimacy without relying on divine right or hereditary transmission alone. Dynastic monarchy offers simplicity of continuity but cannot be the sole or final answer; the question of how the person to exercise absolute sovereignty is identified remains open and is partly what distinguishes a living political order from a merely formal one. [[Liberal Democracy is the Concealment of Power]] argues that procedural democracy does not dissolve sovereignty but obscures it, relocating power to undisclosed consensus networks while maintaining the fiction of popular origination.

## Key Tensions

1. **Personalization vs. Structure**: GA locates sovereignty structurally in the center, yet political practice requires a person or institution to occupy that center. The gap between the anonymous imperative of the originary scene and any concrete sovereign remains theoretically unresolved — every sovereign both embodies and inevitably distorts the center.

2. **Sovereignty and Declarative Culture**: As civilization expands the domain of the [[declarative]], sovereignty appears increasingly as the residual imperative beneath law and norm. The tension between the rule of law and the *exception* (the moment when sovereign will overrides norm) is internal to GA's account of speech-act history, not merely a juridical puzzle.

3. **Nationalism, Empire, Globalism**: [[Nationalism, Globalism, Empire]] and [[The Dialectics of Nationalism]] surface a scalar tension: sovereignty implies a bounded community, but the originary scene's logic extends to ever-wider circles of [[joint attention]]. Whether sovereignty is most coherently national, imperial, or something else remains an open question within GA political thought.
