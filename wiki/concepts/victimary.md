# The Victimary

## Definition

The victimary names the post-Holocaust moral dispensation in which the primary criterion of ethical and political legitimacy is proximity to or identification with victimhood. Within GA, it designates a structural inversion of [[sacrificial thinking]] whereby the formerly marginal or expelled figure of the victim becomes the privileged occupant of the [[center]], such that accusation of victimizing others functions as the dominant currency of social power. This dispensation is not merely ideological error but represents a specific, historically conditioned deformation of the [[originary scene]]'s logic of deferral.

---

## Originary Derivation

On the [[originary scene]], the emission of the [[aborted gesture of appropriation]] produces the first sign, and the object at the center is subsequently consumed in the [[sparagmos]] — distributed among all participants. The victim, in the sacrificial elaboration of this scene, is the surrogate who concentrates communal [[resentment]] so that the community may reconstitute itself around a shared periphery-turned-center. The foundational logic is that the sacred center both attracts and repels; the victim is the one assigned to absorb that dangerous centrality on behalf of the group.

The victimary emerges when this structure is moralized without being transcended. The victim retains the sacred charge of the center but is now imbued with moral rather than ritual authority. The community no longer sacrifices the victim to protect itself from [[firstness]]; instead, it organizes itself in permanent accusatory relation to a designated victimizer. The accusation of causing victimhood becomes the new mode of [[aborted appropriation]] — a sign that defers conflict while simultaneously constituting power for the accuser.

What distinguishes the victimary from ordinary moral concern for the vulnerable is its systematic, self-perpetuating character. Because occupying the victim position confers centrality, the logic generates competitive claims to victimhood and an ever-expanding circle of designated victimizers. [[Resentment]], rather than being deferred, is institutionalized.

## Development in the Archive

Katz traces the operational mechanics of victimary power with particular precision in [[Dismantling the Victimocracy]], where he identifies the technique of anathematization: a term of condemnation is anchored to an act of near-universal opprobrium (the Holocaust, lynching) and then methodically extended to cover ever-wider classes of behavior and persons. The result is a self-reinforcing apparatus — a "victimocracy" — in which the accusatory term functions as an asymmetric weapon: those who wield it gain immunity from reciprocal critique precisely because challenging the accusation confirms the charge.

In [[Syntactic Entanglements]], Katz frames contemporary political conflict as governed by what he calls "Auschwitz theology" — the post-war sacralization of the Holocaust victim as the absolute moral referent. This produces "White guilt" and a "capitulation to victimary blackmail" as structural features of the liberal order, not merely bad-faith performances. The concept of [[firstness]] is implicated here: any assertion of particular identity, achievement, or priority by those coded as dominant becomes morally suspect, while the claim to victimhood authorizes precisely the kind of centered assertion it nominally opposes.

Bouvard's contribution, developed across pieces including [[Enfolding the Four Freedoms]], is to show how victimary logic colonizes the concept of freedom itself, converting "negative freedom" (freedom from coercion) into a trump card held only by the recognized victim-class, while "positive freedom" — the capacity for self-authorship and centered action — is discredited as the prerogative of oppressors. This analysis links the victimary not merely to identity politics but to a broader degradation of [[center-periphery]] reciprocity within liberal institutions.

---

## Key Tensions

1. **Critique vs. Complicity**: GA's analysis of the victimary risks being dismissed as motivated apologetics for actual historical perpetrators. The tension is whether the originary critique of victimary *structure* can be cleanly separated from a defense of those the victimary currently targets — a question the archive does not fully resolve.

2. **Post-Sacrificial vs. Neo-Sacrificial**: GA holds that ethical progress consists in moving beyond [[sacrificial thinking]]. But if the victimary is a deformation of that very progress — sacralization of the victim rather than elimination of the sacred — it is unclear whether the remedy is *more* post-sacrificial thinking or a different kind of it.

3. **Institutional Capture and Resistance**: [[The Counter-Inquisition]] and [[System's Theory]] together raise the question of whether victimary logic is a contingent political formation that can be dismantled or whether it is structurally reproduced by any sufficiently complex modern institution.
