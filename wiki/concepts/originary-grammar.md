# Originary Grammar

## Definition

Originary grammar is the account of grammatical forms — ostensive, imperative, and declarative — derived directly from the [[originary scene]] rather than from empirical linguistics or rationalist universals. Each speech form corresponds to a distinct mode of relation between sign-users and the central object of attention, such that grammar is not a formal structure imposed on language from without but an index of the community's evolving relationship to [[mimetic desire]] and [[resentment]]. The history of language, on this account, is the progressive differentiation and distancing of these forms from one another.

---

## Originary Derivation

On the originary scene, the first sign is an [[ostensive sign]]: an aborted gesture of appropriation toward the central object, converted into a sign that names and defers. This ostensive form is the most primitive grammatical category — it neither commands nor predicates but simply designates, holding open a shared scene of attention. The ostensive is thus simultaneously the origin of reference and of community, since pointing toward the center constitutes both speaker and addressee as participants in a joint scene.

The [[imperative sign]] emerges as a secondary elaboration: the designation of an object is inflected toward another person as a demand for that object or its bestowal. The imperative presupposes the ostensive's scene — one commands what one can first name — but it reactivates the tension of desire within the deferral that the sign had momentarily suspended. In this way, the imperative is the grammatical form closest to the violence the originary sign deferred; it is language under pressure from [[mimetic desire]].

The [[declarative sign]] represents the most complex and most deferred grammatical achievement: a predication that attributes a quality or action to a referent without either pointing or demanding. As developed in [[Programming, Power and Declarative Culture]], the history of civilization is precisely the history of the distancing of the declarative from the imperative — from a world in which declaratives serve as narrative scaffolding for imperative exchanges, toward a culture in which declarative exchange becomes the primary mode of human interaction and the ground of shared knowledge.

## Development in the Archive

Katz develops originary grammar most explicitly in [[Idioms of Inquiry]], arguing that the [[originary hypothesis]] generates not merely new content but a new *grammar* of inquiry — new question-forms, new imperatives, new declarative habits. The originary scene thus does not merely explain language but models how any genuine intellectual practice must constitute its own grammatical idiom, its own characteristic way of moving between ostensive attention, imperative demand, and declarative settlement.

In [[Declarative Imaginary]], the focus shifts to the cultural and cognitive dominance of the declarative: we think almost exclusively in declarative sentences, measuring new utterances against a stored stock of sample sentences. This creates what Katz calls the "declarative imaginary" — the backgrounded normative field within which thought operates. The implication for originary grammar is that the declarative's apparent neutrality conceals its distance from the originary scene; naturalized declaratives can themselves become imperatives in disguise, a point that connects to the analysis of [[resentment]] and [[victimary]] discourse.

Bouvard's contributions, particularly in [[Syntactic Entanglements]], press originary grammar into contact with contemporary political pathology: when declarative culture loses its capacity to defer mimetic conflict, syntax itself becomes a battlefield. The entanglement of ostensive, imperative, and declarative in online and political discourse — where what appears as declarative statement functions as imperative demand or ostensive accusation — shows originary grammar to be a diagnostic tool, not merely a theoretical framework. The [[Grammar of Desire and Resentment]] extends this further, examining how desire "pulverizes" the thetic (propositional/declarative) dimension of language, returning speakers toward the imperative-ostensive complex.

## Key Tensions

1. **Derivation vs. Description**: Is originary grammar a *genetic* account (explaining how grammar arose) or a *structural* one (explaining what grammar is)? The archive oscillates between these, and it is unclear whether the two projects are fully compatible.

2. **Universality vs. Historical Variation**: If the three speech forms are universal, how does originary grammar account for the significant cross-linguistic variation in their realization? [[First Words]] raises the question of universal semantic primitives, but the relationship between Wierzbicka's Natural Semantic Metalanguage and originary grammar remains underdeveloped.

3. **Declarative Autonomy and Its Limits**: [[Programming, Power and Declarative Culture]] posits the distancing of the declarative from the imperative as civilizational progress — yet the [[Declarative Imaginary]] suggests declaratives are always haunted by imperative and ostensive pressures. The normative status of the declarative within GA remains contested.
