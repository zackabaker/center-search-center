## Summary

This post examines René Girard's revolutionary interpretation of Christ's Crucifixion as a desacralizing revelation of the [[scapegoat mechanism]] that undergirds human social order. Rather than viewing the Passion through traditional substitutionary atonement theology (which assumes a vengeful God), Girard sees it as the world-historical unveiling of how sacrifice and scapegoating have structured civilization. The post notes a profound irony: the mechanism Girard argues originated our concept of god is itself exposed when God becomes incarnate and is scapegoated. This revelation makes continued ritualized violence cognitively unconscionable, inaugurating a new moral age grounded in recognition rather than mystification.

## Key Concepts

- [[Scapegoat mechanism]]: The hidden foundation of social order and religious practice; the structural use of victim-blaming to maintain collective cohesion
- [[Sacred violence]]: Ritualized harm mystified as divine necessity; the falsely sacred undergirding traditional religion and politics
- [[Mimetic desire]]: The contagious power of desire and violence that Girard argues makes scapegoating "mesmerizing"
- [[Revelation]]: The Crucifixion as cognitive unveiling of things "hidden since the foundation of the world"
- [[Substitutionary atonement]]: Traditional theology positing Christ's death as retributive payment; critiqued for its assumption of divine vengeance
- [[Demystification]]: The rational, non-mystical recognition that violence is human in origin, not divinely mandated

## Notable Passages

> "The revelation of the truth of scapegoating upsets that structure and inaugurates a new age. The mimetic power of scapegoating and sacrifice is so powerful and mesmerizing that only God, in the person of the Son, could reveal its truth."

> "By demystifying 'sacred' violence as human in origin, the Crucifixion confronts humans with their own violence, allowing them to recognize the true God, and seek salvation through faith."

> "The Passion is a heuristic, in a radical sense: a revelation that makes continued scapegoating unconscionable."

## Questions Raised

1. If the Passion's revelatory power depends on its "spectacular paradox," what happens to Girard's theory in a secular age where this paradox loses its persuasive force? Does demystification require ongoing reference to divine authority?

2. How does recognizing the scapegoat mechanism as foundational to all prior civilization square with actual political behavior—i.e., why do modern societies continue scapegoating after the Crucifixion allegedly made it "unconscionable"?

3. What is the relationship between cognitive recognition of the mechanism and actual moral transformation? Is Girard ultimately arguing for a rationalist or faith-based salvation?
