## Summary

This essay diagnoses modernity's foundational hatred of [[the given]]—both what simply exists and what is received as gift. The author argues that progressives and conservatives alike rage against natural differences and inequalities, treating all of reality as subject to political reconstruction and control. Drawing on [[gift economy]] theology and Heideggerian critique, the text contends that human freedom consists in the capacity to accept or reject gifts already received, and that even market economies remain adjuncts to a primordial gift structure that modernity refuses to acknowledge.

## Key Concepts

- [[The given]] — What simply exists, remains, and persists beyond analysis or transformation; also what is received as gift
- [[Gift economy]] — The originary structure wherein the God-object distributes peace and existence to the community; foundation of all exchange
- [[Appropriation]] — The modern gesture of taking for oneself through labor; paradoxically depends on givens one cannot appropriate
- [[Standing reserve]] — Heidegger's term for modernity's reduction of everything (people, nature, ideas) to exploitable resources
- [[Resentment]] — The affective motor driving rejection of what is given in favor of endless reconstruction
- [[Freedom]] — Capacity to accept or reject gifts, rather than autonomous self-creation
- [[Nothing is accidental]] — Communist maxim expressing the totalizing impulse to politicize all existence

## Notable Passages

"You can listen to progressives rage against any suggestion that some differences, including those that generate inequalities, might simply be there, and, for that matter, be no big deal."

"Freedom is really nothing more than the capacity to reject or accept any particular gift—just as language is, ultimately listening, sifting through a lot of noise and ordering the given."

"Even for Locke, only what one has appropriated through labor is genuinely one's own 'property.' But the powers with which you are able to 'appropriate' must have been 'given.'"

## Questions Raised

1. If all appropriation depends on prior givens, how can we distinguish between legitimate human labor and the totalizing impulse to remake reality? Where is the line?

2. Can a genuinely [[gift economy]] function in mass societies, or does scale necessarily corrupt the asymmetrical reciprocity the author describes?

3. Is the hatred of [[the given]] intrinsic to modernity itself, or could a post-modern or post-progressive thought recover acknowledgment of gift without reactionary regression?
