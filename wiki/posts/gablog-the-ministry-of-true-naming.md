## Summary

This essay proposes a thought experiment in "formalist reactionary theory": what if powerful institutions—media conglomerates, banks, universities—were granted explicit official titles corresponding to the power they already exercise? The paradox revealed is that liberal institutions depend entirely on *not* being officially recognized as wielding power; the moment they claim direct authority, they lose the ideological cover that enables their actual influence. The essay exposes how all liberal power operates through denial and deniability, with each institution claiming to defend the people against illegitimate power while evading reciprocal accountability.

## Key Concepts

- [[Formalism]]: A method of thought-experimentation that makes visible the structure of power by proposing its explicit naming
- [[Liberal Power]]: Authority exercised through deniability and indirection—never in its own name but always in the name of people, law, or truth
- [[The Public/Private Distinction]]: A fraudulent boundary that allows powerful private institutions to operate as if they were neutral arbiters rather than sovereign actors
- [[Reciprocal Relations]]: The genuine basis for accountability that liberalism actively subverts through short-term institutional interests
- [[Accountability]]: The systematic evasion of which depends on power remaining unnamed and diffused across competing institutions
- [[Musical Chairs of Power]]: The circular structure wherein each institution claims to check the illegitimate authority of others while escaping scrutiny itself

## Notable Passages

> "The power exercised by these institutions is, in fact, in flux, and therefore difficult to 'entitle,' because they in turn delegate power to those they depend on."

> "All forms of power under liberalism depend upon the musical chairs game of power—no one ever really does anything on their own authority."

> "Uncertain power equals uncertain accountability. The NY Times, Chase Manhattan, Harvard, etc., strictly speaking don't owe anybody anything."

## Questions Raised

1. If liberalism requires the *invisibility* of power to function, what institutional forms could make power both visible and legitimate without collapsing into either tyranny or paralysis?

2. How might genuine [[reciprocal relations]] be enforced or incentivized within institutions whose short-term interests systematically encourage their subversion?

3. Does the formalist thought experiment suggest that pre-liberal or post-liberal orders—where power is openly claimed—might paradoxically be more accountable than systems that deny their own authority?
