## Summary

This post examines desecration as a political and anthropological strategy, using Ann Coulter's controversial rhetoric as a case study. The author argues that modern liberalism functions as a religion with its own sacred structures, and that the most effective challenge to any religion is sustained violation of its taboos rather than rational argument. He observes that the [[victimary]] system's power depends on credible victims, a resource now exhausted through grotesque overuse. The key question becomes whether desecration can be deployed strategically without undermining the [[egalitarian]] principles that bind the sacred center itself.

## Key Concepts

- [[Victimary]]: A system dependent on the production of victim status and grievance narratives as sources of social power
- [[Sacred]]: The socially binding center that, when unacknowledged, morphs into heretical forms
- [[Desecration]]: Sustained, egregious violation of taboos that weakens their hold once consequences fail to materialize
- [[Egalitarian precepts]]: The foundational principles of the sacred center that must be protected from corrosive desecration
- [[Taboo]]: Social prohibition whose power depends on collective belief and enforcement
- [[Liberal religion]]: The treating of modern progressive ideology as a functional religion with dogma, saints, and doctrine

## Notable Passages

"The real way to fight a religion is not to 'argue' with it or provide a more plausible view of the world; far more effective is acts of sustained and egregious desecration."

"All liberalism has is the resource of those who can plausibly present themselves as victims in a sustained way, and that resource is running out quickly."

"There's nothing wrong with such pales, as long as we can explain where we put them and why, but the purpose of the victimary is to stigmatize."

## Questions Raised

1. How do we distinguish between desecration that weakens genuinely destructive sacred structures versus desecration that erodes the [[egalitarian]] consensus necessary for any functioning society?

2. If the victimary system's collapse is inevitable due to resource exhaustion, what new sacred forms will emerge to fill the space it occupied, and will they be more or less conducive to human flourishing?

3. Can GA itself remain neutral ground for examining desecration, or does the very act of anthropological analysis implicitly validate certain taboos while exposing others?
