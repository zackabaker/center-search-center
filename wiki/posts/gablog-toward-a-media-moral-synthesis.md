## Summary

This essay explores how the shift from orality to literacy fundamentally transformed human language and moral consciousness. Drawing on Haun Saussy's concept of [[ergodic]] systems, the author argues that oral languages are finite and formulaic, while written languages become infinitely generative through metalinguistic rules. This transition enabled new moral vocabularies and the ability to report speech literally rather than mimetically, establishing the conditions for [[Axial Age]] moral innovations and the emergence of [[declarative culture]].

## Key Concepts

- [[Ergodic language]]: A finite signifying system where all elements recur given sufficient time; characteristic of oral communication
- [[Orality]]: Language condition where speech is formulaic, mimetic, and bound to traditional roles and ritual models
- [[Literacy]]: The introduction of writing systems that infinitize language through metalinguistic rules and grammatical definitions
- [[Reported speech]]: The problem of telling what someone else said; solved mimetically in orality, lexically in literacy
- [[Metalanguage]]: The expansion of words representing variations on concepts like "say" and "think," enabling indirect discourse
- [[Axial Age]]: Historical period of moral innovation enabled by literate metalinguistic consciousness
- [[Declarative culture]]: The culture made possible by writing's capacity to represent thought and speech explicitly

## Notable Passages

> "Languages in a condition of orality are constituted by a finite number of 'formulas,' or 'commonplaces,' or 'clichés,' or 'chunks,' that are not infinitely recombinable."

> "Individual speakers are conduits of a language that flows through them, and that they are 'within'—and the language of ritual and myth would, further, be the model and resource for everyday speech."

> "When writing, you can't imitate the way someone said something, so everything apart from the actual words needs to be represented lexically."

## Questions Raised
