## Summary

This post analyzes "Bad Lip Reading"—a creative practice of muting videos and inferring dialogue from lip movements—as a profound mode of inquiry into language, meaning-making, and character. Rather than mere satire, the technique reveals how [[linguistic indeterminacy]] and [[originary mistakenness]] structure both speech and perception. The author argues that this playful procedure exposes vulnerabilities and unexpected strengths in political figures, and suggests it may become a vital cultural practice for marking our shared dependence on meaning's fundamental instability.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Originary mistakenness]]** — the productive error inherent in all language and meaning-making, where misreading generates insight
- **[[Linguistic indeterminacy]]** — the irreducible ambiguity in how sounds map to meaning, especially visible in lip-reading
- **[[Gestural idiom]]** — a speaker's characteristic patterns of bodily movement, usually subsumed by semantic content but revealed through lip-reading
- **[[Scenic reading]]** — attention to the visual/bodily scene of utterance rather than transparent semantic extraction
- **[[Discovery procedure]]** — a method of inquiry that reveals otherwise hidden aspects of character or communication
- **[[Dubbing effect]]** — how viewers "hear" what subtitles/translations say, even when lip movements don't align

## Questions Raised

1. How does the [[scenic]] dimension of language—what we *see* in utterance—constitute a separate channel of meaning-making from semantic content, and what happens when we privilege it?

2. If [[originary mistakenness]] is constitutive of all meaning, does lip-reading simply make visible what is always already true about language itself?

3. Could this practice become a genuine political tool—a way of assessing candidates not through their intended messages but through their gestural and phonetic vulnerability?
