## Summary

This post examines the relationship between [[gift economy]], [[informal economy]], and formal capitalist structures, using Hernando de Soto's work on extralegal property holdings. The informal economy is rooted not in barter but in **reciprocal personal recognition**—everyone knows who owns what because of community knowledge. Formalizing this wealth requires documenting local histories, norms, and resentments, which when made public and subject to scrutiny, creates a bridge between gift and money economies while potentially alienating the very idioms that sustained them.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Gift economy]]**: Economy based on reciprocal personal recognition and obligation rather than formal legal title
- **[[Informal economy]]**: Marginal economic activity held illegally or extralegal; possessions lack legal title and cannot serve as capital
- **[[Idiom]]**: Local, historically-rooted norms, resentments, and sense of justice embedded in community recognition
- **[[Legal title]]**: Formal documentation enabling property to function as collateral and capital
- **[[Reciprocal recognition]]**: Personal knowledge and mutual acknowledgment within a community that guarantees possession
- **[[Alienation]]**: The distancing effect when tacit knowledge becomes articulated and subject to public scrutiny

## Notable Passages

"It is this reliance upon reciprocal personal recognition that seems to me to root the informal economy in the gift economy."

"In order to give legal title to the vast wealth held in the informal economy, one would have to...speak with the inhabitants to find out who owns what, and therefore to gauge their reliability, assess their own conflicting interests."

"All that knowledge, all those resentments, those tacit norms, are articulated and made public, subject to a new kind of scrutiny."

## Questions Raised

1. When informal economies are formalized through documentation and public scrutiny, does the very act of articulation destroy the reciprocal recognition that originally sustained them—and if so, can formalization ever truly "bridge" these economies?

2. What role does [[resentment]] play in determining which local norms become enshrined in formal law versus which are erased or overwritten during this process of articulation?

3. How might de Soto's model apply to digital or non-territorial forms of property and recognition in contemporary economies?
