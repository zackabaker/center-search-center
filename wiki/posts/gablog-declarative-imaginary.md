## Summary

The declarative sentence functions as a [[cease and desist order]] that simultaneously forbids and intensifies desire for its topic. Drawing on [[originary grammar]] and Gans's aesthetics of the originary scene, the author argues that sentences work by deferring access to a desired object while framing it in language, creating an oscillation between the forbidden real object and its representation. This structure mirrors the originary scene's moment of hesitation and generates what might be called the "[[declarative imaginary]]"—the shared representational stock through which communities think and coordinate. The imagination itself extends and completes what is cut off from view, making language fundamentally tied to incompleteness and shared contemplation rather than possession.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Declarative sentence]]**: A linguistic unit with topic and comment that forbids direct access while making an object contemplatable.
- **[[Originary grammar]]**: The grammar of human language grounded in the originary scene's deferral of violence and desire.
- **[[Cease and desist order]]**: The sentence's fundamental function to hold back imperative demands and prevent immediate appropriation.
- **[[Aborted gesture of appropriation]]**: The first sign as simultaneously indicating and forbidding the central object.
- **[[Declarative imaginary]]**: The shared stock of sentences and representational patterns through which communities think and coordinate.
- **[[Imagination]]** (Collingwood): The mental extrapolation and completion of what is cut off or framed by actual vision.
- **[[Oscillation]]**: The movement between object and sign, or between desire and its deferral, that generates aesthetic effect.
- **[[Contemplation]]**: The act of trying new comments on a topic without grasping, changing, or possessing it.

## Notable Passages

> "The sentence is a cease and desist order... The sentence forbids the interlocutor to press the demand further because the referent of the topic has been rendered off limits due to circumstances beyond our control."

> "The imagination is always an extended, extrapolated and completed representation of something 'cut off' by whatever is 'framing' it in your actual vision."

> "Possessing the actual object would abolish the object as desired through the sentence, while the sentence renders the actual object inaccessible."
