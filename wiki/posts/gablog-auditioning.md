## Summary

This essay examines how power operates through institutional "selection processes" rather than through the explicit ideologies or merit of individual actors. Drawing on analysis of three levels—individual actors, power institutions, and the political system—the author argues that cultural and intellectual trends are determined by patronage networks, funding decisions, and institutional promotion rather than inherent historical logic or theoretical superiority. The text confronts the paradox that all would-be cultural producers, regardless of political orientation, must "audition" for institutional legitimacy while simultaneously resisting the very systems that would absorb and neutralize their work.

## Key Concepts

- [[Power institutions]] — Less visible mediating bodies that select, fund, and promote individual actors while serving state interests
- [[Selection process]] — The mechanisms by which institutions distribute resources, authority, and platform to shape cultural production
- [[Anarchist ontology]] — A decentralizing strategy that pulverizes subjects into "free radicals" while centralizing institutional control
- [[Individual actors]] — Activists, academics, and cultural producers whose visibility masks their dependence on institutional selection
- [[Auditioning]] — The inescapable dynamic wherein all cultural producers must seek institutional validation to achieve influence
- [[Sovereignty]] — The state structure toward which competing institutions orient their covert warfare and influence campaigns

## Notable Passages

> "Why does one particular literary theory, or trend within political science, or a new 'studies' program achieve lift off and start dominating all the journals and producing a whole new constellation of 'stars'? There's always an attempt to explain such things in terms of some historical process or social need… but it's also always obvious that things could have gone in a very different direction."

> "To be on the cutting edge of anarchist ontology—that is, to increase the power of the centralizing authority by further pulverizing subjects into free radicals, organizing apparently spontaneously and resistantly but actually in a highly choreographed manner."

> "We're all auditioning for power. No one says or does anything that they don't hope and imagine will become official doctrine and supported practice at some point."

## Questions Raised

1. If all cultural production necessarily involves "auditioning" for institutional power, is there a genuine outside to the selection process—or only degrees of institutional proximity?

2. How might one simultaneously resist institutional absorption while securing the platform and resources necessary to contest dominant narratives?

3. What distinguishes reactionary institutional critique from the leftist critique Kostelanetz pioneered, and does the distinction matter given their shared structural analysis?
