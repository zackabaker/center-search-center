## Summary

This post grounds morality in the act of linguistic reference itself, tracing moral obligation back to the [[originary scene]] where the group first deferred appropriation of a desired object through mutual indication. Gans argues that any reference—no matter how trivial—implicates us in an [[attentional space]] and adds to the "interestingness" of a shared center. True moral reasoning operates at the margins where inherited traditions fail to apply, and the fundamental moral act is sustaining [[linguistic presence]] by keeping a shared center in view, even if that means disrupting degraded conversation or walking away entirely.

## Key Concepts

- [[Originary scene]]: The inaugural human event where desire, deferral, and mutual indication create the first center of attention
- [[Deferral]]: The restraint from appropriation that marks all moral and linguistic action
- [[Attentional space]]: The shared focus created whenever someone refers to something, implicating all parties in a common center
- [[Center]]: The object of collective attention that organizes moral and linguistic community
- [[Linguistic presence]]: The sustained acknowledgment of a shared, intelligible referent among speakers
- [[Moral reasoning]]: Thinking that emerges at the margins of tradition, presupposing only center, origin, and deferral

## Notable Passages

> "Even a casual glance at something or a trivial reference is marked by the history of deferral and discipline initiated on the originary scene."

> "The most fundamentally moral act, I am assuming, is to sustain linguistic presence by keeping that shared center in view."

> "Such an act falls outside of the boundaries of determination of truth/falsity, but it is undeniably meaningful."

## Questions Raised

1. How does the distinction between sustaining a degraded center and walking away to create a new one avoid becoming purely subjective judgment about which conversations are "worth" sustaining?

2. If moral reasoning must operate beyond inherited traditions at their margins, does this privilege the solitary thinker over the community, and if so, isn't that itself a kind of paradox for GA's originary communalism?

3. What prevents the act of "adding interestingness" to a shared center from becoming mere performative attention-seeking rather than genuinely moral reference?
