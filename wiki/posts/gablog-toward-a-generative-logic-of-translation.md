## Summary

This essay proposes replacing traditional Aristotelian logic—which relies on fixed definitions and metaphysical assumptions—with a generative logic grounded in Anna Wierzbicka's natural semantic metalanguage (NSM) primes. Rather than treating words as stable through arbitrary definition, the primes derive their meaning from their near-universal presence across all human languages. The author argues that these irreducible words function by limiting each other's use and represent the minimal declarative infrastructure needed to manage conflict without violence. This approach opens a path toward understanding translation and language origins as fundamentally tied to the [[originary scene]].

## Notable Passages

> "Any definition relies upon metaphysical or anthropological assumptions that can't be 'proven' within the system itself."

> "The primes are the minimal language needed to talk in and about a world in which imperatives can be refused or disappointed without increasing the likelihood of inconclusive and destructive conflict."

> "One thing is constant across all three uses: someone 'thinks' when one doesn't 'know.'"

## Questions Raised

1. How do we map the "paths" from ostensive, imperative, and interrogative modes to the declarative without importing modern linguistic assumptions back into originary reconstruction?

2. If primes limit each other rather than standing alone, does their universality depend on a specific anthropological scene, and does that scene precede or follow declarative language?

3. Can a logic built on "hesitation" and refusal actually adjudicate disputes, or does it merely formalize the conditions under which disputes remain non-violent?
