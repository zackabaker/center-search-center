## Summary

This paper reframes cognitive theory by centering [[intersubjectivity]] and [[linguistic presence]] rather than individual minds encountering objects. Drawing on Tomasello's "joint attention" and Gans's elementary linguistic forms, the author argues that cognition itself should be understood as operations within language that presuppose shared intentionality. The core insight is that thinking, feeling, and remembering are fundamentally linguistic and idiomatic acts oriented toward conserving [[linguistic presence]] through the [[lowering of the threshold of significance]]—progressively expanding what counts as significant in a shared world.

## Key Concepts

- [[Linguistic presence]]: The shared availability of meaning that language must preserve across utterances and speakers
- [[Joint attention]]: Tomasello's concept of minds coordinating focus on objects; foundational to originary understanding of human origin
- [[Elementary linguistic forms]]: The ostensive, imperative, and declarative—successive linguistic modes that Gans analyzes as historical and cognitive structures
- [[Lowering the threshold of significance]]: The movement from rare significant objects to granting significance to ever more objects; a mechanism for conserving linguistic presence
- [[Interdividual cognition]]: Cognition understood not as individual but as constituted between selves, always anthropomorphizing social centers
- [[Declarative culture]]: The cultural order enabled by the declarative form, which can refer to absent or hypothetical objects
- [[Center]]: The focal point constituted from collective desire and resentment around which shared intentionality organizes

## Notable Passages

> "The most basic thing we do as language users is conserve linguistic presence. Another key concept is the 'lowering of the threshold of significance'—the movement from one significant object in a world comprised of insignificant ones to a granting of less and less significance to more and more objects."

> "We could say that lowering the threshold of significance is the way we conserve linguistic presence: what threatens linguistic presence is the loss of a shared center that we could point to; by lowering the threshold of significance we place a newly identified object at that center."

> "Cognition, of what we do when we think, feel, remember and so on [should be understood] directly in linguistic terms, as operations and movements within language, in terms that always already imply shared intentionality."

## Questions Raised

1. If lowering the threshold of significance is how we conserve linguistic presence, what mechanisms determine which new objects get admitted to significance, and how do disagreements about significance generate conflict?

2. How does the understanding of cognition as idiomatic linguistic operation account for the experience of pre-linguistic or non-linguistic consciousness (sensation, emotion, intuition)?

3. What is the relationship between individual cognitive development and the historical emergence of linguistic forms—does ontogeny recapitulate the originary phylogeny of language?
