## Summary

This essay traces the poststructuralist critique of humanism—the Western ideology centering individual dignity, intentionality, and responsibility—and asks how AI forces a reconsideration of that critique. The author argues that GA, despite its originary hypothesis, remains too humanist and existentialist, whereas Center Study properly displaces human essence in favor of a [[relation to the center]] that cannot itself be human. The tension between secular thought and religious reassertions of transcendence, and between structuralist critiques of agency and poststructuralism's search for subaltern agencies, frames the contemporary problem of what "human" means when machines increasingly simulate human functions.

## Key Concepts

- [[Humanism]] — Western ideology granting dignity and intentionality to individuals, legitimating liberal democracy and free-market economics
- [[Poststructuralism]] — Critique of humanism via language, unconscious, desire, and structuring orders beyond individual control
- [[Originary hypothesis]] — GA's account of meaning's emergence, which secularizes but paradoxically remains humanist in centering human representation
- [[Relation to the center]] — Center Study's corrective: not human essence but positional relation to the sacred center governing all
- [[Agency]] — The persistent problem in post-structuralist thought: how subjects act when constitutively overdetermined
- [[Secularism]] — Removal of God from the picture, making the human measure; creates political reversals favoring religious institutions
- [[Existentialism]] — Emphasis on choice, intentionality, responsibility; persists in Gans's use of Sartrean *neant* for deferral

## Notable Passages

> "Humanism was attractive...because it purported to confer dignity upon the person, the individual, in a way that comported with human rights, rule of law and related US-order sanctioned concepts."

> "The originary hypothesis does, indeed, cut the divine...out of the equation and in that sense would be the most 'humanist' mode of thinking imaginable; but in place of the divine or holy we have the center which can never quite be 'human' yet governs all."

> "There is a relation to the center, not a human essence present in each individual."

## Questions Raised

1. Can the poststructuralist search for subaltern agency be reconciled with the claim that all subjects are constitutively overdetermined by language and structure?

2. Does the [[originary hypothesis]] necessarily entail a humanism, or can it be reformulated (as Center Study attempts) to make the center genuinely non-human and transcendent without reverting to traditional theology?

3. If AI reveals the contingency of "human" cognitive and creative distinctiveness, what work does the concept "human" still do—and is religious reassertion a rational response to that loss, or a category mistake?
