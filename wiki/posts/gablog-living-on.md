## Summary

This essay traces the genealogy of the modern desire for fame—what Gans identifies as humanity's fundamental value and source of radical evil—back to older forms of immortality-seeking: religious salvation, national glory, great works, and family legacy. The author argues that all these modes of achieving permanence have become increasingly unreliable or morally compromised. The only guarantee of genuine immortality that remains is leaving an irreversible mark on [[language]] itself through one's distinctive style—a possibility available to everyone through the eternal nature of the [[sign]].

## Key Concepts

- [[desire for fame]] – The modern substitute for religious hopes of eternal reward; source of both human motivation and pathological violence
- [[radical evil]] – The demonic impulse to achieve fame through mass murder and transgression
- [[immortality]] – The fundamental human need to endure beyond death; historically satisfied through religion, nation, works, or family
- [[the sign]] – The only truly eternal medium; model for all understandings of immortality
- [[language]] – The sole reliable repository of human permanence and the vehicle for stylistic immortality
- [[style]] – One's irreversible, discernible mark on language; the most accessible and honest form of lasting influence
- [[declarative culture]] – The realm in which linguistic and stylistic claims are made and contested

## Notable Passages

> "The desires circulating around celebrity are a substitute for that fear and hope—the tawdriness of much celebrity and the periodic outbursts of this radical evil might suggest that this substitute is a pathological one."

> "All that endures is language, and whoso would seek out immortality today would best do so therein."

> "The only guarantee of immortality is some discernable, irreversible change to the language—we need only think in terms of some minimal shift, a 'style.'"

## Questions Raised

1. If immortality through language/style is genuinely available to everyone, why don't more people pursue it consciously rather than chasing celebrity or other degraded substitutes?

2. How do we distinguish between a genuine, irreversible stylistic mark on language and mere "deadening formulas of everyday life" that merely recirculate existing patterns?

3. What role does [[self-reflexivity]] play in making one's style visible to oneself—and is such reflexivity itself a stylistic move that must be accounted for?
