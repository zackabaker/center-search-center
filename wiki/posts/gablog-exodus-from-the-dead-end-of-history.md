## Summary

This text argues that liberal democracy is fundamentally unsustainable because it artificially severs [[liberty]] and [[equality]], which can only be mutually reinforcing. Rather than accept this dead end, the author proposes a post-liberal framework grounded in two reciprocal rights: the individual right to exit any community and the communal right to determine admission. Within this model, specific freedoms and protections emerge from the terms communities establish for themselves, creating a competitive marketplace of dynamic and static communities that must negotiate legitimacy through openness or face external pressure.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Liberty and equality]]** – Reciprocally defining principles that liberal democracy falsely treats as antagonistic
- **[[Freedom of movement]]** – The fundamental individual right to leave any community; the basis for post-liberal order
- **[[Communal sovereignty]]** – The right of established communities to determine whom they admit and what rights obtain within their space
- **[[Reciprocal rights]]** – Rights that mutually sustain one another rather than compete; the foundation for sustainable political order
- **[[Single-scenism of modernity]]** – The monolithic historical project of liberal democracy that the author seeks to move beyond
- **[[Dynamic vs. static communities]]** – Communities distinguished by their stance toward immigration and internal openness
- **[[Ownership and belonging]]** – The principle that any space (physical or virtual) is owned by someone, determining access and speech rights within it

## Notable Passages

> "I can only be free together with my equals; I can only be equal with others within a reciprocal respect for each other's freedom."

> "The basic individual right is to leave a community, and the basic communal right is to determine whom to allow in. The terms of entrance in turn define the specific rights that will be protected within that space."

> "Communities that want fewer immigrants are more likely to be those that imprison their own population...we consider your refusal to let individuals leave a threat...to us."

## Questions Raised

1. How do communities enforce exit rights against those who would imprison or coerce their members without imposing a universal sovereign authority—precisely what the framework seeks to avoid?

2. If communities can establish any terms of entrance and determine internal rights accordingly, what prevents the emergence of internally tyrannical but externally "open" communities that attract vulnerable populations precisely to exploit them?

3. Does framing post-liberal order through competitive communities adequately address the problem of [[power]] asymmetries, or does it merely relocate rather than resolve them?
