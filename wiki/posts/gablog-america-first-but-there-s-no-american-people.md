## Summary

This post argues that references to a unified "American people" obscure electoral reality: Democratic voters are locked into identity-based blocs fundamentally opposed to American sovereignty, while Republican strategy must focus on winning a supermajority (65-75%) of white middle-class voters with actual stakes in the nation. The author contends that appealing to these locked-in demographic groups is futile, and that clear-eyed electoral thinking requires abandoning the fiction of a single "American people" and recognizing a deep [[victimary]] vs. sovereign divide.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Victimary]]** – those organized around identity grievance and vendetta against "badwhites," opposed to sovereignty
- **[[Sovereignty]]** – the nation-state's capacity to enforce borders and maintain itself; threatened by open immigration
- **[[Center]]** – the middle ground of shared American identity that serves as the actual political battleground
- **[[Identity politics]]** – bloc-voting organized around demographic categories rather than shared civic commitment
- **[[Amerikaner]]/Badwhite** – pejorative term for white citizens seen as obstacles to progressive transformation
- **[[Demographic destiny]]** – the assumption that demographic change guarantees political outcomes independent of persuasion
- **[[Electoral calculation]]** – rigorous analysis of actual swing constituencies rather than rhetorical appeals to unity

## Notable Passages

> "These are the voters opposed to America as a sovereign entity. On some level, they rightly realize that according to any rigorous and non-legalistic definition of 'American,' they would be excluded, or at least 'graded.'"

> "Everything else is irrelevant: Trump needs something like 65% of the white vote to win... if they don't know this they are throwing the election."

> "It's not a question of what the 'American people' think; it's a question of whether there is enough of a constituency (a large enough sup[port base]."

## Questions Raised

1. If Democrats are demographically locked in and Republicans must build white supermajorities, what happens to the [[center]] as a mediating principle—does electoral politics become purely zero-sum?

2. How do we define "American" rigorously and non-legalistically without merely reproducing the very exclusions the author critiques in the victimary left?

3. Does focusing on white middle-class voters as the only swing constituency risk abandoning the universalist pretense that made national politics coherent in the first place?
