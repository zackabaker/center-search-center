## Summary

This essay diagnoses the foundational confusion underlying modern political thought: the assumption that government requires external justification through "political philosophy" rather than representing the irreducible [[social center]] itself. The author argues that [[anarchist ontology]]—which treats desires and resentments as natural forces to be equilibrated—has colonized liberal discourse, creating a perpetual logic of critique against power. The real problem is that all modern [[disciplines]] are constitutively indebted to the very ontological commitments they claim to analyze objectively, preventing us from developing a discourse of [[sovereignty]] adequate to control the drift of disciplines into [[imperium in imperio]].

## Key Concepts

- **[[Absolutist ontology]]**: Thinking that preserves the sovereign center through supplementation by the attentional center; desires and resentments are addressed through imperatives rather than accepted as natural defaults.

- **[[Anarchist ontology]]**: The view that desires and resentments are natural forces around which social order must be constructed; justifies the state only as equilibrator of competing passions.

- **[[Supplementation of sovereignty]]**: The mechanism by which unfulfilled desires are met not through satisfaction but through reframing via directive from the center.

- **[[Disciplines]]**: Institutionalized discursive spaces that assume subordination of the center to their object of study; they house forms of autonomous power that drift from original purpose.

- **[[Imperium in imperio]]**: "Empire within empire"—autonomous domains of disciplinary authority that escape sovereign control.

- **[[Disciplinary space]]**: Inquiry into commands from the center; distinct from disciplines proper, which advise the state based on their own logic.

- **[[Political philosophy]]**: The problematic notion that government requires external justification rather than being the default representation of the social center.

## Notable Passages

> "Once you grant the need for a 'political philosophy,' you grant the possibility of an arbiter of the legitimacy of any government; once you grant the possibility, you grant the reality."

> "All the discourses of sovereignty we have until this point are indebted in a constitutive way to the disciplines."

> "The spontaneous impulse is to criticize power—the government, corporations, the 1%, the patriarchy, whatever. In fact it takes considerable effort to break this habit of thought."

## Questions Raised

1. How can a discourse of [[sovereignty]] be developed that is *not* constitutively indebted to the disciplinary categories it seeks to govern?

2. What would it mean to present government as the default representation of the social center rather than as something requiring external philosophical justification?

3. Can the "anarchist habit of thought" ever be genuinely displaced in a liberal order, or is it structurally inevitable to such orders?
