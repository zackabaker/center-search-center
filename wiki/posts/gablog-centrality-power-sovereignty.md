## Summary

This text traces a genealogy of social organization from the [[originary scene]] through increasingly complex formations of [[centrality]], [[power]], and [[sovereignty]]. Beginning with "centered ordinality"—where all members defer to a forbidden center—the model shows how differential proximity to the center eventually produces a permanent occupant, introducing [[power]]. As multiple power centers emerge around the central authority, the ruler must choose between moral cooperation or sovereign assertion, the latter inevitably [[de-moralizing]] social life. The text concludes that sovereignty, lacking internally generated standards, must apply existing standards from subordinate power centers, perpetuating resentment.

## Key Concepts

- [[Originary scene]]: The primordial moment where humans constitute themselves as members through shared deferral to a central object
- [[Centered ordinality]]: Differential proximity to center without anyone occupying it; sequential hesitation and imitation
- [[Centrality]]: The state of being the focal point of mimetic attention and ritual repetition
- [[Power]]: Permanent occupation of center through trusted mediation in mimetic crises
- [[Sovereignty]]: A new mode of centrality that claims and enforces right to be judge of last resort
- [[Mimetic rivalry]]: Competitive desire arising from shared deferral; intensified under power structures
- [[De-moralizing]]: The erosion of cooperative moral culture when sovereignty subordinates all institutions to preservation of rule
- [[Sacred culture]]: Multi-layered centrality mediating between community, other communities, gods, and cosmos

## Notable Passages

"Centrality can now be modeled on power, and new power centers can emerge...central power must now find ways to differentiate itself from these orbiting centers."

"Sovereignty can be de-moralizing, as all other institutions are now directly subordinated to the needs of sovereign power."

"The sovereign will apply standards immanent in the power centers themselves...But this just keeps the resentments simmering."

## Questions Raised

1. Can a sovereign ever escape the trap of de-moralizing subordination while still maintaining the monopoly on final judgment that defines sovereignty itself?

2. What would [[centered ordinality]] look like in contemporary horizontal or networked societies—has the originary deferral simply dispersed rather than disappeared?

3. Is the perpetual simmering of resentment under sovereignty an inevitable feature, or can new institutional forms (democratic, procedural) genuinely transform the underlying mimetic dynamics?
