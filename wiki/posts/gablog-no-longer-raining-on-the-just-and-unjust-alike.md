## Summary

This brief post challenges the proliferation of studies documenting disparate outcomes between racial and ethnic groups, arguing that such investigations presume systematic discrimination without evidence and undermine individual accountability. The author contends that once laws against discrimination are established and enforced, individuals must bear responsibility for their own outcomes rather than society engineering equality through redistributive measures. The piece critiques what it sees as a cultural slide toward victimocratic thinking that abandons personal agency in favor of systemic blame-shifting.

## Key Concepts

- [[Victimocracy]]: A system organized around documenting and redressing group victimhood rather than individual responsibility
- [[Individual responsibility]]: The foundational principle that persons must account for their own outcomes
- [[Resentment]]: The underlying emotional driver of victim-narratives and demands for structural compensation
- [[Declarative culture]]: Culture organized around performative declarations of group harm and systemic injustice
- [[Discrimination]]: Actionable legal violation, distinct from mere disparity in outcomes
- [[Equality of outcome]]: The impossible and corrosive goal of engineering identical results across groups

## Notable Passages

"Yet there has been no evidence produced of discrimination in the school system, nor even any allegations of discrimination. Aren't individuals responsible for themselves anymore?"

"All we can do is make and enforce the laws against discrimination; after that individuals have to take responsibility for their own outcomes."

## Questions Raised

1. How does a society distinguish between legitimate evidence of systemic discrimination and statistical disparity that reflects individual choices, effort, or circumstance?

2. What is the relationship between the expansion of victimocratic narratives and the erosion of the [[declarative]] capacity to make binding claims about individual merit and responsibility?

3. Does the author's framework adequately account for historical or structural barriers that might constrain individual agency independent of current legal discrimination?
