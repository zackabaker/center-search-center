# Fraud and Force

## Summary

This essay traces the moral history of civilization from the emergence of the [[Big Man]] in egalitarian communities through sacrificial and post-sacrificial orders. The author argues that once a human occupies the [[center]], they become responsible for all community outcomes, creating conditions for unlimited [[scapegoating]]. Post-sacrificial civilizations (via Biblical revelation in the West, Buddhism/Confucianism elsewhere) attempt to resist this logic through two strategies: either pragmatic self-control enforced by justice systems, or the transformative confrontation with bad faith implicit in the [[Christian revelation]]'s call for universal reciprocity.

## Key Concepts

- [[Center]]: The focal position occupied by ruler or sacred object; the source of power and blame
- [[Sacrifice]]: Ritualized deferral of the killing of the central figure; foundational to sacrificial morality
- [[Scapegoating]]: The mechanism by which unlimited responsibility is placed on a central figure for community failures
- [[Post-sacrificial civilization]]: Efforts to bring into focus which actions a ruler can legitimately be held responsible for
- [[Christian revelation]]: Universal moral reciprocity that forces confrontation with sacrificial institutions
- [[Reciprocity]]: Symmetry of obligation recovered from the originary scene; antidote to scapegoating
- [[Self-control]]: Restraint from enacting resentment outside socially approved channels; Confucian model
- [[Bad faith]]: The implicit denial of shared humanity that enables scapegoating

## Notable Passages

> "Once a human occupies the center, that human can be held responsible for everything attributed to the center, which is everything required for the well-being of the community."

> "The Christian revelation confronts each one of us with the bad faith implicit in our impulse to scapegoat, to pile unlimited responsibility on some other placed at the center."

> "The resistance to potentially unlimited scapegoating becomes the moral problem."

## Questions Raised

1. Can the Confucian model of self-control truly resist scapegoating, or does it merely displace resentment into ritual and aesthetic channels rather than genuinely transforming it?

2. How does the Christian revelation's insistence on universal reciprocity navigate the vulnerability it creates—what protects a post-sacrificial civilization from those who exploit its refusal of systematic scapegoating?

3. In contemporary politics, are we witnessing a collapse back into sacrificial logic, or a new form of [[victimocracy]] that weaponizes the language of reciprocity itself?
