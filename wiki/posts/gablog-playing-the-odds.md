## Summary

This essay argues that postmodern thought is fundamentally probabilistic rather than rights-based, and that information technologies have made probabilistic reasoning about social categories inescapable and threatening to victimary ideology. The author contends that all institutional decision-making (lending, hiring, admissions) and everyday social interaction operate through statistical inference, yet progressive movements attempt to suppress the public acknowledgment of such probabilities. The tension emerges because consistent probabilistic reasoning necessarily generates group-level claims that produce resentment—precisely what victimary culture seeks to eliminate.

## Key Concepts

- [[Probabilism]]: A postmodern mode of thought that replaces rights-based reasoning with calculation of likelihoods and thresholds
- [[Victimary hysteria]]: Panic response to the availability of statistical information that undermines anti-discrimination ideology
- [[Statistical reasoning]]: The practical logic governing institutional and interpersonal decisions through markers correlated with outcomes
- [[Risk thresholds]]: Context-dependent calculations of acceptable danger or cost in social interaction
- [[Resentment]]: The inevitable emotional consequence of stating group-level probabilities publicly
- [[Information technologies]]: Systems that embed probabilistic logic and make statistical patterns more visible and actionable
- [[Declarative culture]]: The public assertion of claims (here, probabilistic ones) that generate social conflict

## Notable Passages

"If there is a genuinely postmodern mode of thought, one that comes after modernity, it is a radical probabilism that is incompatible with thinking in terms of rights, justice, progress and good vs. evil."

"The most radical thing one can do today is act, and proclaim that one acts, upon probabilistic reasoning."

"For a probabilistic reasoner, it would be impossible to speak about broader social questions without speaking in group terms, however qualified."

## Questions Raised

1. **How does probabilistic reasoning maintain coherence across different contexts?** The author notes that probabilities are "highly context-dependent," yet victimary critique treats all group-based claims as equivalent. Can a probabilistic reasoner articulate when demographic inference is and isn't appropriate without collapsing back into the very resentment they wish to avoid?

2. **What happens to the epistemic status of statistics themselves?** If victimary movements succeed in delegitimizing the *utterance* of statistical claims, does this not corrupt the data-collection process itself, creating a feedback loop where suppressed reasoning produces worse information?

3. **Is there a post-victimary politics possible on probabilistic grounds?** Can one acknowledge that institutions *must* reason probabilistically while still constraining which probabilities get acted upon or declared?
