## Summary

This post examines the historical continuity (or lack thereof) between classical leftism and contemporary victimary progressivism by reconsidering what "discipline" means in relation to [[sovereignty]]. Rather than accepting that classical leftists opposed discipline while modern leftists expose it, the author argues that all forms of sovereignty—from Big Man to monarchy to bourgeois market order—presuppose discipline as fundamental deferral. The real leftist gesture lies in rejecting monarchism itself as a form of [[institutionalized firstness]], regardless of whether the alternative proposed is economic productivity or victimary exposure of "stolen centrality."

## Key Concepts

- **[[Institutionalized Firstness]]** — The foundational authority granted to a sovereign to distribute rewards and establish social order
- **[[Discipline]]** — Not merely economic regimentation, but the deferral of violent desire and competing claims to centrality
- **[[Sovereignty]]** — The locus of legitimate first-mover power; the form varies (monarchy, market, democracy) but the function persists
- **[[Stolen Centrality]]** — The accusation that those in power have appropriated collective focus illegitimately
- **[[Resentment]]** — The emotional-intellectual foundation of leftist critique across historical periods
- **[[The Big Man]]** — The originary figure whose accumulation and consumption restraint established the precedent for all later sovereign forms
- **[[Bourgeois Order]]** — The market-based alternative to monarchy that classical leftists championed, prioritizing productive merit over inherited privilege

## Notable Passages

> "If 'discipline' involves all kinds of deferrals, including the deferral of the desire to overthrow the sovereign in favor of one's own faction...then 'institutionalized firstness' is the first form of discipline—not only historically, but as the mode of discipline which all the others must presuppose."

> "Insofar as the left rejected [the possibility of a rejuvenated monarchy], it clearly rejected monarchism as such, making that the foundational leftist gesture."

> "If it rejected monarchism in the name of a more 'productive' or 'functional' form of sovereignty...then it is certainly rejecting the form of discipline requiring respect for at least the accumulated results of previous increments of discipline."

## Questions Raised

1. Does the rejection of monarchism as such entail a permanent incompleteness in leftist thought—since all alternatives to monarchy must still instantiate *some* form of institutionalized firstness, making the leftist critique perpetually applicable?

2. If classical and contemporary leftism both rest on exposure of illegitimate centrality, what explains the shift from demanding "productive" discipline (markets, meritocracy) to demanding the dissolution of disciplinary structures altogether?

3. Can a sovereignty that abandons the accumulated discipline of institutional precedent actually maintain itself, or does the left's rejection of monarchical tradition necessarily lead to the instability Gans observes in contemporary victimary orders?
