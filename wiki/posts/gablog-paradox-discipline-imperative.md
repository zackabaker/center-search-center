# Paradox, Discipline, Imperative

## Summary

The human sciences, beginning with philosophy, systematically conceal the [[signifying paradox]] constitutive of human meaning-making. By obscuring the [[scene of writing]] upon which all post-sacrificial disciplines depend, they falsely claim to adjudicate sovereignty through objective criteria (justice, rights, law, prosperity). Yet these disciplines are themselves paradoxical: their declarative utterances must suppress the very iterability—the capacity of signs to be re-used in unanticipated contexts—that makes human communication possible. Only by acknowledging this irreducible paradox can inquiry become genuinely humanistic.

## Key Concepts

- [[Signifying paradox]]: The fundamental human condition that we discover meaning precisely where we assumed it absent
- [[Scene of writing]]: The originary media scene whose concealment enables disciplinary authority
- [[Iterability]]: Derrida's insight that signs' true power lies in their capacity for re-use across contexts, not fixed intention
- [[Center and imperative]]: The sovereign's paradoxical situation: precise commands require unanticipated obedience
- [[Declarative culture]]: The disciplines' false claim to issue neutral, univocal verdicts on legitimacy
- [[Circular criteria]]: How human sciences disguise tautology as measurement (clarity, precision, comprehension)

## Notable Passages

> "Writing reveals that what is truly originary in the sign is its iterability, not its meaning or the intention behind it; its iterability, which guarantees the possibility of future human scenes, is its meaning."

> "The disciplines would have to 'abdicate' their self-appointment as those who provide the criteria upon which we judge the legitimacy of the sovereign."

> "All the criteria used to determine whether student writing was 'good' or 'improving' was circular—terms like 'clarity,' 'precision,' 'deep analysis' really don't mean anything."
