## Summary

This post grounds Anna Wierzbicka's Natural Semantic Metalanguage (NSM) project in [[generative anthropology|GA]]'s originary hypothesis, arguing that universal semantic primitives can be understood through the [[imperative order]]. Rather than treating verbs as abstract linguistic categories, the author derives them from the [[evasive object]]—the proto-declarative rejection of an unavailable demand—and shows how all human action can be traced back to imperatives rather than mysterious acts of "will." This approach reframes classical philosophical problems (free will, agency) as grammatical rather than metaphysical.

## Key Concepts

- [[Natural Semantic Metalanguage]] – Universal set of words found across all human languages; Wierzbicka's completion of Leibniz's alphabet of thought
- [[Imperative order]] – The originary system of commands and demands that grounds all verb usage and human action
- [[Evasive object]] – The unavailable object whose demanded presence is rejected, generating the proto-predicate and originating verbs
- [[Negative ostensive]] – The gesture indicating what is absent or prohibited, foundational to proto-declarative speech
- [[Declarative order]] – The realm of knowledge and assertion, dependent on but distinct from imperative command
- [[Operator of negation]] – The linguistic/gestural mechanism that refuses a demand, marking unavailability
- [[Semantic primes]] – The irreducible core verbs (think, know, want, do, say, exist, etc.) from which all meaning derives

## Notable Passages

"All the questions of 'will' and 'free will'…can be approached in a completely different way by simply positing an imperative initiating these actions. Instead of struggling to figure out what comes between the 'he' and the 'running' (his will to run!) we can just say he was ordered to run."

"'Knowing' doesn't seem to be the kind of thing, unlike, say, 'learning,' that can be commanded. Knowing exists only in the declarative order, then."

"To be engaged in another activity is to be doing something which might be done pursuant to an imperative (ultimately, even conditions and qualities can be viewed as determined by imperatives)."

## Questions Raised

1. If all verbs ultimately derive from imperatives, what is the status of verbs describing purely internal states (dream, imagine, remember) where no external command seems operative?

2. How does this originary grammar account for the progressive and habitual aspects of language (I am running, I used to run) that seem to operate outside discrete imperative moments?

3. Can Wierzbicka's semantic metalanguage be exhaustively derived from imperative logic, or are there irreducible declarative/contemplative dimensions to human meaning-making that resist this reduction?
