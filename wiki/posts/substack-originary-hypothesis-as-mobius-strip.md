## Summary

The [[originary hypothesis]] occupies an impossible middle position: it is neither faith nor philosophy, yet closer to both than either discipline can comfortably accept. GA repels the "initiatory download" necessary to understand it while refusing both Enlightenment debunking and philosophical legitimation of faith traditions. The hypothesis's power derives from its irreducible logic of [[mimesis]] and [[rivalry]], yet this very radicalism makes it "disreputable from all sides"—atheist to believers, mere speculation to scientists, and arrogant to all established knowledge.

## Key Concepts

- [[Originary hypothesis]] — The founding insight that human culture emerges from a scene of sacred violence resolved through [[designation]]
- [[Mimesis]] — The fundamentally imitative character of human being, imitation leading inexorably to rivalry
- [[Faith and philosophy]] — Two incompatible modes of knowledge that GA paradoxically transcends while validating both
- [[Scandal of GA]] — The illegitimacy GA incurs by sweeping aside all prior human knowledge
- [[Declarative culture]] — The originary moment of meaning-making through shared sign and deferral
- [[Center]] — The sacred object around which mimetic desire crystallizes and from which all culture radiates

## Notable Passages

> "GA cannot claim to be an 'Enlightenment' philosophy that stands outside of faith and 'debunks' its pretensions—on the contrary, the implication of the originary hypothesis is that the faiths are truer than philosophy, and it's not even close."

> "To take on the originary hypothesis, you have to be prepared to be disreputable from all sides—an atheist to believers, a mere speculator to philosophers and 'scientists'—and simply unacceptably arrogant."

> "If you accept that human beings are imitative, can you set a limit to imitation...And if we're mimetic all the way down, can anyone deny that imitation leads to rivalry?"

## Questions Raised

1. Does GA require what might be called an "esoteric dimension"—a more ambitious scope than Gans himself pursued—to fully account for its implications?

2. If mimesis and rivalry are truly foundational, what prevents GA from becoming itself a "faith" despite its logical rigor?

3. How can a thinker inhabit the Möbius strip position of being simultaneously closest and furthest from all existing intellectual traditions without succumbing to either arrogance or self-abnegation?
