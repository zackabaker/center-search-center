## Summary

This analysis examines Michael Haneke's *Caché* as a film about [[white guilt]] structured as an irresolvable mystery. A French intellectual receives mysterious videotapes documenting his house and an Algerian man whose family he destroyed in childhood; the Algerian eventually commits suicide. Though Haneke insists the spectator must determine truth, the film's actual argument is fixed: the protagonist is guilty toward the Arab world. The work exemplifies postmodern aesthetics masking a deeper victimary morality—guilt is the only non-ambiguous element.

## Key Concepts

- [[White Guilt]]: The unstated but operative moral framework that makes the European protagonist inherently culpable toward the subaltern Other
- [[Victimary Logic]]: The structure wherein historical wrongs (the 1961 Paris massacre) bind present subjects to retroactive responsibility
- [[Postmodernity]]: Surface indeterminacy and undecidability that conceals a hidden moral determinism
- [[Scenic Apparatus]]: The videotapes as mediating device that render life as text subject to interpretation and reinterpretation
- [[Resentment]]: The emotional substance linking past injury to present demand for acknowledgment
- [[Media as Guilt-Machine]]: Technology that projects subjective guilt onto objective reality

## Questions Raised

1. How does the distinction between postmodern formal undecidability and [[victimary]] moral determinism clarify contemporary ethical discourse more broadly?

2. If the filmmaker "sends" the tapes as projection of the protagonist's guilt, what anthropological mechanism explains why guilt must exist prior to evidence or even narrative coherence?

3. Does Haneke's own position as German filmmaker relocating French guilt suggest a [[centering]] mechanism where guilt becomes a portable, transferable medium rather than tied to specific historical actors?
