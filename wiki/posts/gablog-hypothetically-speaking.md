## Summary

This essay examines how [[meaning]] depends fundamentally on [[medium]], using online communication and meme culture as case studies. The author argues that new media typically attempt to "saturate" the conditions of older media they replace—radio mimics conversation, TV mimics theater—but this creates representational problems. Rather than treating media as transparent containers for content, we should explore each medium's distinctive capacity to organize [[attention]]. The essay suggests that understanding media as [[disciplinary space|disciplinary spaces]] rather than mere content-vehicles opens new analytical possibilities.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Medium]]** – The form through which meaning is constituted; meaning cannot be separated from its medium of transmission
- **[[Saturation]]** – The attempt by new media to fill gaps and replicate the assumed conditions of older media they displace
- **[[Disciplinary space]]** – A framework treating any medium as a distinctive way of organizing attention rather than as neutral content-carrier
- **[[Oral culture]]** – Communication structured by physical presence, gesture, and immediate response; online forums paradoxically revive oral codes in written form
- **[[Literacy]]** – The transformation of social order through writing; creates distance from violence that classical prose simulates rather than represents
- **[[Attention]]** – The universal dimension across all media; the key to understanding how different forms organize human perception and response

## Notable Passages

"Meaning depends upon medium. But there are some less obvious consequences to this observation."

"The fact of widespread literacy transforms social order, or entails a transformed social order, such that a certain distance from violence can be assumed, rather than having the line at which we pass into violence represented regularly."

"If there is a universal across all media, it is not content or ideas, but that any medium is a distinctive way of organizing attention."

## Questions Raised

1. If online memes reveal something about responders through forced reactions, what does this tell us about the [[originary scene]] and how attention to signs generates social knowledge?

2. How do we develop genuinely new representational forms that don't unconsciously smuggle in the assumptions of older media we're trying to transcend?

3. What would "exploring media as media" look like in practice for political or ethical discourse currently trapped in classical prose conventions?
