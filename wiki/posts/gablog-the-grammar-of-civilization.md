## Summary

This essay argues that civilization is fundamentally defined not by the elimination of barbarism, but by its strategic marginalization through appeal to impartial third-party justice. The civilized individual defers private revenge by submitting resentments to arbitration, yet this system depends paradoxically on the continued existence of barbaric impulses—the desire for revenge itself. Civilization mediates hostage-taking in all its forms (legal, domestic, metaphorical), managing it through law and moral vocabulary rather than eliminating it. The essay contends that denying this dependency is the defining pathology of modern political thought.

## Key Concepts

- [[Barbarism]] - Politics of honor, direct retaliation, and hostage-taking; operates through deterrence and overwhelming punishment of spontaneous resentment
- [[Civilization]] - System that delegates resentment management to impartial third parties; marginalizes rather than eliminates barbaric impulses
- [[Third-party arbitration]] - The core mechanism of civilized politics; allows deferral of private revenge through provisional trust in justice
- [[Hostage-taking]] - Taking control of what matters most to a victim to compel attention and compliance; exists in literal and metaphorical forms
- [[Attentional economy]] - The strategic management of what one pays attention to; barbarism "grips" attention more effectively than civilization
- [[White Guilt]] - A sophisticated, metaphorical form of hostage-taking operating in contemporary civilized discourse
- [[Resentment deferral]] - The willingness to submit one's own grievances to external judgment rather than settling them privately

## Notable Passages

> "Civilization, I have hypothesized, meanwhile, can never do away with barbarism once and for all—it marginalizes it, represses it, simulates it, neutralizes it, but never eliminates it."

> "Only people who want revenge can defer it through provisional trust in a justice system that will supply a more legitimate and effective equivalent."

> "Barbarism simply involves a more 'gripping' economy of attention than does civilization."

## Questions Raised

1. If civilization depends on the suppression rather than elimination of barbaric revenge impulses, what happens to the social contract when populations become too "enfeebled" to execute private revenge—does civilization then collapse or transform entirely?

2. How does the concept of [[hostage-taking]] as metaphor help explain contemporary political movements that traffic in guilt and victimhood as attentional mechanisms?

3. Can modern therapeutic vocabularies ("acting out trauma") genuinely replace the deterrent function of punishment, or do they simply obscure civilization's continued reliance on the threat of barbaric response?
