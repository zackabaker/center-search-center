## Summary

This post argues that advanced societies rest on two foundational asymmetrical exchanges: one with a central [[sovereign]] power, and one with the "infinite" (God, Being, grammar, or language). The sovereign's legitimacy derives from superior [[discipline]], which attracts emulation and contains [[rivalry]] within bounded systems. Yet sovereignty itself remains vulnerable to the very rivalries it contains, creating an incommensurable relation to transcendence—a permanent wager that centralized power can limit destructive competition, a wager "won until it is lost."

## Key Concepts

- [[Sovereign]] — the central authority whose superior discipline and visible model-status restrains rivalries among community members
- [[Asymmetrical gift exchange]] — unequal reciprocal obligation binding subjects to central power
- [[Incommensurable gift exchange]] — the infinite relation that exceeds all calculation; the transcendent dimension of social order
- [[Rivalry]] — the fundamental competitive drive that sovereignty must contain, bound, and redirect
- [[Resentment]] — the emotional fuel of rivalry; dies in successful sovereignty but is reborn as deference
- [[Discipline]] — the capacity to stand out and become a model; the root of sovereignty's authority
- [[Vendetta]] — the unbounded, retaliatory form of gift-exchange that sovereignty prevents
- [[Center]] — the organizing principle that makes civilization possible by limiting and formalizing competition

## Notable Passages

> "Sovereignty is where resentments go to die, as we discover that targeting the presumed source of our resentment does not assuage that resentment and, moreover, creates new resentments in turn."

> "The more operative sovereignty is throughout society, the more progress along these lines (i.e., civilization) is possible."

> "This is always a wager that is won until it is lost."

## Questions Raised

1. If sovereignty's legitimacy depends on continuous successful containment of rivalry, what determines *when* the wager is lost—and is collapse inevitable?

2. How does the "incommensurable relation to the infinite" function differently across various historical articulations of transcendence (religious, linguistic, grammatical), and does one form prove more stable than others?

3. Can sovereignty exist without a visible, disciplined center, or does [[dedifferentiation]] inevitably undermine the model-effect required for order?
