## Summary

Resentment is culture's fundamental paradox: it arises from moral objections to ethically necessary hierarchies, yet the very act of identifying resentment in others implicates oneself in resentment. Drawing on Gans's insight that resentment "cannot be deconstructed," this essay explores how culture—art, philosophy, science—attempts to defer resentment by creating shared spaces of unfulfillable desire. The text raises a crucial gap in Gans's work: while esthetic and philosophical deferral receive extensive treatment, institutional and judicial mechanisms for managing resentment remain largely unexamined, despite being the obvious starting point for such inquiry.

## Key Concepts

- [[Resentment]] — The scandal of the peripheral self at the centrality of another; a necessary evil arising from moral objection to communal hierarchies
- [[Deferral]] — Culture's primary strategy for managing resentment through representation (art, philosophy, science) rather than resolution
- [[Moral vs. Ethical]] — Moral objections to hierarchy conflict with ethical (necessary) differences; justice systems might reconcile both
- [[Original Scene]] — The primordial scene of representation where equality transforms into hierarchical significance
- [[Desire]] — The generative source concealed in cultural products; resentment's hidden origin
- [[Solidarity]] — New forms of collective identity forged through shared inability to obtain transcendent objects (beauty, truth)

## Notable Passages

> "Resentment is the one category that cannot be deconstructed... Only resentment can know resentment; yet resentment knows nothing since it distorts the reality of what it observes."

> "All of culture is nothing but this attempt" to represent and defer resentment through language—art, philosophy, and science are failures, however great, because desire's origins can never be fully effaced.

> "Isn't a court or justice system precisely where the 'moral' would be built into the 'ethical'?" — suggesting institutional design as unexplored terrain for resentment management.

## Questions Raised

1. If identifying resentment in others necessarily implicates oneself, does Gans's own diagnostic project escape the trap he describes, or does it exemplify culture's "impossible attempt"?

2. Why has institutional/judicial design received so little attention in GA despite being, as suggested, the obvious locus where moral and ethical orders intersect?

3. Can any deferral system—legal, esthetic, scientific—actually reduce resentment, or do all institutions merely "spread it thin enough to let some light pass through"?
