## Summary

This post proposes that all categorical thinking—especially political accusations like "racism," "fascism," and "Nazi"—operates through an [[originary event]] that serves as a model against which actual events are measured for similarity. Using the Holocaust as the ur-model event of modern victimary logic, the author sketches how one might algorithmically formalize the criteria for identifying perpetrators, bystanders, and victims. Rather than viewing algorithmic thinking as dehumanizing, the post argues that explicating these models serves both satiric and interventionist purposes in remaking [[declarative culture]].

## Key Concepts

- [[Originary event]]: A historical or retroactively posited foundational event that grounds subsequent categorical judgments and gives meaning to epithets.
- [[Model event]]: The template against which actual events are assessed for similarity; distinct from propositional definitions.
- [[Algorithmic inquiry]]: A method of making implicit criteria explicit to understand how meaning-making and judgment actually function.
- [[Victimary position]]: A mode of political consciousness organized around victimhood, with the Holocaust as its ur-event.
- [[Declarative culture]]: Culture oriented toward explicit statements and meaning-making; distinct from imperative needs.
- [[Marker]]: Observable features or criteria that identify an event or agent as instantiating the model event.
- [[Imperative gap]]: The unmet practical needs that declarative culture should address but often obscures.

## Notable Passages

> "One works with what we can call an 'originary' event of 'racism,' and then calls subsequent acts 'racist' insofar as they are 'similar to' the originary event."

> "Whoever makes the most forceful identification of some current event with the Nazi genocide of the Jews 'wins'."

> "What framing their actions algorithmically does is, first, help us display how automatic and programmed their actions are—it provides us with a satiric grammar."

## Questions Raised

1. If victimary agents themselves cannot articulate the criteria by which they judge "racism" or fascism, does making those criteria explicit actually interrupt their power, or does it merely provide them with better tools for enforcement?

2. How does the shift from propositional definition to model-event matching alter our understanding of how political language actually persuades—and what would a counter-model look like?

3. What would it mean to apply this algorithmic method to conservative or non-victimary positions—do they rely on originary model events in the same way, or is this structure specific to victimary culture?
