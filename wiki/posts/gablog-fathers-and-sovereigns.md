## Summary

This essay recuperates Robert Filmer's absolutist patriarchal theory through a Generative Anthropology lens, arguing that while his biblical genealogies are no longer credible, the underlying insight—that all rule is fundamentally paternal—can be rescued by reading the Bible as a narrative about the emergence of [[resentment]] in agricultural societies. The text proposes that Adam represents the first figure forced to acquire knowledge of hierarchy and power differentiation, making Filmer's genealogical claim anthropologically defensible if reinterpreted through [[originary thinking]]. By contrasting nomadic patriarchal communities (Israel's shepherds) with agricultural imperial megasocieties (Egypt), the argument suggests that foundational power derives from the necessity to manage [[social differentiation]] and the resentments it generates.

## Key Concepts

- [[Sovereignty]]: Absolute rule derived ultimately from a founding father figure; power held in trust against discovery of the true heir
- [[Paternal rule]]: The model for all legitimate authority; rule by a master over helpless, dependent descendants
- [[Resentment]]: The knowledge of good and evil acquired through social differentiation; the emotional force driving the need for hierarchical control
- [[Agricultural society]]: The origin point of social hierarchy, property rights, and structured resentment
- [[Originary scene]]: The founding moment where [[differentiation]] between ruler and ruled emerges, paralleled in the Fall narrative
- [[Big Man]]: The first stable hierarchical figure to emerge from egalitarian communities
- [[Ancestor worship]]: A pre-agricultural transcendence mechanism contrasted with imperial and patriarchal alternatives

## Notable Passages

> "Filmer's use of Adam as the origin of authority is justified insofar as Adam really a kind of synthetic version of the first father/king and the first to attain knowledge of the need for father/kings in a resentful world."

> "The Bible as a whole can be read as an ongoing protest of the pre-agricultural, nomadic communities against the ancient agriculturally based imperial mega-societies, along with the recuperation of this protest as divinely commanded moral constraints."

> "All power is held in trust against the discovery of the true heir, while being no less absolute for that."

## Questions Raised

1. If paternal rule emerges necessarily from [[resentment]] triggered by agricultural differentiation, what model of authority becomes relevant in post-scarcity or post-hierarchical societies—or is such transcendence impossible?

2. How does the nomadic patriarchal structure (represented by Israel's shepherds) differ fundamentally from later monarchical rule, and can this distinction clarify different types of legitimate sovereignty?

3. Does the Bible's "ongoing protest" against imperial megasocieties suggest that moral transcendence itself is fundamentally an anti-imperial force?
