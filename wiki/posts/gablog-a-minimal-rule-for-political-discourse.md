## Summary

This essay proposes a constraint on political speech: eliminate imperatives directed at third parties ("x must realize," "we must demand"). Such commands fantasy a shared [[center]] that doesn't exist and collapse under first-person scrutiny. Instead, Gans advocates for [[indicative]] chains of conditional reasoning ("if…then…") that respect [[standing]]—the speaker's actual position to address injury or answer accusation. This shift from imperative to indicative discourse removes coercive fantasy and grounds rhetoric in mimetic reality.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Imperative]]**: A command directed outward; problematic when addressing third parties who haven't consented to hear it
- **[[Standing]]**: Legal and ontological position to speak; one can address injury to oneself or answer accusations, but not "defend civilization"
- **[[Center]]**: The imagined shared focal point imperatives assume but rarely establish
- **[[Indicative]]**: Conditional chains of causation ("if…then…") that allow insertion of further conditions, mapping actual choice sequences
- **[[Performativity]]**: Speech acts that can only be meaningful if stated in first person before the intended recipient
- **[[Declarative culture]]**: Communication grounded in what can actually be declared and sustained

## Notable Passages

"Nobody really has to do anything, and stating that they do simply establishes a fantasy scenario in which others come to occupy the same scene as you and recognize the same center."

"A performative, like an imperative or judgment, that can't be stated in the first person before the intended recipient?" (rhetorical question exposing the constraint's logic)

"Everyone is simply confronted with choice after choice, with each choice generated a new series, which we can anticipate and formulate as mimetic, speaking beings."

## Questions Raised

1. How does this constraint apply to prescriptive ethical or legal systems, which seem to require third-party imperatives to function? Is all law thereby incoherent?

2. Can indicative chains realistically substitute for political mobilization, which often depends on shared imperatives to generate collective action?

3. Does eliminating third-party imperatives risk paralyzing speech about systemic injustice, where individual [[standing]] seems insufficient to address structural problems?
