# Relevance

## Summary

This post interrogates the common distinction between "distracting" events and "really important" matters, arguing that judgments of relative importance always presuppose and seek to impose a particular model of reality. The author challenges the implicit assumption that widespread attention to significant processes translates into political power or coherent public opinion. Instead, he proposes that genuine thinking emerges by engaging with the anomalies and paradoxes most forcefully present to oneself, rather than seeking a "commanding metalanguage" from which to judge what others ought to find important. The desire to identify the "most real" realities often masks a resentful intellectual fantasy of superiority and control.

## Key Concepts

- [[Reality]] — what fits your presupposed model of the world; always contested and strategically defined
- [[Attention]] — the distribution of consciousness across events; implies assumptions about power and consequence
- [[Center]] — the position from which one can "give orders" and command interpretation of what matters
- [[Resentment]] — the satisfying feeling of being superior to those "distracted" by manufactured events
- [[Declarative culture]] — the impulse to impose your model of reality on others through assertion of importance
- [[Anomaly]] — the paradox or irregularity most forcefully present to you; the actual ground of thinking
- [[Metalanguage]] — a commanding framework that positions you above those you judge as confused or manipulated

## Notable Passages

> "A distinction between what is more and what is less important is essentially a distinction between what is more real and what is less real. What is more real, it will always turn out, is what better fits the model of reality you presuppose—and wish to impose on others."

> "The one criticizing the distraction and the distracted, then, is the one out of touch with reality: more people paying attention to the latest economic developments does not add up to more people having intelligent, informed discussions about those developments."

> "The desire to pitch one's tent at the realest of the realities is the desire to have a commanding metalanguage that enables you to give orders, at least in your own mind, or the space you share with others, to those who actually command, who occupy centers."

## Questions Raised

1. If attending to "important" processes grants no actual political power, is the distinction between distraction and focus a purely psychological or spiritual matter—one of internal coherence rather than external effect?

2. How can one distinguish between genuinely self-determined thinking (following one's own anomalies) and a rationalization of powerlessness dressed up as intellectual humility?

3. Does the author's own critique here escape the resentful desire for a metalanguage from which to judge others' misplaced attention, or is that escape impossible?
