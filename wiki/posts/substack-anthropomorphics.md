## Summary

The author reconsiders the concept of "anthropomorphics" as a disciplinary framework for understanding how humans model themselves on the [[center]] before projecting human qualities onto the non-human world. Rather than treating anthropomorphism as a logical fallacy to be eliminated (as Cartesian modernity attempted), recent work in material cognition and AI studies reveals it as epistemically necessary. The human itself is fundamentally artificial—always already an imitation of the center that vectorizes converging desires back through prohibition—making the historicity and performativity of the human the proper subject of inquiry.

## Key Concepts

- [[Anthropomorphics]]: The science of how humans model themselves on the center, then project human qualities onto non-human realities; emphasizes artificiality and historicity of the human from the beginning.
- [[Center]]: The vectorization of converging desires turned back at participants through prohibition; the originary model on which humans pattern themselves.
- [[Hominization]]: GA's term for human creation/invention; rejected here because it elides the step of modeling oneself on the center.
- [[Extended mind]]: The thesis that cognition occurs not in the brain alone but across embodied, material, and environmental engagement.
- [[Material engagement]]: Lambos Malafouris's framework showing anthropomorphism as necessary to thinking and knowing, not merely illusory projection.
- [[Performativity]]: The exhaustive enactment of the human across the world; the human is what it does.
- [[Declarative culture]]: The system of signs and prohibitions that constitute the human through shared deferral.

## Notable Passages

> "Humans had to be anthropomorphized before they could get around to anthropomorphizing anything else."

> "We were always already imitating the center that was itself nothing more than a vectorization of our converging desires turned back at us through a prohibition."

> "Anthropomorphism is not merely 'natural' (but 'illusory') but necessary and a critical part of thinking and knowing the world."

## Questions Raised

1. If anthropomorphism is epistemically necessary rather than a cognitive bias to overcome, how do we distinguish between productive and destructive modes of projection onto non-human realities (AI, nature, institutions)?

2. How does the performative, historically contingent nature of the human change our understanding of what makes something "human" in ethical or political contexts?

3. Can the paradox Ionesco discovers—that simulations incorporate themselves into what they simulate—be resolved through a center-study approach to feedback, or does it reveal something fundamental about the circularity of anthropomorphic knowledge?
