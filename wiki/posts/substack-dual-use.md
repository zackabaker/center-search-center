## Summary

Eric Jacobus proposes a modification to the originary hypothesis by introducing [[recursive object directed aggression]]—the capacity to anticipate and prepare for escalating violence through weapons. Rather than bare-handed conflict, proto-humans wielding stones and sticks would enter an "annihilatory imaginary" requiring the deferral sign. This resolves the question of why the sign becomes necessary. The author extends this insight to argue that all technology is fundamentally "dual use," oscillating between peaceful and violent applications, making [[pedagogy]] central to human culture from its origins.

## Key Concepts

- [[Originary hypothesis]] — The minimal account of language's emergence at the human origin scene
- [[Recursive object directed aggression]] — Unlike animals, proto-humans could anticipate and prepare for escalating violence by others
- [[Annihilatory imaginary]] — The shared vision of mutual destruction that makes the deferral sign salvational
- [[Dual use technology]] — Every tool contains latent capacity for both peaceful construction and violent harm
- [[Mimetic crisis]] — The competitive/conflictual moment where the sign must intervene
- [[Ritual pedagogy]] — The teaching of distinctions between tool and weapon use as foundational to group identity
- [[Technics]] — The ongoing composition of scenes for increasingly complex rituals through material design

## Notable Passages

> "One of the proto-humans would escalate a confrontation by raising, say, a stone in a pre-emptive way, generating a corresponding response in others. Now, we have a new situation in which each has to weigh the possibility that the others will initiate a new and unprecedented mode of violence."

> "All technologies are 'dual use,' in that case. And that means that any prop or 'furnishing' serves as a potential pedagogical platform upon which not only is its use taught, but one kind of use is distinguished from another."

> "The earliest initiation rituals would involve teaching young men to distinguish tool from weapon construction and use, and this would become a principle of design because it would define the group in its ritual opera."

## Questions Raised

1. If all technology is dual-use by design principle, can we identify moments in technological history where this distinction between peaceful and violent applications becomes obscured or collapsed, and what are the cultural consequences?

2. Does the pedagogical distinction between tool and weapon use require explicit ritual transmission, or can it be absorbed tacitly? How does this vary across different cultural contexts?

3. If recursive anticipation of escalating violence generates the need for the sign, what role does [[imagination]] play in distinguishing the proto-human scene from animal conflict—is it sufficient to *imagine* weapons one doesn't yet possess?
