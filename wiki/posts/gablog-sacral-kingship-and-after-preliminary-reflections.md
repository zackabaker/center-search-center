## Summary

This essay traces the anthropological evolution from the [[Big Man]] to [[sacral kingship]], arguing that kingship represents the institutionalization of human occupation of the [[sacred center]]. The sacral king bears dual responsibility: maintaining cosmic and social harmony through ritual, while serving as the community's scapegoat for failure. The text explores how capable kings consolidated power by building bureaucracy and territorial control, eventually creating stable imperial dynasties that maintained sacred kingship structures for millennia.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Sacred center]]**: The focal point of community harmony, originally seized by the Big Man and later institutionalized through ritual kingship
- **[[Big Man]]**: The founding entrepreneur of political order, replaceable within gift economies; predecessor to the sacral king
- **[[Sacral kingship]]**: Political form investing the king with responsibility for cosmic and social order, hedged by ritual constraints
- **[[Scapegoat mechanism]]**: The ritual vulnerability of the sacral king to community resentment when expectations (fertility, weather) go unmet
- **[[Ritual slaughter]]**: Common practice of executing sacral kings, sometimes ritually prescribed, to reset cosmic order
- **[[Division of power]]**: Separation of ritual and executive functions, allowing societies to manage tension between symbolic and practical authority
- **[[God Emperor]]**: The successful sacral king who consolidates territory, bureaucracy, and dynastic stability into millennial rule

## Notable Passages

> "The king is responsible not only for peace in the community but peace between humans and the world—quite literally, the king is responsible for the growth of crops, the mildness of the weather, the fertility of livestock."

> "If the sacral king is the nodal point of the community's hopes he is equally the scapegoat of its resentments."

> "Once the center has been humanly occupied, it must remain humanly occupied, while ongoing clarification regarding the mode of occupation would be determined by the needs of deferring new forms of potential violence."

## Questions Raised

1. How does the transition from Big Man to sacral king illuminate the originary scene itself—does the sacral king represent a second-order institutionalization of the [[central participant]]?

2. If ritual slaughter of sacral kings was common practice, what mechanisms eventually broke this cycle and allowed dynasties to stabilize, and what does this reveal about the relationship between [[deferral]] and institutional legitimacy?

3. Does the modern democratic elimination of sacral kingship represent genuine progress, or merely the concealment of the sacred center under new forms ([[liberal democracy]], [[victimocracy]]) that mask rather than eliminate its anthropological necessity?
