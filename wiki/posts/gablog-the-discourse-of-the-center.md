## Summary

The text argues that human beings are fundamentally "bound to the center"—all our utterances and actions constitute homage to some authoritative locus that issues commands and receives dialogue. Rather than free agents or evolved primates, we are creatures engaged in an ongoing effort to align multiple centers (from daily references to divine beings to the originary event itself) through what Gans calls "imperative exchange." Cultural creation proceeds through mistaken signs that are corrected and transformed into new iterable forms. The perpetual question—"what is the center telling us to do?"—once answered by shamans and prophets, now lacks credible interpreters.

## Key Concepts

- [[Center]] – the locus of authority that issues imperatives and receives dialogue from the community
- [[Originary scene]] – the founding moment of language where an inappropriate ostensive sign was corrected
- [[Imperative]] – the command structure through which the center governs collective resentment
- [[Imperative exchange]] – the ongoing dialogue between community and center that defines authority
- [[Ostensive sign]] – the primary linguistic form naming an object in presence; source of new imperatives
- [[Metapersons]] – supernatural figures (ancestors, gods, spirits) functioning as governing centers in primitive societies
- [[Resentment]] – the collective passion that centers must control through command structures
- [[Sufficient center]] – the axiom that societies invent/discover whatever centers needed to manage social tension

## Notable Passages

> "Everything that we say, think or do is homage to the center. If there is a logic to history it is a very uneven one: we are all working to bring all of the centers into alignment."

> "The first imperative was an inappropriate ostensive: someone named an object without realizing it wasn't there, and another member of the community retrieved it, so as to 'make good' on the sign."

> "Human societies will discover/invent the centers they need in order to generate the command structure necessary to control resentment."

## Questions Raised

1. If the center is perpetually reconstructed through imperative exchange, what happens when no credible interpreter can reliably tell the community what the center demands—as Gans suggests is true today?

2. Does Sahlins' distinction between "metapersonal" governing structures and actual social relations truly prove complexity requires non-human actors, or could such complexity still represent projections of human relations?

3. How does the axiom of the "sufficient center" explain the proliferation of competing, incommensurable centers in modern pluralistic societies?
