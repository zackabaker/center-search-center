## Summary

This post traces the victimary stance—the rhetorical and political posture of opposing established order—back to early modern radicalism through Richard Hooker's 1594 analysis. Drawing on Eric Voegelin's reading of Gnosticism in modernity, the author argues that victimary consciousness emerges from a fundamentally modern desire to replace Christian uncertainty with historical certainty. The victimary subject achieves moral credibility precisely by positioning itself against the "establishment," while those defending existing orders are automatically suspect as complicit in their defects. Understanding imperativity requires recognizing how this stance constitutes modernity itself, not merely its recent identity-political manifestations.

## Key Concepts

- [[Victimary stance]]: The rhetorical position of opposing established order while claiming moral authority through that opposition
- [[Gnosticism]]: The heretical impulse (traced from antiquity through modernity) to replace divine mystery with human certainty about history
- [[Imperativity]]: The demand-structure underlying modern consciousness; the force of commands and imperatives that supersede declarative understanding
- [[Resentment]]: The animating passion of revolutionary consciousness that seeks to overturn hierarchies perceived as unjust
- [[Establishment]]: The order being defended; automatically delegitimized in victimary logic through association with complicity
- [[Christian uncertainty]]: The acceptance of mystery and incompleteness characteristic of pre-modern consciousness, rejected by modern resentment

## Notable Passages

> "If you oppose the existing order, all defects are credited to your honesty while defenders of things established can easily be presumed guilty of complicity in its manifold defects."

> "The victimary stance places its targets in a double bind: criticism gains moral credibility while defense becomes automatically suspect."

> "The prevailing form of modern, revolutionary resentment is driven by the desire to replace the 'uncertainty' which 'is the very essence of Christianity' with certainty about history and one's place in it."
