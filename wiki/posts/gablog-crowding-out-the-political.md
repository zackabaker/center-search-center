## Summary

This post argues for replacing the liberal political order by incrementally transforming its institutions rather than through revolutionary rupture. Using the paradox of identity (the Ship of Theseus), the author suggests that institutional change can proceed undetected—replacing one "plank" at a time—until the existing order is no longer recognizable. The key strategy is "crowding out the political": neutralizing the liberal demand that all decisions be publicly contested and opposed, thereby allowing authority, competence, and tradition to function without constant obstruction.

## Key Concepts

- [[Liberal order]]: A system requiring public contestation and explicit opposition to all proposed actions
- [[Paradox of identity]]: The problem of determining when gradual replacement makes something no longer itself
- [[Crowding out the political]]: Reducing the space for political opposition by rendering the electoral process and public debate less relevant
- [[Authority]]: Chains of command, competence, and tradition as alternatives to democratic contestation
- [[Institutional leverage]]: The strategic points where liberal systems can be undermined through analytical decomposition
- [[Memed into existence]]: Using informal, satirical naming to gradually replace existing institutional language
- [[Electoral irrelevance]]: Making voting results vestigial while keeping the form of elections intact

## Notable Passages

> "The assumption that all political intentions be widely telegraphed and explicitly stated is itself a liberal one that liberalism itself never abides by."

> "By 'political,' I mean the assumption that nothing can be done without stimulating opposition... you see an 80% incumbent and think 'totalitarian conformism' rather than 'these people agree.'"

> "Replace one plank at a time, and at the same time stockpile nicely prepared wood and devise some better names for various institutions."

## Questions Raised

1. How does "crowding out the political" differ from the very deception and concealment that liberalism is already accused of practicing—and what prevents it from becoming equally corrupting?

2. If institutional transformation succeeds by remaining largely unnoticed, how can it avoid becoming arbitrary or detached from actual consent or genuine expertise?

3. At what point does making electoral results "irrelevant" require explicit force, and doesn't that threshold mark where the plank-by-plank strategy must finally announce itself as what it is?
