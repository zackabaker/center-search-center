## Summary

This piece critiques the McLuhanite tradition of viewing technology as bodily prosthesis, arguing instead that technology must be understood through the [[imperative]] and [[governance]] rather than desire. The author contends that technology fundamentally organizes collective motions and interconnections from a [[center]], instrumentalizing others in a way that cannot be reduced to individual sensory extension. By grounding technology in [[originary grammar]] and the imperative sequence, GA offers a corrective to theories that flatten power relations into organic metaphors of bodily extension.

## Key Concepts

- [[Imperative]] — A speech form that instrumentalizes another; the foundational mode through which technology organizes collective action
- [[Center]] — The occupant who benefits from extended senses, ears, and limbs distributed across society; the locus of technological control
- [[Prosthesis]] — Misleading metaphor for technology when applied to individual senses; more accurately describes the center's relationship to distributed agents
- [[Governance]] — The proper framework for understanding technology, opposed to the desire-based approach of McLuhan and consumer theory
- [[Originary Grammar]] — The sequence of speech forms (declarative, imperative, etc.) through which technology and social organization must be analyzed
- [[Scenic Construction]] — The design dimension of technology; necessary but insufficient for fundamental thinking about technological systems
- [[Instrumentalization]] — The process by which imperatives remake others as means to an end; reciprocal and self-modifying across hierarchies

## Notable Passages

"We are all prostheses of the center. Imperatives get prolonged and modified along the way, and the instrumentalized instrumentalize themselves and others."

## Questions Raised

1. How do pedagogical imperatives (apprenticeship, teaching) differ fundamentally from instrumental imperatives that create prostheses? Can GA's framework distinguish these without collapsing into either domination or reciprocity?

2. If "we are all prostheses of the center," does this dissolve individual agency entirely, or does the recursion of imperatives (the instrumentalized instrumentalizing others) preserve some space for counter-imperative formation?

3. What would a GA analysis of AI infrastructure look like that takes the imperative as primary rather than falling back on desire-based or sensory-extension metaphors?
