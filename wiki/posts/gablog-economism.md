## Summary

Economism as reductionism—whether Marxist or free-market—fails when it imposes a single economic model onto all social reality. Against this, the author argues that multiple economies coexist and scaffold each other: gift, market, Big Man, imperial, ecumenical, and Judaic. These are not ranked hierarchies but distinct [[mimetic]] structures, each organizing [[joint attention]] differently. Nations survive and thrive by mediating between these plural economies rather than collapsing them into one. The key insight is that all economies are fundamentally economies of [[attention]], not mere material exchange.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Economism]]**: Reductive application of a single economic framework to all social phenomena, ignoring local mediation
- **[[Joint attention]]**: The fundamental human need for shared focus; absence threatens violence rather than indifference
- **[[Mimetic desire]]**: Desire constituted through imitation; scarcity is perpetual because desire itself is mimetic
- **[[Gift economy]]**: Exchange among friends and rivals based on obligation and reciprocity, not calculation
- **[[Imperial economy]]**: Tributary system where margins pay center in exchange for protection and infrastructure (coins, merchant routes)
- **[[Ecumenical economy]]**: Decadent imperial form offering universal inclusion if margins render local economies "innocuous"
- **[[Judaic economy]]**: Internal gift economy (God's donation of Torah) paired with external market relations; mediates resentment of ecumenical power
- **[[Attention economy]]**: All exchange—romantic, political, economic—fundamentally exchanges signs to sustain joint attention

## Notable Passages

> "Economies are always economies of attention: we exchange signs, like glances in the romantic cliché, and the point of exchanging signs is to sustain joint attention in a human world where the alternative to joint attention is not, ultimately, mutual indifference, but violence."

> "The Judaic economy is a gift economy internally and a market economy externally... knowledge which ultimately dissolves all boundaries to exchange between strangers, the antithesis to the gift economy."

> "Nations are powerful, meaningful, resilient and lasting to the extent they mediate in their inner and outer relations to these different economies."

## Questions Raised

1. How do nations navigate the fundamental tension between the ecumenical economy's universalizing impulse and the Judaic economy's resentment of that very universalism without collapsing into either imperialism or victimary reaction?

2. If all economies are economies of [[attention]], can we identify specific [[scenic]] structures or [[declarative]] forms that distinguish one economy from another, or are they ultimately indistinguishable in their mechanisms?

3. What happens when a modern nation-state loses the capacity to mediate between plural economies—i.e., when it ceases to be a "nation" in Gans's sense?
