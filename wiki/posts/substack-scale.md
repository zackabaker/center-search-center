## Summary

This piece critiques what the author calls "sceneless thinking" in mainstream GA—specifically Gans's approach to scenic thinking, which describes others' behavior through the originary scene without accounting for the thinker's own scenic position. The author argues that direct one-to-one mappings of contemporary categories (like "morality" or "Jews") onto the originary scene commit a form of Whig historicism, freezing historical agents in ahistorical poses. True [[scenic thinking]] must address the problem of [[scale]]: how categories gain meanings through historical differentiation that cannot simply be read backward into the origin.

## Key Concepts

- [[Scenic thinking]]: Thought that renders the scenic conditions of discourse itself, not merely describes others' scenes
- [[Sceneless thinking]]: Analysis that omits the thinker's own position, leaving it dependent on institutional frames
- [[Originary scene]]: The hypothetical founding moment of human language and culture
- [[Scale problem]]: The difficulty of mapping contemporary, historically-evolved categories onto the originary scene without anachronism
- [[Categorical anachronism]]: Projecting modern meanings backward (e.g., "morality," "Jews") as if implicit from origin
- [[One-to-one mapping]]: Gans's method of finding direct equivalents between originary and contemporary scenes
- [[Whig historicism]]: Teleological assumption that present meanings represent cumulative wisdom already present at origin

## Notable Passages

> "If the scenic conditions of your own discourse are not translated or translatable into that discourse, in my view you aren't really thinking scenically—yours is a sceneless thinking."

> "Jews are perpetually frozen, much like the figures on Keats's Grecian Urn, in the pose of those who invented the one true God...The question of why anyone would resent those called 'Jews' today for a discovery made by some very different people who weren't even called 'Jews' 2,500 years ago, never gets asked."

> "A term like 'moral' is a latecomer to human societies, one that has taken on a distinctive meaning in certain sectors of the modern West...you have to acknowledge that 'moral' is a modification [of earlier forms]."

## Questions Raised

1. If everything human traces back to the originary scene, how do we theorize genuine historical emergence—moments where new categories crystallize that weren't latent at origin?

2. What would a properly scaled [[scenic thinking]] look like that avoids both Gans's freezing of categories and a pure historicism that severs present from origin?

3. Does the author's critique require abandoning one-to-one mapping entirely, or refining it to account for the different historical "layers" through which categories pass?
