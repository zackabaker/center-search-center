## Summary

This essay examines the paradoxical prestige of radical leftist intellectuals within the academy, arguing that their extreme views function not as handicaps but as assets—allowing academics to perform vicarious critique of the systems they depend upon. Through close analysis of Eisenstein's *Bezhin Meadow*, the author demonstrates how leftist violence and resentment are aestheticized and celebrated as "lyrical," while equivalent right-wing work faces moral condemnation. The piece asks why campuses embrace radical left thinkers while excluding right-wing ones, suggesting this reveals something fundamental about how [[victimary progressivism]] operates within institutional structures.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Victimary progressivism]]**: A political framework that defines itself entirely through resentment and denunciation of enemies, using moral claims to mask violent impulses
- **[[Resentment]]**: The emotional fuel of leftist ideology, structured as righteous anger at systemic injustice
- **[[Declarative culture]]**: The power to name and frame reality through institutional pronouncement (here, the academy's authority to declare who deserves praise or condemnation)
- **[[Moral reasoning]]**: The framework by which intellectuals justify selective application of ethical standards
- **[[Sacral violence]]**: Ritualized destruction (of churches, kulaks, traditions) presented as necessary purification
- **[[Academic left]]**: The institutionalized radical intelligentsia that enjoys prestige precisely through radical critique

## Questions Raised

1. How does the academy's embrace of radical left ideology while excluding right-wing thinkers reveal the actual structure of power in contemporary institutions—is it itself a form of [[declarative culture]] that conceals rather than exposes domination?

2. What distinguishes the [[resentment]] that animates leftist critique from other forms of resentment, and why does institutional prestige attach specifically to leftist rather than other expressions of it?

3. Can the selectivity in moral judgment (condemning Nazi aesthetics while praising Soviet aesthetics) be overcome through better reasoning, or is it structurally embedded in how institutions protect their own legitimacy?
