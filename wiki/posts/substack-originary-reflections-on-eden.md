## Summary

This essay reconsiders the Adam and Eve story as a product of the Babylonian exile period, arguing that it functions as a narrative about humans as "always already" exiled. Rather than deriving directly from Mesopotamian sources like *Gilgamesh*, the author suggests the Eden story bears Persian literary influence and may be a universalized version of stories about royal decision-making, particularly how women exercise informal influence over male rulers. The text explores how the garden setting, the theme of feminine persuasion, and the expulsion narrative all reflect concerns specific to post-exilic Jewish thought about power, desire, and human limitation.

## Key Concepts

- [[Exilic literature]]: Biblical texts written or revised during/after the Babylonian exile that encode political theology through myth
- [[Anti-imperialist imperialism]]: The paradoxical stance of monotheism and liberalism that resist empire while asserting their own universal claims
- [[Textual layering]]: The compositional practice of revising and appropriating rival imperial mythologies into sacred narrative
- [[Mimetic desire]]: The mechanism by which one agent's choice influences another's, here figured through Eve's persuasion of Adam
- [[The originary scene]]: The founding moment of human culture, here reconsidered as an exilic consciousness
- [[Gender and power]]: The literary exploration of how informal, subtle influence (particularly feminine) shapes consequential decisions

## Notable Passages

> "The Adam and Eve story is at best vaguely related to *Gilgamesh*... it seems to me that the story is very much one of exile—humans as 'always already' exiled."

> "This concern could only have become explicit and interesting in the case of kings, who really made the only consequential decisions in the human orders under consideration here."

> "The Adam and Eve story is a revision of a kind of story told of kings, here 'universalized' and situated at the origin of humanity."

## Questions Raised

1. If the Eden story is indeed a universalized revision of royal decision-making narratives, what does this tell us about how [[mimetic desire]] and gender operate differently at the level of originary anthropology versus political history?

2. How does understanding Eden as exilic literature (rather than cosmogonic myth) change our reading of [[the originary scene]] itself—is exile constitutive of human consciousness rather than merely a historical accident?

3. Does the Persian literary context (gardens, feminine influence, reversals of fate) suggest that post-exilic Jewish thought was fundamentally shaped by Persian administrative and literary models in ways we've underestimated?
