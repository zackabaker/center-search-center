## Summary

This post examines the rhetorical and moral relationship between victimary discourse and political violence, specifically analyzing how the framing of systemic injustice as requiring immediate redress creates a discursive environment that can justify or inspire violent action. Using the 2014 killing of NYPD officers Wenjin Liu and Raphael Ramos by Ismailly Brinsley as a focal point, the author argues that when activists and leaders deploy clichés like "no justice, no peace" seriously rather than performatively, they cross a threshold from protest into implicit endorsement of asymmetrical warfare. The piece contends that [[victimary progressivism]] operates through calculated chaos and demands for "inconvenience" that contain no logical stopping point once violence enters the equation.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Victimary culture]]** — A political framework centering on victim status and grievance as the primary claim to moral authority and social redress
- **[[Declarative culture]]** — Culture constituted through declarations and proclamations that reshape the discursive field and what counts as truth
- **[[Resentment]]** — The emotional and moral fuel of victimary politics; the grammar underlying demands for retribution
- **[[Moral responsibility]]** — The question of whether ideological figures bear causal responsibility for violent acts performed by followers
- **[[Asymmetrical warfare]]** — Framing political action as combat where designated enemies become legitimate targets for violence
- **[[Inconvenience]]** — The rhetorical minimization of disruptive tactics (occupation, shutdown) that logically escalate toward violence
- **[[Complicity]]** — The accusatory framework that distributes moral guilt across entire institutions or demographics

## Notable Passages

> "Once you say that people need to be 'inconvenienced' in the name of justice, the next question is how severe and chronic must the inconvenience be? As severe as the injustice, presumably; or more simply, as severe as 'necessary.'"

> "If you think of what you are doing as asymmetrical warfare, police officers are certainly legitimate targets. And why shouldn't occupiers think of themselves this way? What moral principle restrains them?"

> "People are just starting to figure out what these clichés really mean, if you take them seriously."

## Questions Raised

1. Where is the principled moral boundary between holding speakers responsible for their words' effects and suppressing free speech—and does that boundary hold once violence actually occurs?

2. If victimary frameworks logically contain no stopping point once violence is justified, are they inherently destabilizing to political order, or is there an internal mechanism of moderation we're missing?

3. What distinguishes "playing with fire" (performative victimary politics) from genuine revolutionary intent, and can leaders maintain that distinction once their rhetoric has been taken literally by followers?
