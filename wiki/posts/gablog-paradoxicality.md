## Summary

This post argues that paradoxicality—the constitutive contradiction at the heart of human meaning-making—is not a bug to be eliminated but the very foundation of language and thought. Drawing on the [[originary event]], the author shows how [[ostensivity]] itself is paradoxical: we create the sacred object through naming while recognizing it as already present. Rather than seeking to resolve paradox, genuine dialogue between faiths should embrace various ways of "dwelling within" this founding paradox, and the same paradoxical structure repeats across all grammatical modes—imperative, declarative, ostensive.

## Key Concepts

- [[Originary event]]: The moment humans point to and name the central object, creating meaning through deferral of mimetic violence
- [[Ostensivity]]: The paradoxical act of reference that creates what it appears merely to recognize
- [[Declarative culture]]: Language use that seeks clarity and unanimity by embedding imperatives in a shared representational world
- [[Paradoxicality]]: The irreducible self-referential contradiction constitutive of all human meaning
- [[Disciplinary space]]: A community of discourse defined by shared conceptual frameworks and representational traditions
- [[Imperative]]: The asymmetrical command relation paradoxically dependent on the subordinate's fulfillment

## Notable Passages

> "Paradoxicality is the non-material reality theists are always arguing with atheists about, and if there is to be some kind of dialogue between the different faiths, it would far better be constructed around the assumption that there are various ways of dwelling within the founding paradox."

> "The more you seek absolute clarity, the more you approximate complete idiosyncrasy; the more precise, micrological and self-reflexive, the more you anticipate a possible universal scene."

> "Ostensivity itself" is paradoxical because we refer to something "created by and yet pre-existing our referring to it."

## Questions Raised

1. If paradoxicality is irreducible and constitutive, what distinguishes productive "dwelling within" paradox from paralyzing relativism or bad faith?

2. How does the oscillation between clarity and idiosyncrasy in disciplinary discourse map onto the actual historical emergence and transformation of epistemic communities?

3. Can the paradox of the declarative—that perfect clarity requires idiosyncratic precision—be operationalized as a principle for identifying when a discourse has genuinely become intelligible to a new audience?
