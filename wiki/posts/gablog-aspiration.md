## Summary

This post challenges postliberal dreams of unified social projects by grounding aspiration in the [[originary scene]] and its logic of [[deferral]]. Rather than society needing a collectively chosen goal, genuine aspiration emerges from mimetic practice—the observation that all human acts are imitations embedded in particular scenes. True originality requires recognizing mimetic saturation while discovering points where competing imitations cancel themselves out, creating space for creative transformation. The author argues that social coherence emerges organically from attention to these mimetic dynamics rather than from imposed consensus.

## Key Concepts

- [[Originary scene]]: The founding moment of human community where deferral of appropriation transforms a carcass into a sacred being, initiating morality and ritual.
- [[Deferral]]: A negative act (restraint) that becomes immediately positive and creative by opening new perception and meaning.
- [[Mimesis]]: The universal mimetic construction underlying all human acts, gestures, and desires—never perfect, always scene-dependent.
- [[Postliberalism]]: Political orientation seeking unified social projects with unanimous consent, yet obscuring organic grounding of aspiration.
- [[Mimic self-cancellation]]: The point where everyone imitating the same thing makes that imitation impossible, revealing creative potential.
- [[Scene]]: The particular context that gives meaning to any act or gesture through its embedding in social practice.

## Notable Passages

> "If the question becomes, 'to what should we, as a society, aspire,' we already border on the ridiculous—it sounds like we're filling a slot in a questionnaire, rather than pursuing something organically grounded in our practices and institutions."

> "If we want to be original, we must first divest ourselves of our presumptions of originality."

> "Any creative or original act will be one that modifies that scene so that the 'mimism' can convert itself in such a way that imitative 'drift' provides for the flourishing of the mimism."

## Questions Raised

1. How do we practically distinguish between mimetic saturation that produces creative self-cancellation versus mere stagnation or repetitive decline?

2. If all social aspiration must ground itself organically in mimetic practice rather than conscious collective choice, what role remains for deliberate institutional design or reform?

3. Can postliberal movements recognize their own mimetic entanglement without collapsing their political projects?
