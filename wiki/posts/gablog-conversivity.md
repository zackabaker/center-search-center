# Conversivity

## Summary

This post argues that genuine political and pedagogical engagement operates through [[aesthetic representation]] rather than accusation or logical argument. Drawing on Hamlet's play-within-a-play, the author shows how making implicit social roles and modes of address explicit can solicit a more authentic response from an interlocutor than direct confrontation. The key insight is that all social activity is fundamentally [[scenic]] and [[mimetic]]—no neutral address exists—and effective pedagogy works by accentuating the scene itself rather than trying to impose predetermined conclusions.

## Key Concepts

- [[Scenic representation]]: The foregrounding of the mimetic, performative character of social interaction to make implicit roles explicit
- [[Mode of address]]: The implicit positioning of interlocutors (friend/enemy, leader/follower) that structures every utterance
- [[Sovereign imaginary]]: The other's self-representation within a scene; what can be solicited through aesthetic means
- [[Aesthetic-political pedagogy]]: Engagement through representation rather than accusation or logical proof
- [[Mimetic anthropology]]: The recognition that all social activity is fundamentally imitative and scene-dependent
- [[Conversion]] (implied): The transformation achieved when one's implicit role becomes explicitly recognizable

## Notable Passages

> "Hamlet's abandoned method is a model of political-pedagogical engagement—a much more effective one than accusations of some kind of betrayal, or attempts through argument to convince the other with lists of pros and cons."

> "The aesthetic-pedagogical stance is to accentuate the mode of address—to make what is implicit in it a bit more explicit... The point is to exhaust the implications of the scene."

> "To turn an implicit role into an explicit one is to foreground the mimetic and scenic character of all social activity."

## Questions Raised

1. How does the author's model distinguish between legitimate "accentuation" of a scene and manipulative theatrical framing? What prevents conversivity from becoming propaganda?

2. Can this method scale beyond intimate or small-group settings to mass political engagement, or does it necessarily depend on conditions of relative naiveté about representation?

3. What happens when both parties recognize the scene as scene—does awareness of its constructed nature exhaust its pedagogical force, or does it open new possibilities?
