# Media as Scene

## Summary

This essay develops a GA-consistent definition of media as whatever enables the constitution of a [[scene]]. Rather than drawing rigid distinctions between [[sign]] and medium, the author argues they shade into one another—what matters is understanding their functional relationship in generating shared attention. The [[originary scene]] provides the model: a circular configuration where all participants see both the object and each other seeing it. All subsequent media—writing, film, radio—are modeled on this speech scene structure, even when separated by vast temporal and spatial distances. The essay suggests that media should be understood through the scenes they generate, including the hermeneutic scenes of reading, commentary, and discussion that texts perpetually create.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Scene]]**: A spatial-temporal configuration where participants' attention is coordinated through shared reference to an object or sign.
- **[[Sign]]**: An instrument of attention-direction that both depends on and constitutes the scene; shades into its medium without clear boundary.
- **[[Originary scene]]**: The circular gathering where all see both the central object and each other seeing it; the primordial model for all subsequent media.
- **[[Medium]]**: The condition or configuration enabling a sign's effective constitution and transmission across participants or time.
- **[[Reciprocity]]**: The mutual visibility and acknowledgment among scene participants; foundational to meaningful communication.
- **[[Attention]]**: The directional focus coordinated by a sign within a scene; what media ultimately organize and distribute.
- **[[Hermeneutics]]**: The tradition understanding text transmission as ongoing dialogue between readers across time, preserving scenic relations.

## Notable Passages

> "The 'media' is whatever enables the constitution of a scene. A sign directs participants on a scene's attention in a particular way and thereby constitutes the scene; but, reciprocally, relying on the elements of the scene makes the sign possible."

> "No matter how technologically advanced and no matter how much temporal and spatial distance they put between the users, we always use terms from a speech scene—'conversation,' 'dialogue,' 'discuss,' etc."

> "If we read a poem by Juvenal, do I have a scenic relation to an ancient Roman who read it? We're not looking for yes/no answers here: if we take that as a genuine question, we would try to answer it by expanding our conception of 'media.'"

## Questions Raised

1. **Where exactly is the boundary between sign and medium, and does GA require us to abandon the quest for such boundaries entirely?** The essay suggests we should focus on functional relationships rather than classification—but does this dissolve the conceptual distinction or merely defer it?

2. **Can all media truly be modeled on the speech scene?** What about asynchronous, one-way, or algorithmic media that seem to violate the reciprocity principle—are these genuinely exceptional or do they reveal something about the framework itself?

3. **Does the hermeneutic tradition's notion of dialogue across centuries preserve genuine scenic relation, or does it risk anthropomorphizing the text?** How does GA distinguish between metaphorical and literal claims about scenic participation?
