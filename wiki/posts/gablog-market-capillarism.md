## Summary

The market emerges wherever [[central authority]] cannot directly supervise all interactions—it is the space of exchanges operating "under the radar" of comprehensive control. Money functions as both a tool of individual flexibility and an instrument of [[central authority]], introducing [[abstraction]] that separates groups from their embedded settings and mobilizes them as capital. The modern liberal order sacralizes market power over central authority, yet this depends entirely on the center's foundational role; the text suggests this unresolved tension defines contemporary politics and the impossibility of pure [[market]] autonomy.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Market]]** — interactions generated by free choices under partial but not total supervision by central authority
- **[[Central Authority]]** — the institutional power that maintains social order while necessarily leaving margins for unsupervised exchange
- **[[Abstraction]]** — the power to separate individuals and groups from their embedded settings, enabling mobilization
- **[[Capital]]** — abstraction extended to disciplines, knowledges, media, and technologies themselves
- **[[Money]]** — debt owned by central authority that expands market capacity while simultaneously competing with and potentially marketizing the center itself
- **[[Supervision]]** — the threshold of oversight that shapes whether market activity enhances or threatens institutional function
- **[[Embedment]]** — the traditionalist resistance to abstraction through commitment to full social situatedness

## Notable Passages

"The market, in its most abstract, praxeological terms, is understood as the interactions generated by the free choices of individuals. As an ontology, this is absurd, if for no other reason than that no one chooses the language in which these choices are made."

"Money is introduced as debt, debt which is ultimately owned by the central authority... The more money in the system, the more the central authority is likely to be marketized as well."

"The market is still inconceivable without the central authority, reconceived as a political market, in which citizenship is defined as a certain quantity of tokens authorizing one to make withdrawals from the center."

## Questions Raised

1. If [[money]] necessarily marketizes even [[central authority]] itself as it increases in circulation, what structural mechanism could arrest or stabilize this process without returning to pre-market embedment?

2. How does the distinction between market [[abstraction]] and the traditionalist ideal of full [[embedment]] map onto the originary scene—was the market always latent in the deferral of [[central authority]]?

3. Can [[liberal democracy]]'s consecration of market power over the center ultimately survive the recognition that markets are entirely dependent on centralized debt-creation and enforcement?
