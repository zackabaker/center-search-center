## Summary

This text develops a theory of political and ethical decision-making grounded in [[marginalism]]—the observation that all meaningful choices reduce to binary decisions at the moment of commitment. Drawing from [[generative anthropology]], the author traces how every binary choice retrojects a history of prior bifurcations and how criteria for choosing only become explicit *after the act*. The framework explains how [[resentment]] structures political consciousness, how thresholds of scene-collapse emerge tacitly before articulation, and how individual decisions are always already embedded in the scenarios they construct. Political change becomes intelligible not as rational deliberation but as crossing a threshold whose reality is confirmed only retrospectively.

## Key Concepts

- [[Marginalism]]: Decision-making at the margin where infinite possibilities collapse into binary choice (a or not-a)
- [[Originary Scene]]: The founding human scene where the choice between mimetic seizure and deferral through the sign first crystallized
- [[Binary Structure]]: All meaningful decisions reduce to a binary, embedded in a history of prior bifurcations
- [[Resentment]]: The fundamentally binary emotion that structures political consciousness (he or me; him or not-him)
- [[Threshold]]: The tacit crossing-point where a scene becomes unsustainable and transition to a new scene becomes possible
- [[Scene Reconstruction]]: The constant retroactive reframing of the scene to justify or reinterpret one's choice
- [[Tacit Criteria]]: Decision-making grounds that remain implicit until disclosed by the act itself

## Notable Passages

> "Since the right choice was made on the scene, it is impossible for us not to think of ourselves as making the right choice now: even if I egregiously violate the terms of the scene I am on I will reconstruct another scene upon which no such violation took place."

> "Criteria for choosing one way or another are always embedded in the binary situation, but only become explicit after the fact, once the act has disclosed the scene I am on now."

> "There must always be such thresholds—for there to be a scene is for the scene to be capable of collapse into the desires and resentments it has deferred."

## Questions Raised

1. How do we distinguish between genuine threshold-crossing (where a scene truly becomes unsustainable) and mere rationalization of choices already tacitly made for other reasons?

2. If all scenes can be retroactively reconstructed to justify any choice, what prevents total moral relativism—and does marginalism require some grounding outside the binary itself?

3. How does the tacit detection of thresholds in collective consciousness (e.g., widespread demoralization) propagate or synchronize across individuals without explicit communication?
