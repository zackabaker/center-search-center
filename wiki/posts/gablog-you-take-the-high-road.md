## Summary

This post examines Daniel Greenfield's distinction between "high" and "low" roads in fighting political correctness, reading it as an oblique critique of alt-right provocateurs like Milo Yiannopoulos. The author questions whether Greenfield's preference for principled argumentation over populist vulgarity is coherent, given that ridicule itself is a time-honored political tool. The piece probes tensions within conservative anti-PC discourse and the emerging right's difficulty establishing clear boundaries around acceptable transgression.

## Key Concepts

- [[Political Correctness]]: Institutionalized victimary rhetoric that frames power as victimhood
- [[Victimocracy]]: A political structure where victim status confers authority and shields from critique
- [[Populist vulgarity]]: Low-status speech modes contrasted with procedural/principled argument
- [[Declarative culture]]: The power to define acceptable discourse and enforce compliance
- [[The victimary]]: Those who deploy victim narratives as political weapons
- [[Resentment]]: Underlying motor of PC activism and its opposition

## Notable Passages

> "The low road is enjoyable, but plays into the portrayal of politically correct activists as victims. The high road exposes them for the totalitarian bullies that they truly are."

> "Greenfield surely shares much of the alt-right's critique of mainstream conservatism, especially on the point he is addressing here—the seemingly congenital inability of those conservatives to resist the victimary onslaught."

> "Greenfield's caution makes it difficult to tell exactly what he is against, and why."

## Questions Raised

1. Can anti-victimocracy rhetoric maintain coherent boundaries between justified ridicule and "low" vulgarity, or is the distinction ultimately serving a different declarative function?

2. Does the "high road" of principled argument actually prove more effective at destabilizing [[victimocracy]], or does it cede the attentional economy to more transgressive speech acts?

3. What does Greenfield's caution about the alt-right reveal about the right's difficulty establishing a genuine alternative center, versus merely positioning itself on the margin of the left's declarative authority?
