## Summary

This post critiques the moral logic dominating contemporary discourse on the Gaza conflict, arguing that casualty counts have become the primary metric for judging warfare—a framework that actually incentivizes generating civilian victims. The author defends the concept of proportionality in war as a matter of using necessary force to achieve legitimate aims, not matching firepower. He contends that criticism of Israeli self-defense often masks a genocidal position, and warns against the fantasy that forceful responses can "solve" the problem of Palestinian resentment, while maintaining they remain necessary to prevent escalation.

## Key Concepts

- [[Proportionality in war]]: Using the minimum force necessary to achieve legitimate military aims, not matching an opponent's available weapons
- [[Victimary logic]]: A moral framework that privileges suffering and casualty counts as the primary measure of justice, inverting rational thought
- [[Resentment]]: The deep historical grievance that prevents rational negotiation and perpetuates cycles of violence
- [[Self-defense]]: The fundamental right of a state to protect its citizens from attack; denying this right is equivalent to endorsing genocide
- [[Declarative culture]]: The realm of moral pronouncements disconnected from practical consequences or logical consistency
- [[The cycle of violence]]: The assumption that forceful responses fail because problems recur, ignoring that deterrence works by prevention, not elimination

## Notable Passages

"If one side wins by having the most civilian victims, then an incentive is created for that side to generate as many civilian victims as possible. As is often the case, what looks like scrupulousness is really a justification for barbarism."

"If no Israeli self-defense is permissible, then the only conclusion to be drawn is that Israelis should allow themselves to be slaughtered. In other words, the critic of Israel is a genocidal anti-Semite."

"For the forseeable future, the Palestinians will find no way out of their resentments other than fantasies of Israel's destruction...nevertheless, in both cases forceful responses can prevent things from getting much worse than they might otherwise be."

## Questions Raised

1. If casualty counts cannot be the metric for judging proportionality in war, what institutional or declarative mechanisms could actually constrain military force in ways that satisfy both justice and practical necessity?

2. Does the author's analogy between Israel's cycles of deterrence and society's repeated imprisonment of criminals adequately account for the political and symbolic dimensions of the Israeli-Palestinian conflict that distinguish it from criminal justice?

3. How can one coherently maintain that forceful responses are necessary *and* that resentment will persist indefinitely—what role, if any, can political resolution play?
