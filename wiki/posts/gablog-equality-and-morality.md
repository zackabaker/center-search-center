## Summary

This essay challenges the moral use of "equality" as a concept, arguing that it obscures rather than clarifies group dynamics. The author contends that all group cooperation involves hierarchical ordering—someone must lead, others follow—and that describing this as "spontaneous" or "voluntary" collapses into incoherence. Rather than debating equality, moral discourse should focus on the ontological facts of group formation and the functional requirements of coordination, particularly how leadership emerges without coercive force.

## Key Concepts

- [[Originary scene]] — The foundational moment of human coordination from which all subsequent group dynamics derive
- [[Ontological questions]] — Fundamental questions about being and group structure that precede empirical observation
- [[Spontaneity]] — A descriptive claim about group formation that obscures the sequential ordering necessarily present in any coordination
- [[Leadership]] — The inevitable emergence of directional force within groups, independent of formal authority or coercion
- [[Declarative culture]] — The systems by which groups establish and maintain shared understandings through utterance and coordination
- [[Hierarchical ordering]] — The necessary temporal and functional structure of any group action
- [[Voluntary cooperation]] — The appearance of choice that masks underlying ontological realities of following and leading

## Notable Passages

> "To describe any group as organizing itself spontaneously collapses into incoherence. They can't all act simultaneously, can they? If not, one is in the lead at any moment, and the others are following, in some order that we could identify."

> "Democracy in this sense doesn't deny that some people may have better judgment than others, but it permits unanimous cooperation, and I venture to say, corresponds to 'natural' human interaction since the originary scene."

> "The question, then, is which provides the better description? I think that the absolutist ontology I propose does, because to describe any group as organizing itself spontaneously collapses into incoherence."

## Questions Raised

1. If all group action necessarily involves sequential ordering and leadership, what distinguishes genuinely just governance from merely efficient hierarchy?

2. How can we develop a moral vocabulary that acknowledges ontological realities of leadership while still critiquing unjust power structures?

3. Does rejecting "equality" as a moral concept leave us without resources to resist authoritarian coordination, or does it clarify what we should actually be defending?
