## Summary

This essay asks what a [[market]] would look like if it explicitly acknowledged its dependence on [[central power]] and on the moral-ethical welfare of workers and consumers. Using the heroin trade as a limiting case of anti-social exchange, the author explores how individual and firm choices could orient toward societal flourishing rather than extraction. By examining markets broadly—from sexual markets to warrior hierarchies in Homer—the text traces how [[value]] assessment and [[exchange]] interact with constraining institutions, suggesting that markets and institutions co-create one another rather than following linear progression.

## Key Concepts

- [[Market]]: An arena of systematic exchange; extends metaphorically beyond monetary transactions
- [[Central Power]]: The state authority on which market function depends and to which markets create feedback effects
- [[Exchange]]: Give-and-take interaction, asymmetrical but reciprocal, distinct from [[communism]] and [[hierarchy]]
- [[Value]]: What is assessed and traded; more directly testable in pre-monetary markets (warrior prowess, beauty, alphas)
- [[Hierarchy]]: Unidirectional power relation; shades into exchange at its margins
- [[Communism]]: Taking what one needs, giving what one can; found in families and teams
- [[Moral-Ethical Life]]: Long-term social sustainability; the welfare of workers, consumers, and the broader society

## Notable Passages

"If I sell heroin, I'm going to keep running out of customers, because they will keep dying or ruining their lives and therefore be unable to pay me anymore."

"The better warrior, the more beautiful woman, the more alpha man—these values can be tested fairly easily, and virtual unanimity achieved."

"It may very well be that, rather than a strict linear procession of markets, constraining institutions, modified markets, the markets and institutions are co-created in various ways in different situations."

## Questions Raised

1. How do we design feedback mechanisms so that market actors internalize the long-term social costs their choices generate, without collapsing into paternalism or state control?

2. If markets and institutions co-create one another rather than follow linear progression, what determines which institution-market pairs are stable or unstable in a given era?

3. Can modern monetary markets recover the directness of value assessment found in pre-monetary markets (where warrior prowess or beauty could achieve "virtual unanimity"), or is opacity a permanent feature of complex exchange?
