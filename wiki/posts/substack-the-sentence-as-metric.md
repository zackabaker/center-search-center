## Summary

The author argues that the [[declarative sentence]] provides a metric for understanding media evolution from orality through literacy to digital conditions. Against the "digital orality" thesis, which sees social media as retribalizing communication, he proposes that [[declarativity]] itself transforms across media regimes. The shift from oral imperative forms (oaths, curses, threats) to written "classic prose" (which creates a fictional shared scene) represents a crucial development in how language objectifies reality. The question becomes: what happens to this declarative regime in contemporary digital conditions that both resurrect performativity *and* introduce surveillance?

## Key Concepts

- [[Declarativity]] – The isolatable, analyzable form of the declarative sentence that emerges distinctly in writing, distinct from oral imperatives
- [[Classic prose]] – A literary form (per Thomas & Turner) that creates a fictional scene where reader and writer presumably see the same thing
- [[Metalanguage of literacy]] – Olson's concept: writing records another's speech via linguistic formulas that filter out mimetic performance (e.g., "he exclaimed" vs. direct imitation)
- [[Digital orality]] – The thesis that social media retribalizes communication by restoring oral performativity and polarization
- [[Performativity]] – The recovered dimension of language in digital media that undermines the neutralizing pretensions of classic prose
- [[The Stack]] – The order of intelligence operating beyond and through digital orality; the infrastructure that a consumerist media analysis omits

## Notable Passages

> "The declarative sentence, under orality, would always approximate the imperative, in the form of oaths, curses, prayers, accusations, prophecies, threats, spells, and so on."

> "Classic prose creates a fictional scene upon which the reader is placed—you describe, as a writer, what both you and your reader can presumably see in front of you."

> "My own follow-up hypothesis is that all the disciplines, starting with philosophy, are attempts to ontologize these formulas of implication rather than contribute to their stacking in scenes."

## Questions Raised

1. If [[declarativity]] in classic prose depends on a shared fictional scene between writer and reader, what happens when digital surveillance makes the *actual* scene (of data collection, algorithmic mediation) invisible to the reader—does declarativity collapse into mere performative noise?

2. Does the author's distinction between oral imperative and written declarative survive the digital condition, or do we need an entirely new category beyond the binary of "orality vs. literacy"?

3. How does the "Stack"—the infrastructural order he mentions but doesn't fully elaborate—relate to the recovery of performativity in digital media? Is it another regime of declarativity altogether?
