## Summary

This post traces the ideological roots of contemporary political correctness to post-WWII Western self-doubt, arguing that influential Western intellectuals created a climate of pessimism that led them to romanticize any opposition to the West—from Maoism to the PLO—regardless of objective merit or moral content. Drawing on David Pryce-Jones's *The Closed Circle*, the author identifies this pattern as stemming from Romantic-era skepticism about industrialization, which mutated into a compulsive tendency to blame Western power for global ills. Rather than temporary aberration, this dynamic has become a permanent feature of democratic societies' self-relation.

## Key Concepts

- [[White Guilt]] — Western self-blame for historical and contemporary inequities, rooted in Romantic pessimism about modernity
- [[Victimocracy]] — political order structured around the authority of victimhood claims and grievance narratives
- [[Declarative Culture]] — societies organized through performative assertions of values (here: self-criticism as moral performance)
- [[Democratic Doubt]] — structural tendency of democracies to accommodate hostile criticism and self-question
- [[Romanticism]] — aesthetic-philosophical movement that idealized pre-industrial life and opposed rationalist modernity
- [[Aberrant Period]] — the post-1945 era of Western self-undermining, though presented as permanent rather than aberrant

## Notable Passages

"Citizens of democracies incline to attend to their critics and to accommodate through intellectual speculation even the most hostile views...doubt, therefore self-doubt too, must always be in play."

"To believe that the blame for the ills and barbarities of other nations or cultures lay with the West was another instance of Eurocentric self-importance, a gratuitous posture."

"The Palestine Liberation Organization was a phenomenon of this aberrant period"—yet the period has proven no aberration but a juggernaut.

## Questions Raised

1. If [[democratic doubt]] is structurally necessary to democracy's functioning, how can societies resist the pathological variant that Pryce-Jones describes without abandoning democratic openness itself?

2. Is the equation of anti-Western positioning with moral virtue a permanent feature of [[declarative culture]], or are there historical moments when it can be arrested or reversed?

3. How does the Romantic-era critique of industrial dehumanization differ substantively from contemporary critiques, and why has its descendant become immune to empirical refutation?
