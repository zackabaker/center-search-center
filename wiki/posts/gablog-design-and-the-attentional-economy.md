## Summary

This essay challenges the "canonical" GA assumption that the Christian revelation against scapegoating necessarily entails a mythology of moral equality and individual dignity. The author argues instead that the prohibition on scapegoating requires only a [[semiotics of marking]]—attention to how framings incite social responses—rather than an ontology of the individual. Design thinking provides the framework: individuals are best understood as sustainable [[centers of attention]] whose construction is fundamentally pragmatic, not metaphysical. This reorientation opens space for acknowledging actual differences among people without collapsing into [[violent centralization]].

## Key Concepts

- [[Scapegoating]] — violent [[centralization]] of community crisis onto a marked victim rendered retroactively "guilty"
- [[Post-sacrificial morality]] — social order where scapegoating is universally recognized as wrong, requiring elaborate rationalization
- [[Moral equality]] — the canonical GA claim that dignity/consciousness ground a prohibition on marking; challenged here as unnecessary mythology
- [[Centers of attention]] — the sustainable, constructed self; an alternative to ontologies of the soul or intrinsic dignity
- [[Semiotics of marking]] — how framings and designations incite predictable social responses across different audiences
- [[Christian revelation]] — scriptural exposure of sacrifice's "bad faith"; knowledge that victim-choice has nothing to do with the victim
- [[Violent centralization]] — the author's preferred term for what Girard calls scapegoating

## Notable Passages

> "The social injunction to refrain from scapegoating implies nothing, and need imply nothing, regarding the 'being' of the potential victim."

> "Rather than such projections, all we need to be able to say about the self is that it is continually constructed as a sustainable center of attention, of others and the self itself."

> "A cataloguing of such 'markings' would tell us nothing about individuals, but only of possible social constructions of them."

## Questions Raised

1. If we abandon the mythology of moral equality for a semiotics of marking, does this leave us vulnerable to justified claims that we're merely describing power relations rather than prescribing ethics?

2. How does the shift from ontology to pragmatics change our ability to critique actual scapegoating mechanisms—do we lose normative ground?

3. What would a fully developed "design" approach to social prohibition look like, and how would it differ from existing frameworks in addressing contemporary forms of stigmatization?
