## Summary

The author argues that "post-sacrificial culture" is a useful but ultimately incomplete placeholder term for describing modernity's transformation of sacrifice from literal to figurative exchange. While we still engage in [[imperative exchange]] with institutions—assuming compliance will yield reward—we find it embarrassing to name this as "sacrifice" to abstract ideas. The breakdown occurred because [[mimetic desire]] reveals that sacred centrality derives from human focus rather than inherent divine attributes, and human sacrifice inevitably emerges when [[sacrifice]] logic reaches its limit, forcing cultures toward juridical systems and regularized compensation rather than violent ritual.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Sacrifice]]**: Imperative exchange where community members offer fruits of deity-provided abundance in return for continued provision and peace
- **[[Imperative exchange]]**: Contractual obligation between a center and its subjects, foundational to both sacrificial and institutional relations
- **[[Axial Age acquisitions]]**: Intellectual and spiritual developments that discredited literal sacrifice and shifted it to figural forms
- **[[Mimetic desire]]**: Human focusing that confers centrality on sacred beings, revealing sacrifice's fraudulent basis in collective attention
- **[[Sacred king]]**: Early centralizing figure who mediated cosmos and community, eventually ritualized and replaced by bureaucratic layers
- **[[Institutional order]]**: Modern systems substituting regularized compensation and justice for sacrificial violence
- **[[Centrality]]**: The positioning effect produced by collective focus rather than inherent attribute

## Notable Passages

"We speak in terms of trust, consent, contract, and so on, and keep extending those terms into areas where the 'superstitious' nature of our 'faith' on institutional structures would be embarrassing."

"It's our own mimetic desires that confer centrality on the 'sacred' being, not any attribute of the being itself."

"Human sacrifice emerges along with human hierarchy, as the first figures to make a claim to permanent centrality were sacral kings, who were no doubt often killed in manners that...became increasingly ritualized."

## Questions Raised

1. If all institutional systems remain fundamentally structured by [[imperative exchange]], what would a genuinely non-sacrificial society require? Can we think beyond this anthropological constant?

2. How does the transition from sacral kingship through bureaucratic layers to modern justice systems illuminate current crises in institutional legitimacy—are we witnessing the collapse of the compensation system itself?

3. Does the embarrassment we feel naming institutional compliance as "sacrifice" represent moral progress or merely the aestheticization of the same violent centralizing mechanism?
