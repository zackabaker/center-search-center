## Summary

This essay examines Abraham's negotiation with God over Sodom and Gomorrah as a foundational moment in moral history—a shift from collective punishment toward individual culpability. The author argues the story is a post-exilic reworking of older material, where scribes transformed an ancestral narrative about Abraham's military victory into a meditation on ethical discrimination. By reconstructing the historical layers beneath the text, the essay suggests Abraham's pleading represents the emergence of [[juridical thinking]] that distinguishes the innocent from the guilty, prefiguring a God-individual relationship that bypasses collective sacrifice.

## Key Concepts

- [[Collective punishment]] — destroying entire populations regardless of individual guilt; the pre-juridical norm Abraham challenges
- [[Post-exilic interpretation]] — scribal reworking of inherited traditions to construct identity around rejection of human sacrifice
- [[Screen memory]] — Sodom/Gomorrah as psychic displacement of Judah's destruction, reframed through moral dialogue
- [[Sacrificial cult]] — earlier religious framework where Abraham may have functioned as intermediary receiving petitions
- [[Moral progress]] — the historical trajectory from honor-based genocide to individual accountability
- [[Direct communication with God]] — the covenantal promise making each person "like God" without mediating sacrifice
- [[Juridical thinking]] — reasoning from law and individual culpability rather than group identity or ritual obligation

## Notable Passages

> "This seems to be an origin story of moral progress, from collective to individual punishment, from the honor system or routine genocide in the course of war to the juridical."

> "The story is completely set up to lead us to agree that the cities have it coming and yet we are expected to adopt Abraham's perspective."

> "Post-exilic scribes reworked the traditions they both inherited and repudiated so as to create a national identity predicated on the rejection of human sacrifice and human godhood."

## Questions Raised

1. If Abraham represents an older sacrificial cult intermediary, how does his successful pleading with God actually *dismantle* that role rather than reinforce it? Does the shift to direct communication with God eliminate the need for Abrahamic intercession?

2. How would post-exilic scribes have consciously *known* they were rewriting military narrative into moral philosophy—or is this reconstruction speculative precisely because the ideological work was naturalizing?

3. What distinguishes the "high culture" perspective Abraham models (defending the potentially innocent) from modern [[victimary consciousness]]? Is Abraham proto-victimary or is there a crucial difference?
