## Summary

Bouvard reflects on the originary hypothesis as a transdisciplinary theory fundamentally incompatible with conventional academic frameworks. Rather than seeking institutional legitimacy through explicit metalanguage (which breeds factionalism), he proposes treating GA as a "rumbling underneath"—a cybernetic-inflected praxis that demands clearing away foundational categories. The core insight remains irreducible: humans can point to something and affirm identity only through the originary scene of [[mimetic crisis]], [[deferral]], and iterable gesture. All historical idioms are elaborations of this singular event that must be endlessly redone.

## Key Concepts

- [[Originary hypothesis]]: The founding act of human language and culture emerging from a mimetic crisis resolved through deferral and the sacred object.
- [[Mimetic crisis]]: The moment when rivalrous desire threatens community collapse, requiring the emergence of the sign and the sacred.
- [[Transdisciplinarity]]: A mode of theoretical work that displaces competing frameworks (Marxism, psychoanalysis) by refusing explicit metalanguage that breeds terminological disputes.
- [[Idiom]]: A historical elaboration of the originary scene; more flexible and tacit than "theory" or "discourse."
- [[Deferral]]: The mechanism by which the originary gesture stays or interrupts mimetic violence through the sign.
- [[Center Study]]: The disciplinary practice of textual appraisal grounded in the originary hypothesis.
- [[Negative capability]]: Keatsian receptivity required to approach the originary hypothesis without "irritable questioning."

## Notable Passages

> "You really must clear out all foundational categories to take on the originary hypothesis—you have to make a kind of leap of faith that sets aside 'irritable questioning' to see that there's simply no other way to account for the simple fact that humans can point to something and affirm that it is the same thing."

> "All the idioms we enter as little elaborations under specific historical conditions, of what was done once and for all in such a way that it always needs to be redone on the originary scene."

> "GA is a mode of textual appraisal with some of Eric Gans's (generally unwelcome) counter-resentful politics dropped in on occasion."

## Questions Raised

1. How does treating GA as "cybernetics underneath" rather than an explicit metalanguage protect it from the terminological disputes that plague Marxism and psychoanalysis, yet risk rendering it incommunicable to those outside the praxis?

2. What distinguishes an "idiom" (as tacit historical elaboration) from a "discourse" or "theory"—and does this distinction itself risk becoming the very metalanguage Bouvard seeks to avoid?

3. If the originary scene must be "always redone," does this mean each historical moment requires a new interpretation of the originary hypothesis, or a renewed commitment to the same one?
