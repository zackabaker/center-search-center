## Summary

Moral action sustains the [[center]] by maintaining shared attention and linguistic presence. Drawing on classical thought (Greek, Hebrew, Christian), this essay argues that [[mimesis]] is foundational to both virtue and sin—we are constituted entirely by imitation of models. Rather than denying this troubling fact, moral practices should explicitly transform [[negative mimesis]] (rivalry, status competition) into [[positive mimesis]] (instruction, emulation, shared goods). All moral activity is fundamentally [[pedagogical]], scaling from intimate relationships to governance, though modern autonomy ideology resists this vision.

## Key Concepts

- [[Mimesis]]: The originary human capacity for imitation that structures all action, speech, and desire
- [[Center]]: The focal point of shared attention that moral action must sustain and repair
- [[Negative mimesis]]: Rivalrous imitation over scarce moral goods (admiration, honor, appreciation)
- [[Positive mimesis]]: Constructive imitation through instruction, emulation, and shared practice
- [[Moral practice]]: Activity that consciously transforms negative into positive mimesis through explicit pedagogy
- [[Pedagogy]]: The reciprocal teaching relationship underlying all social bonds and institutions
- [[Desacralized power analysts]]: Critics who resist acknowledging imitation's totality, clinging to autonomous individuality

## Notable Passages

"To act morally is to sustain the center, which means sustaining, repairing and extending the shared attention or linguistic presence that relies on the center."

"If everything is imitation, who am I? What do I ever say or do that I can actually claim is mine, and therefore control and take responsibility for?"

"All moral practices are pedagogical...Pedagogy is reciprocal: the teacher must learn from the learner how to teach."

## Questions Raised

1. How do we distinguish genuine pedagogical reciprocity from manipulative mimetic capture disguised as teaching? Where is the line between healthy modeling and coercive imitation?

2. If modern autonomy ideology actively severs pedagogical bonds between institutions, what social forms might recover explicit mimetic awareness without collapsing back into hierarchical authority?

3. Can [[moral practice]] operating at the level of individual relationships scale effectively to mass societies where direct modeling and reciprocal pedagogy become structurally impossible?
