# Political Syntax II: Generative Declaratives

## Summary

This post develops a typology of political utterances based on how they manage the [[imperative]] crisis at the heart of human communication. The author distinguishes between **naive declaratives** (presenting fulfilled imperatives as their center) and **normative declaratives** (distinguishing acceptable from unacceptable imperatives). Both forms create distance from dangerous imperative enforcement by placing names adjacently, creating a field of semblances. The work maps how utterance becomes intelligibility, and how societies graduate from ritual judgments to fully formalized legal orders.

## Key Concepts

- [[Declarative]]: An utterance that creates order by presenting a fulfilled imperative as its center
- [[Imperative]]: The underlying demand structure that language must manage and defer
- [[Center]]: The sacred reference point around which declaratives organize meaning
- [[Normative sentence]]: A judgment (moral or aesthetic) that distinguishes acceptable from forbidden imperatives
- [[Field of semblances]]: The space of meaningful objects created when imperatives are suspended
- [[Ostensive verification]]: The speaker's standing-in for the availability of a desired object, shared with the listener
- [[Untotalizable imperatives]]: The infinite, conflicting demands that threaten to destabilize order

## Notable Passages

> "The uttered sentence wants to create distance from the imperative arena, or space between any of the imperatives and their (disastrous) enforcement; the intelligible sentence has embedded within it an imperative directing the listener regarding the new mode of appropriation."

> "The normative declarative ensures that the distinction it draws will have to be drawn repeatedly, which means that the imperatives it authorizes will have to return to the declarative which, in an a priori manner, clears the ground of competing imperatives."

> "By placing one name next to another so as to...produce an object...that, in effect, involves the dissolution of the entire imperative arena."

## Questions Raised

1. How does the distinction between "uttered" and "intelligible" sentences map onto the gap between speaker intention and listener interpretation? Does this gap constitute an irreducible site of political contestation?

2. What allows normative declaratives to "graduate" into fully developed legal orders? Is there a threshold where ritual judgment becomes law, and what marks that transition?

3. If normative sentences must continually redraw their distinctions, how do they avoid either collapsing into arbitrary assertion or ossifying into dead ritual?
