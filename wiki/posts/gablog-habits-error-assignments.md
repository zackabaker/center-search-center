## Summary

This post argues that institutional failure originates in the degradation of [[habit]]—the foundational layer beneath formal rules and codes. Rather than analyzing contemporary politics through conventional frameworks, the author redirects attention to how [[habit]] operates through [[imitation]] of unquestionable [[model]]s. When institutions break down, it is because competing sets of habits interfere with one another. The restoration of social order requires recovering stable, shared models that citizens can internalize without critical distance—a counterintuitive suggestion in an age of permanent skepticism.

## Key Concepts

- [[Habit]] — The foundational behavioral patterns that institutions codify and police; degradation occurs through interference between competing habit-sets
- [[Model]] — An exemplary figure whose activity we imitate tacitly and whose rivals become our rivals
- [[Imitation]] — The complicated process of deriving imperatives from another's being-in-activity, requiring suspension of critical judgment
- [[Error]] — Idiosyncratic deviation from shared habits; what appears "bad" only from the standpoint of alternative habit-systems
- [[Institutional decay]] — Failure occurring when habits degenerate, making formal codes unable to maintain order
- [[Resentment]] — The political affect that transcends meaningful analysis; emerges when models are questioned rather than internalized

## Notable Passages

"Habits are 'bad' only from the standpoint of some other set of habits, and norms are nothing more than the winnowing process of distinguishing what is shared in our habits."

"In order to derive imperatives from someone's activity... I must suspend all criticism of that figure—indeed, defending the model against criticism is part of the process of 'internalizing' it."

"If we want to restore habits, we will need to restore unquestionable models, something which may not be as impossible as it sounds."

## Questions Raised

1. Is the restoration of "unquestionable models" compatible with modern pluralism, or does it require a return to pre-modern forms of authority?

2. How do we distinguish between habits worth preserving and those that deserve to degenerate—without invoking the very critical distance the author says we must suspend?

3. Can a society intentionally rebuild its [[habit]]-layer, or does habit restoration require organic emergence rather than deliberate design?
