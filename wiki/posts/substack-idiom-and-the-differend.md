## Summary

This post argues that "idiom" offers a better framework than "theory" or "metalanguage" for center study because it resists the creation of non-convertible meta-levels of analysis. The author traces how [[metalanguage]] operates through [[scenic design]]—particularly in literate discourse and "classic prose"—to compress multiple scenes into a single apparent non-scene. Rather than treating center study as deconstruction (endlessly dismantling metalanguages), the post proposes [[anthropomorphics]] as a practice that infiltrates existing discourses to make them approximate the [[originary sign]], thereby re-enacting the originary event in language use itself.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Idiom]]** — a way of speaking that marks the oscillation between clarity and opacity, making visible what we attend from while enabling others to see what we see
- **[[Metalanguage]]** — a discourse that claims to transcend its object language; center study's challenge is converting metalanguage back into the scenes it describes
- **[[Originary scene]]** — the anthropological founding moment; any trace of an idiom leads back to originary hypothesizing
- **[[Scenic design]]** — the means by which certain objects are brought into view while obscured by the appearance of transparency
- **[[Anthropomorphics]]** — the practice of infiltrating other discourses to make them successor-discourses to the originary sign, rather than reducing them to abstract concepts
- **[[Classic prose]]** — a style that creates the illusion of writer and reader on the same scene pointing to the same object, obscuring its own scenic apparatus
- **[[Mental verbs]]** — linguistic markers (assume, suggest, indicate) that replace mimetic reproduction with judgments about stance

## Notable Passages

> "Idiom marks this oscillation of clarity and opacity, which is always pedagogical, enabling someone to see what you see, while indicating what one is attending from in order to attend to."

> "The originary scene or, even more, the originary hypothesizing on the originary scene, is the entrance into the idiom, which is re-enacted whenever we use language."

> "Anthropomorphics, then, is not so interested in reducing or explaining...any discourse to the abstract concepts developed in the center study factory as in infiltrating other discourses and having them approximate or become successor discourses to the originary sign."

## Questions Raised

1. Can we actually remember the moment when "not understanding" became "understanding," or is forgetting inherent to how learning becomes fluency?
2. How does [[anthropomorphics]] as a practice differ operationally from the deconstruction it critiques—does it avoid the infinite regress of metalanguage dismantling itself?
3. If every utterance re-enacts the originary event, what distinguishes successful idiom-infiltration from mere linguistic variation?
