## Summary

This reflection grapples with the paradox of consciousness and free will from two opposing perspectives. From one angle, consciousness is the most familiar and indubitable empirical fact—we observe it constantly in ourselves and other animals, making it the plainest reality requiring no explanation. From the opposite angle, consciousness and human life are anomalous miracles in an ancient, lifeless universe where such complexity should be vanishingly improbable. The text suggests that dismissing this paradox as a pseudo-problem reflects inadequate scientific methodology rather than genuine philosophical resolution.

## Key Concepts

- [[Consciousness]] — the most immediate and familiar aspect of existence, both undeniable and scientifically inexplicable
- [[Free will]] — the empirical capacity to make unpredictable decisions and change one's mind
- [[Empirical fact]] — observable reality that grounds knowledge claims
- [[Anomaly]] — the miraculous improbability of life and human consciousness in a vast, ancient universe
- [[Human uniqueness]] — awareness of moral categories (right and wrong) that distinguishes humans from other conscious beings
- [[Paradox]] — the irreconcilable tension between consciousness as both obvious and impossible

## Notable Passages

> "Consciousness is simply the nature of my existence... If the sciences can't explain the physical basis of consciousness, then so much the worse for them."

> "Life on earth appears nothing less than miraculous. That, by some completely random process, some mud should get up and start walking around appears highly unlikely, even impossible."

> "There is no mystery of consciousness, nor of freewill" alongside the invocation of Blake's *Tiger*—presenting the paradox without resolving it.
