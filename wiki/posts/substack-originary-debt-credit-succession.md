## Summary

This post develops a "post-economics" theory grounded in Colin Drumm's concepts of [[outside spread]] (lender of last resort) and [[outside option]] (potential successor to the monarch). The author traces how the Stuart crisis internalized both concepts: the central bank displaced the monarch as [[outside spread]], while party rotation simulates the [[outside option]]. The core argument proposes collapsing this distinction through "singularized succession in perpetuity," where public nomination of successors by all authority figures creates a [[distributed dictator]] who determines [[credit]] and counters the central bank's power.

## Key Concepts

- [[Outside spread]]: The lender of last resort who funds government and shapes credit availability
- [[Outside option]]: The potential replacement figure whose existence constrains the ruler's power
- [[Credit]]: Confidence others have in one's reliability; the fundamental unit of cultural and political analysis
- [[Distributed dictator]]: A decentralized authority system where succession is continuously publicized, replacing central banking authority
- [[Pointman]]: The figure whose public succession nominations determine credit allocation across the system
- [[Counter-usurpation]]: The process by which distributed succession authority neutralizes central bank power

## Notable Passages

> "Everyone is trying to increase and expand their own credit (the confidence others—and especially others in whom others have more confidence—have in them) and the only way to do this is by buttressing or undermining the credit of the central banker or outside spread."

> "The more everyone in every position of any authority whatsoever is publicly choosing their successor and even ranking possible successors, and doing so publicly, on a kind of digital bulletin board (on which we would see fluctuations, like stock prices, which these nominations would replace), the more the pointman is determining credit."

## Questions Raised

1. How would a [[distributed dictator]] system actually gather and process the information that Hayek's critique suggests is impossible for centralized decision-making to handle?

2. If nomination fluctuations replace stock prices as signals, what prevents these from becoming another form of speculative abstraction—merely relocating rather than solving the problem of credit manipulation?

3. How does the collapse of [[outside spread]] and [[outside option]] avoid reproducing the totalitarian dangers the author acknowledges while still maintaining the stability that competing centers of power currently provide?
