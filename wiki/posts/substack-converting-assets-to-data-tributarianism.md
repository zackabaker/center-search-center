## Summary

This essay extends Bichler and Nitzan's *Capital as Power* framework to argue that capitalism emerged when 17th-century England authorized banks to create money through national debt, subordinating production to struggles over legal and political conditions. The [[juridical form]] becomes the battlefield where capitalists compete for advantage, making politics itself an extension of property law applied to the center. Two-party electoral systems function as ritualized trials replacing civil war, and the stability of this system depends on regular rotation of power—suggesting that the form matters more than policy substance.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Capital as Power]]** — Capital's value derives not from production but from struggles over the juridical conditions that enable profit extraction
- **[[Juridical Form]]** — The legal regime that makes property determinable and future earnings calculable; the true site of capitalist competition
- **[[Money Creation]]** — When states delegate monetary authority to banks, capital gains structural power over state policy and growth trajectories
- **[[Politics as Trial]]** — Electoral cycles function as bloodless substitutes for civil war, allowing the center to be cyclically prosecuted by competing factions
- **[[Tributarianism]]** — The conversion of assets into data-driven flows extractable through juridical mechanisms rather than direct production
- **[[Mock Trial]]** — Elections serve to stabilize power transfers without revolutionary rupture, channeling resentment into procedural form

## Notable Passages

> "Whatever adds to the wealth of the bank adds to the potential wealth of the state, and what adds to the wealth of the bank is loaning money to enterprises with the highest rate of expected future earnings."

> "Politics is nothing more than the extension of the juridical form to the figure occupying the center. Elections are trials of the incumbents, carried out in place of a civil war."

> "You would think that it's at least conceivable that one party would govern better than the other...but the system's stability depends on rotation regardless of policy."

## Questions Raised

1. If electoral rotation is intrinsically valuable *regardless of policy outcome*, what happens when one party achieves such institutional dominance that voters perceive rotation as impossible—does the system revert to actual civil war dynamics?

2. How does the shift from asset-ownership to data-driven tributarianism change the relationship between the [[juridical form]] and actual property? Can derivatives and algorithmic capital still be "tried" in electoral form?

3. Does the move to "convert assets to data" (the title's promise) represent a further abstraction of capital from production, or does it finally reveal capital's true nature as always having been informational rather than material?
