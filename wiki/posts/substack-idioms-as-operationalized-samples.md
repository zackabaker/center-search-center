## Summary

The traditional Enlightenment model of reasoned public discourse has become obsolete under imperial conditions and digital saturation. Rather than imagining rational debate on a shared scene, we should recognize that most communication now targets algorithmic systems, not human readers. The author proposes using operationalized [[idioms]]—reduced, combinable linguistic units based on Natural Semantic Primes—as tools for teams to directly influence the probability field of discourse. This approach shifts from abstract policy arguments to concrete thought experiments about actual [[sovereignty]] and institutional alignment.

## Key Concepts

- [[Idiom]]: A reduced, combinable linguistic unit designed to operate on algorithmic filtering systems rather than persuade individual readers
- [[Scene]]: The shared space imagined in traditional rhetoric; now defunct at scale
- [[Probability field]]: The space of possible meanings and outcomes that any utterance attempts to shift
- [[Sovereignty]]: Not abstract power but concrete institutional alignment capable of actually implementing desired outcomes
- [[Natural Semantic Primes]]: Weirzbicka's minimal semantic vocabulary used to build operationalized language
- [[Declarative culture]]: Language and thought directed at shaping what is sayable and thinkable
- [[Thought experiment]]: A bounded imaginative exercise that reveals contradictions between wishes and actual institutional capacity

## Notable Passages

> "Any act of signification aims at changing the field of probabilities within which it is situated."

> "We should be taking our audience to be the whole field of algorithms filtering and weighing discourse. When I speak about 'working on language,' that's what I mean."

> "If we were all to accept those conditions, we could skip over the fairly meaningless policy differences and proceed to speaking about the kind of sovereignty that might actually do what it purports to do."

## Questions Raised

1. Can idiom-based language systems genuinely operate at scale on algorithmic networks, or does targeting algorithms require fundamentally different communicative logic than even reduced human language allows?

2. How do we prevent operationalized idioms from becoming merely another form of elite gatekeeping—replacing Lippmann's media managers with teams of "programmers" claiming superior institutional analysis?

3. If the sovereignty thought experiment reveals our actual system cannot deliver on stated goals, what moves us from that recognition toward institutional reorganization rather than cynical acceptance?
