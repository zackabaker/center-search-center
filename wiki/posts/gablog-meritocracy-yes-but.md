## Summary

This essay argues that a truly free meritocratic society inevitably produces asymmetrical group outcomes—not because the system is unfair, but because freedom itself amplifies existing differences. The author contends that once we abandon "blank slate" egalitarianism, we must accept that identical opportunity produces unequal results, which then generates the appearance (and suspicion) of hidden domination. This creates an impossible bind: meritocracies cannot prove they are actually meritocratic, making them vulnerable to victimary accusations regardless of their legitimacy.

## Key Concepts

- [[Meritocracy]]: A system awarding status and resources based on demonstrated ability rather than inherited privilege or group identity
- [[Ascriptive vs. performative criteria]]: The distinction between judging people by fixed characteristics (race, sex) versus actual demonstrated performance
- [[Victimary culture]]: The interpretive framework treating historical and present inequalities as evidence of systemic oppression requiring redress
- [[Post-victimary]]: Emerging thought that moves beyond victimary logic while still acknowledging real asymmetries
- [[Stratification]]: The inevitable hierarchical ordering of groups and individuals in any functioning society
- [[Blank slatism]]: The naive Enlightenment belief that removing legal restrictions alone produces equal outcomes
- [[Institutional control]]: The appearance of domination created when one group is disproportionately represented in powerful institutions

## Notable Passages

> "The more an institution converges towards the highest abstract standard of social and distributive justice, the less it is able to perform its primary function."

> "The very disproportions that must emerge provide prima facie evidence for the belief that the 'so-called' meritocracy in fact veils the domination of society by privileged minorities."

> "It is impossible to prove that a society is really free."

## Questions Raised

1. If freedom necessarily produces stratification, and stratification necessarily invites victimary interpretation, how can post-victimary thought legitimize inequality without returning to pre-modern hierarchies?

2. Is the disproportionate institutional representation the author describes primarily cultural, biological, or driven by network effects—and does it matter if the outcome is identical?

3. What would a "rough" post-victimary society look like that acknowledges group differences while avoiding both blank-slate egalitarianism and victimocratic ressentiment?
