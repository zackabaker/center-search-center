## Summary

This post argues that Western societies have spent seventy years suppressing inquiry into human biodiversity (HBD) differences between groups for understandable but ultimately unsustainable reasons. The author contends that the cognitive costs of this suppression—and the resentments it breeds—now rival or exceed the dangers it was meant to prevent. Rather than continue enforcing silence, mature civilization must find ways to discuss empirical findings about group differences openly while maintaining social coherence and ethical commitment to individual dignity.

## Key Concepts

- [[Human biodiversity (HBD)]] – Factual differences between human groups that may be genetic, biological, cultural, or historical in origin
- [[Victimocracy]] – A social order organized around the suppression of uncomfortable truths to protect designated victim groups
- [[Declarative culture]] – A culture that enforces what can and cannot be said, independent of empirical reality
- [[Resentment]] – The hidden toxin bred by forced civility masking deep disapproval
- [[Center and periphery]] – The tension between shared civic space and the margins where forbidden inquiries occur
- [[Inquiry]] – The foundational human gesture toward truth that precedes any particular conclusion

## Notable Passages

> "We can't exclude the possibility that some differences would be uncovered by future research" — the logical impossibility of proving negatively what empirical inquiry might discover.

> "If inquiries into HBD continue and expand...but prohibitions on public discussions remain in place...the situation we face" — the inevitable pressure cooker of hidden knowledge.

> "For about 70 years we in the West have chosen the first option...but ultimately at great cognitive cost" — the unstated bill for enforced silence.

## Questions Raised

1. Can a genuinely pluralistic society sustain itself while enforcing asymmetrical silences about empirical questions—or does this create the very factionalism it aims to prevent?

2. What is the relationship between the suppression of HBD inquiry and broader patterns of [[declarative culture]] that establish what can be thought versus what can be said?

3. If differences within groups typically exceed differences between them, why does the mere *possibility* of between-group differences carry such civilizational weight?
