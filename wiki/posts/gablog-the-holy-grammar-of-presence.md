## Summary

This piece responds to Eric Gans's reframing of scapegoating and hierarchy through the concept of "firstness" on the originary scene itself. The author argues that post-Auschwitz victimary discourse has inverted the fundamental human paradox—subordinating our transcendent relation to God to an obsession with eliminating all hierarchy. However, victimary thinking has also "implicated" transcendence in oppression, making any return to transcendence-centered thought politically impossible. The solution requires recovering awareness of our collective dependence on the signs that sustain us, grounded in acknowledgment of the [[sceneness]] of the scene itself.

## Key Concepts

- [[Originary scene]]: The foundational human moment where deferral and meaning emerge through collective gesture toward a transcendent sign
- [[Victimary discourse]]: Post-modern ideology that subordinates transcendence to the project of eliminating all social hierarchy
- [[Firstness]]: Gans's concept that hierarchy emerges primitively on the originary scene, prior to institutional embodiment
- [[The human paradox]]: The core tension between mortals reproducing immortal signs; now subordinated to an "ethical paradox" about egalitarian language enforcing hierarchy
- [[Transcendence]]: The transcendent sign's independence from human perception, yet dependence on humans for its subsistence
- [[Sceneness]]: The temporal unfolding of the scene itself—that something happens first, second, third—grounding all iteration and norming
- [[Deferral]]: The originary mechanism of postponing violence through meaningful gesture, necessary regardless of social order

## Notable Passages

> "The advent of victimary discourse in the post-Auschwitz era has, for the first time, subordinated the primary paradox to the secondary one, leading to the widely shared assumption that the elimination of hierarchies between subjects would abolish all conflict."

> "The victimary order has installed itself not only by reversing the priority between transcendence and inequality; it did so by 'implicating' transcendence in inequality—scapegoating representations of transcendence as 'alibis' for the continuance of social hierarchies."

> "If the fundamental human paradox is to be brought back to the center of cultural life it will have to be through an awareness of the way all of us need to contribute to the subsistence of the signs that sustain us."

## Questions Raised

1. How can we articulate transcendence and hierarchy without victimary discourse immediately collapsing these concepts back into oppression?

2. What would it mean materially—in language, ritual, education—to foster collective "awareness of the way all of us need to contribute to the subsistence of the signs that sustain us"?

3. Does grounding hierarchy in [[sceneness]] itself (temporal sequence rather than power) offer genuine protection against the victimary inversion, or does victimary thinking dissolve this distinction?
