## Summary

This essay examines the paradox at the heart of liberal political theory: the "rule of law" claims to replace rule by men with rule by impersonal laws, yet every law requires human enforcement and interpretation. Drawing on Schmitt's definition of sovereignty as the power to decide on the exception, the author argues that liberalism's attempt to make all decisions reviewable and predictable actually generates countless unaccountable micro-decisions and exceptions. The law's original function—replacing vendetta with orderly dispute resolution—becomes corrupted when liberalism tries to abolish disputes entirely rather than merely settle them.

## Key Concepts

- [[Rule of Law]] — The liberal fiction that laws govern without requiring human enforcement or interpretation
- [[Sovereign Decision]] — The power to determine when and how general rules apply to particular cases
- [[Exception]] — A particular instance where a general rule must be suspended to preserve the rule itself
- [[Vendetta]] — The pre-sovereign system of dispute resolution through reciprocal violence
- [[Accountability]] — The liberal aspiration to make every decision subject to review by a higher authority
- [[Absolutism]] — A political order where all law flows from a single will, eliminating the possibility of exceptions
- [[Dispute Resolution]] — The proper, limited function of law in maintaining social peace

## Notable Passages

> "Every law is enforced (or left unenforced) by someone. What the 'rule of law' really means is that power has no final destination."

> "The liberal ideal is to arrange things so that we always know which enforcer of the law in one instance will be subject to another enforcer of the law in another instance."

> "The very thing for which the head of one family would have had to have ordered a member of his clan to wreak vengeance upon the member of another clan must now be something recognized by the law."

## Questions Raised

1. If liberalism's attempt to make all decisions reviewable generates more exceptions and unaccountable micro-decisions, is there a way to restore law's proper limited function without abandoning democratic accountability?

2. How does the distinction between settling disputes and abolishing them map onto the difference between a functional legal system and an increasingly litigious, precautionary one?

3. Can a sovereign order exist without either Schmittean exception-making or the liberal pretense that law can rule without men?
