## Summary

Gans undertakes a grammatical analysis of the [[imperative]] in ethics, politics, and economics, arguing that imperatives range from brute commands to divine proclamation. The key distinction lies between [[acknowledged imperative|acknowledged imperatives]]—which presuppose minimal equality and prior endorsement—and unacknowledged ones that require only post-facto verification. This framework aims to reconcile the imperative order with the concept of [[covenant]], ultimately reframing the polemic between Christian and Judaic revelation as a productive tension for originary thinking.

## Key Concepts

- [[Imperative]] — A command that ranges from brute domination to divine creative utterance; foundational to how action occurs within ostensive frames
- [[Ostensive]] — A sign that presupposes fulfillment; some imperatives require ostensive acknowledgement *before* fulfillment, others only verification after
- [[Acknowledged imperative]] — Commands requiring advance endorsement, presupposing autonomous persons and minimal equality
- [[Unacknowledged imperative]] — Commands requiring only post-facto verification; imply domination or collective emergency response
- [[Covenant]] — At first appearing antipodal to imperative order; represents binding reciprocal acknowledgement
- [[Originary grammar]] — Gans's project to generate grammatical vocabulary mapping how meaning actually emerges in scenes of representation

## Notable Passages

"An imperative requiring acknowledgement presupposes two separate and autonomous persons, whereas one calling only for verification after the fact implies complete domination."

"The acknowledged imperative implies a minimal equality (even, say in the soldier's 'Yes sir!'), while equality is either absent or beside the point in unacknowledged ones."

"God's command to the not yet existing world in Genesis: 'let there be…' (really, just 'Be…!')" — illustrating the imperative's metaphysical reach.
