## Summary

This essay explores how medieval English monarchs managed competing claims to succession through monetary policy, drawing on Colin Drumm's analysis of the "outside option." The king's power depended on balancing austerity (favoring nobility) against currency devaluation (favoring merchants and centralization). The essay traces how England ultimately resolved this tension through the Glorious Revolution and the Bank of England, transforming the problem of succession into market logic and establishing a "lender of last resort" structure that subordinates the crown to empire-scale financial systems.

## Key Concepts

- [[Sovereign Selection|Sovereign Selection]]: The mechanism by which competing legitimate claims to rule are adjudicated through institutional means
- [[Outside Option]]: An alternative recourse that constrains or validates a central actor's power—for kings, rival heirs or foreign currency
- [[Monetary Policy]]: The choice between austerity (metallic backing) and liberality (declarative value) as expressions of centralized vs. distributed power
- [[Center]]: The locus of power that must maintain balance among competing factions to prevent civil war
- [[Dedication]]: The pledge embedded in currency itself—the king's promise that coins will retain value
- [[Market Logic]]: The translation of succession problems into financial instruments and options

## Notable Passages

> "Having such options raises the possibility of civil war if the king fails to maintain some concord or balance between the various social factions. And whether or not he does so depends heavily upon monetary policy."

> "The 'outside option' on sovereignty...would have first of all have been possible but less direct heirs, while the outside option on coinage was international currency."

> "England itself becomes subordinate to empire" once it completes the revolution in monetary policy and establishes itself as the ultimate "outside option" through financial markets.

## Questions Raised

2. If England's imperial power ultimately rests on the Bank of England's role as "lender of last resort," what happens when that institution's credibility is called into question? What is the next outside option?
