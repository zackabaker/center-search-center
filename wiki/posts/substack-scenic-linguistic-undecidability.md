## Summary

The author argues that [[Generative Anthropology]] needs to take linguistic naming far more seriously than Eric Gans's "semantic leniency" permits. Drawing on the originary scene where the Name-of-God was tightly controlled, the text contends that rigorous discourse requires care in how concepts are named—not through pedantic definitions but through awareness of the theoretical or disciplinary events that generated them. The author proposes an "originary grammar of the center" that would map every utterance and event through the [[speech forms]] (ostensive, imperative, interrogative, declarative), potentially rendering traditional terms like "scene" and "event" obsolete.

## Key Concepts

- [[Semantic leniency]] – Gans's resistance to terminological disputes and the assumption that conceptual precision adds little analytical clarity
- [[Speech forms]] – The successive linguistic modes (ostensive, imperative, interrogative, declarative) derived from the originary hypothesis
- [[Originary grammar of the center]] – A specialized project mapping human utterances and orders through the four speech modes
- [[Name-of-God]] – The originary sign whose ritual use was tightly controlled, establishing naming as a sacred social practice
- [[Declarative sentence]] – For Gans, the form that metaphysics privileges; the structure of Hebraic monotheism itself
- [[Mimetic crisis]] – Moments of collective tension resolved through the conferring of names at lowering thresholds of significance

## Notable Passages

> "The concept of 'resentment,' for example, will at some point have to be nailed down if we are to have serious discussions about it."

> "Names probably were all, originally, something like 'God's this,' 'God-like,' 'son of God,' 'beloved by God'—and naming individuals consolidated their position as protected members within a group."

> "If you want your discourse to be a sustainable, ongoing reading of the imperatives of the center you will take care in your naming."

## Questions Raised

1. How would a fully developed [[originary grammar of the center]] actually *replace* the analysis of "scenes" and "events," rather than merely reformulate them? What explanatory gains would accrue?

2. If naming emerged to consolidate membership and ritual obligation at the originary scene, how do we distinguish between names that truly commemorate founding events versus those that merely preserve historical accident or convenience?

3. What would be the practical disciplinary consequences of accepting that every utterance must be analyzable through the four [[speech forms]]—would this constrain or liberate interpretation?
