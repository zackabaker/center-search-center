## Summary

GA's neoabsolutism rejects "supersovereignties"—abstract ideals like justice, rights, and freedom that license resistance to authority. Yet this doesn't eliminate defiance itself; as long as imperatives exist, so does the possibility of refusing them. The challenge becomes: what would genuine resistance look like stripped of these fraudulent rationalizations? The author argues for two forms: pragmatic professional refusal grounded in technical contradiction, and deeper moral questioning that requires participants to think through their actions as members of a larger project rather than abstract rights-bearers.

## Key Concepts

- [[Supersovereignty]]: Fraudulent metalinguistic categories (justice, rights, democracy) superstructured on literacy to rationalize resistance to power
- [[Primearchy]]: A post-absolutist order demanding greater obedience but issuing "better commands" that make compliance reasonable
- [[Imperative gap]]: The space between command and execution where various interpretations and refusals become possible
- [[Imperative]]: The fundamental unit of authority; its issuance always implies possible defiance
- [[Performative resistance]]: Disobedience enacted publicly and strategically, not merely as private refusal
- [[Professional competence]]: The narrow ground on which legitimate technical resistance can stand
- [[Declarative culture]]: A framework treating all participants as obligated thinkers within collective projects, not abstract subjects with "rights"

## Notable Passages

"As long as there are commands, there will be defiance. So, the question is, what would disobedience and defiance look like, how would such practices be thought of, and how would they be enacted without all the supersovereign concepts that now provide a virtual menu of rationalizations?"

"The reluctant resistance I am more interested in could only be done in the name of a practice which has become impossible due to inconsistent imperatives."

"Insofar as everyone is treated as a participant in some larger project, part of a 'team' trying to 'win' some 'game,' everyone is obligated to think through the morality of one's actions."

## Questions Raised

1. If professional/technical resistance requires special competence to justify refusal, what prevents the sovereign from simply redefining competence domains to eliminate spaces of legitimate dissent?

2. Can "thinking through the morality of one's actions" as a team member remain genuinely critical, or does the framework's emphasis on collective participation inevitably subordinate individual moral judgment?

3. How would a neoabsolutist order prevent the re-emergence of supersovereignties as informal rationalizations for resistance, given that humans seem structurally prone to generating such abstractions?
