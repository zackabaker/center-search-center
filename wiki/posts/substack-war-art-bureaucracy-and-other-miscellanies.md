# War, Art, Bureaucracy and other Miscellanies

## Summary

This essay proposes that human social reality can be comprehended through three fundamental categories: the [[ritual]], the [[juridical]], and the [[disciplinary]]. These emerge from and remain anchored to the [[originary scene]], where ritual first defers violence and sustains community. The author argues that all major human institutions—including war, art, and bureaucracy—can be mapped onto these three categories, which operate at different spatial and temporal scales. By tracing how the juridical and disciplinary infiltrate and gradually conquer territory from ritual governance, the essay establishes a unified framework for understanding institutional history and "admissible language use."

## Key Concepts

- **[[Ritual]]**: The originary human scene that defers violence through centering and maintains community presence.
- **[[Juridical]]**: Proto-legal concepts and practices addressing conflicts at scales beyond ritual's capacity; presupposes accountability and fairness.
- **[[Disciplinary]]**: Truth-seeking and standardizing practices that establish preliminary order; all languages contain equivalents of "true."
- **[[Originary scene]]**: The foundational human scene where deferral of violence and language emerge together.
- **[[Honor culture]]**: Pre-state kinship-based social organization where the clan head bears personal responsibility for all members' actions.
- **[[Big Men]]**: Ritual and distributory center-controllers in honor cultures who share power intermittently with peers.
- **[[Admissible language use]]**: The question of when claims about truth, fairness, justice, and interest can legitimately be made.

## Notable Passages

> "Just as the ritual scene defers violence and thereby maintains community by summoning the presence at the center, the emergence of concepts covering 'right speaking' must evoke some center in the interest of sustaining institutions of deferral."

> "The lines of command, through the family and concerning women and children, are sharply defined and defended under the principle that the head of the clan is solely and personally responsible both for whatever happens to any member of the clan but for what every member of the clan does."

> "Honor culture also verges upon the juridical, insofar as injuries done by one tribe[...presuppose accountability]."

## Questions Raised

1. How do war, art, and bureaucracy each distribute across the ritual-juridical-disciplinary spectrum, and what determines institutional migration between these categories?

2. Can honor culture's logic of collective responsibility and indirect causation be reconciled with modern juridical individualism, or are they fundamentally incommensurable?

3. If all human institutions must derive legitimacy by evoking some center, what happens when modern scales of organization (global markets, digital networks) make centering impossible?
