## Summary

This essay distinguishes between high and popular culture through the lens of audience identification: high culture asks us to identify with the victim, while popular culture aligns us with the lynch mob. The author argues that popular texts permit free iteration of elements without regard to original context, whereas high culture demands preservation of textual integrity and historical labor. Contemporary culture's dominance of the popular represents the ascendancy of the [[victimary]] and a violent appropriation of high cultural texts themselves, collapsing the traditional boundary between registers in ways that postmodern theory inadequately captures.

## Key Concepts

- **[[High culture vs popular culture]]** — Distinction based on whether audiences identify with victim or lynch mob
- **[[Victimary]]** — The political-cultural force that treats high cultural texts as oppressive and worthy of "violent" appropriation
- **[[Appropriation]]** — The mode of consuming cultural materials; differs radically between registers
- **[[Textual integrity]]** — The high cultural demand to preserve and examine artifacts holistically and historically
- **[[Mechanical reproduction]]** — Mass production that enabled popular culture's rise and the collapse of aesthetic hierarchy
- **[[Citationality]]** — The cannibalistic iteration of cultural phrases and elements outside original context
- **[[Interpretive faculty]]** — The critical depth and perspectival capacity that high culture traditionally cultivated

## Notable Passages

> "In popular culture, the audience identifies with the lynch mob, while in high culture they identify with the victim."

> "We have no compunction about repeating catch phrases from a sitcom or movie in ways that show no respect at all to the way that phrase functioned in its 'original' context."

> "The high cultural texts are themselves viewed as oppressors, and by 'appropriating' them 'violently' we take our justified revenge upon them for their presumption of centrality."

## Questions Raised

1. If popular cultural engagement already develops genuine interpretive and moral faculties, what distinct value can high culture pedagogy offer—or does the question itself reveal the loss of a necessary [[center]]?

2. Is the "violent appropriation" of high cultural texts by the victimary a genuine act of meaning-making, or does it represent the final evacuation of interpretive depth?

3. What institutional or attentional structures would be required to recover the boundary between high and popular culture, and is such recovery possible or desirable in a genuinely democratic order?
