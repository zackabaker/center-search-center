## Summary

This paper reconceptualizes post-colonialism through Girardian and Gansian mimetic theory, arguing that the post-colonial condition emerges when [[mimesis]] replaces violence as the primary social force. Drawing on Eric Gans's revision of René Girard's originary scene, the author shows how [[sacrifice]] and scapegoating arise later, after the emergence of the "Big Man" who centralizes both resources and resentment. Modernity inherits a fundamentally [[victimary]] understanding of violence that Christianity both exposes and intensifies. The post-colonial represents a state where we recognize asymmetries without believing in their apocalyptic resolution—a genuinely post-sacrificial condition.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Originary scene]]**: The founding moment of human community through a deferred sign rather than violent resolution
- **[[Mimetic crisis]]**: Contagious desire and rivalry that threatens group cohesion
- **[[Scapegoating]]**: The arbitrary singling out of a community member whose expulsion restores order
- **[[Big Man]]**: The centralizing figure who both defers and intensifies violence by monopolizing resentment
- **[[Victimary]]**: The modern ethical framework understanding all violence as implicating universal guilt
- **[[Post-sacrificial]]**: A semiotic condition where asymmetries persist without sacrificial resolution
- **[[Logocentrism]]**: Western metaphysical privileging of presence and rational speech over writing's différance

## Notable Passages

> "The originary sign which founds the community must be a deferral of violence, even on Girard's terms, since the lynching itself would only resolve the crisis through some shared sign that could in that case have come in place of the violence."

> "Once the origin of goods is centralized, in other words, so can be the origin of contagion. The Big Man both defers violence by deflecting resentments within the community and becomes a pole of mimetic attraction and hence a more focused violence."

> "If we are post-colonial, then, it is because we recognize and resist asymmetries without claiming we can register or transform them in any systemic way."

## Questions Raised

1. How does the post-sacrificial condition differ functionally from pre-sacrificial (purely mimetic) communities—or are they structurally identical despite different historical consciousness?

2. If post-colonial theory makes victimary ambivalence "constitutive," does this mean the post-colonial can never achieve genuine semiotic agency, remaining perpetually trapped in resentment?

3. Can [[logocentrism]] and writing's différance be understood as post-sacrificial technologies, or do they actually intensify the victimary structure by creating new asymmetries of representation?
