## Summary

This brief clarification distinguishes the [[originary]] proper from all subsequent cultural developments. While all culture theoretically traces to the [[originary scene]], the term meaningfully refers only to what is actually *present* at that founding moment: the [[sacred]], the [[sign]], the [[aesthetic]], and fundamental [[cultural universals]]. Literature, tragedy, and other specialized forms evolved later and are therefore derivative, not originary, regardless of their cultural importance. Only what is universal across all human cultures everywhere can claim originary status.

## Key Concepts

- [[Originary scene]]: The founding anthropological moment from which all culture derives
- [[Sacred]]: Fundamental category present at the originary scene
- [[Sign]]: The representational capacity established at origin
- [[Aesthetic]]: Originary dimension of human culture
- [[Cultural universals]]: Categories present in all human cultures; the markers of originary status
- [[Narrative]]: Implicitly originary; grounded in the originary scene
- [[Literature]]: Derivative form that evolved after origin; not universal to all cultures
- [[Sacrifice]]: Originary practice, distinct from tragedy (its later literary form)

## Notable Passages

> "The originary is actually present at the originary scene, and includes the sacred, the sign, the aesthetic, and so on."

> "What is not universal to all cultures everywhere cannot be originary."

> "There have been many cultures without literature. (Myth, of course, is not literature.)"
