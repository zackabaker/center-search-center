## Summary

This post traces how [[sacral kingship]] weakens as the king distances himself from the community through layers of mediation—elites who buffer him from direct accountability. The author argues that removing a sovereign requires sacral, not rational, grounds: no purely functional argument can justify the disruption of replacing one ruler with another. Modern revolutions bypass this by either pretending the new ruler was always-already sovereign or by replacing the figure with a procedure, yet this formalism masks the persistence of sacrality at the center itself.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Sacral kingship]]** — The king's sacred function as mediator between community and divine, vulnerable to being scapegoated for collective misfortune
- **[[Mediation]]** — Layers of elites interposed between sovereign and subjects, rationalizing but also insulating the center
- **[[Center]]** — The position of sovereignty whose occupant is confirmed by resentment directed toward it
- **[[Scapegoating]]** — The mechanism by which intra-group rivalries are deferred by targeting a central figure
- **[[Resentment]]** — The emotional substance that actually confirms sovereignty; overthrowing the center requires equal and opposite sacrality
- **[[Sovereignty]]** — Authority validated not by rational grounds but by occupying the center itself

## Notable Passages

"If the king is no longer available as a scapegoat, he can no longer be held directly responsible for mediating the community's relation to the divine and the divinely controlled universe."

"It would be impossible to make a case for removing the king on purely rational grounds... you could never show that the superior management skills of prospective king B outweighs in importance the disruptive effects of removing actual king A."

"The final residue of sacral kingship... is simply that someone must be at the center, and actually being at the center is a kind of a priori proof that it is you that should be there."

## Questions Raised

1. If overthrowing a center requires "equal and opposite sacrality," how would contemporary movements recognize or mobilize such a force without reverting to pre-modern religious idioms?

2. Does the author's claim that genuine revolutions have "never actually happened" hold even for originary scenes, or does GA's founding gesture escape this formalism?

3. If being at the center is self-validating proof of rightness, what distinguishes legitimate from illegitimate sovereignty except the effectiveness of the resentment apparatus itself?
