## Summary

This essay traces writing's origin to the simultaneous emergence of property accounting, currency, and debt—beginning with ledger entries like "3 oxen." The author argues that writing as [[token]] creation retains intrinsic connections to ownership and obligation across all domains, from commercial notation to sacred texts. Both economic tokens and recorded speech acts circulate through systems of [[dissemination]] that obligate receivers to future performance. The essay suggests that reading a text, like redeeming a debt note, issues a new token that binds the reader to live out those words in future situations.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Token]]** — A mark or inscription that designates property, enacts exchange, and creates debt obligation
- **[[Originary writing]]** — The first inscriptions combining subject-predicate structure (e.g., "3 oxen") that establish ownership and transfer
- **[[Declarative culture]]** — The recording and circulation of speech acts that bind communities through repeated utterance and pedagogical obligation
- **[[Dissemination]]** — The circulation of both economic tokens and texts through networks that create new claims and obligations
- **[[Autoprobatory event]]** — An event that proves itself true through its representation (Gans's concept)
- **[[Debt]]** — The fundamental obligation incurred when receiving a token, whether economic or textual

## Notable Passages

> "To read these texts, that is, to repeat out loud what others have said out loud, is to issue a token and take out a debt—one is obliged to be ready to live out those words in other situations."

> "The first writing is the first designation of property and the first token of exchange and issuance of debt. Implications must follow from this."

> "The note can further be circulated along with other similar notes as a form of currency, without anyone ever necessarily going to collect the 3 oxen."

## Questions Raised

1. If all writing creates [[debt]], what distinguishes the obligation incurred by reading scripture from that incurred by reading a contract—or are they fundamentally identical?

2. How does the [[dissemination]] of a text through interpretive communities transform or preserve the original "autoprobatory" claim embedded in its first utterance?

3. Can the token logic of writing extend to digital communication and blockchain currencies, or do new media require fundamentally different thinking about inscription and obligation?
