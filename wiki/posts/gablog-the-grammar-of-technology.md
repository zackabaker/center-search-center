# The Grammar of Technology

## Summary

This post develops a theory of technology through the lens of [[originary anthropology|originary]] grammar, specifically via the concept of [[imperative exchange]]. The author argues that language itself—particularly the evolution from [[ostensive]] utterance to imperative command—provides the fundamental structure for understanding contemporary technology and power. By tracing how the [[center]] emerges as a locus of intentionality that can be addressed and obeyed, the text sketches an "absolutist ontology" grounded in how humans already speak about communications and information technology.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Ostensive]]**: The most basic layer of language, based on gesture and naming objects assumed to be present; still foundational to human culture
- **[[Imperative exchange]]**: The fundamental dynamic emerging when ostensive reference fails and one party must request (or be commanded) to retrieve an absent object
- **[[Center]]**: On the originary scene, the object of collective desire whose intentionality is attributed by the group but cannot initially speak for itself
- **[[Declarative culture]]**: The implicit counterpart to ostensive/imperative, where linguistic presence itself becomes the organizing principle
- **[[Human temporality]]**: The distinctly human experience of time created by the gap between sign and its completion in imperative exchange
- **[[Absolute ontology]]**: A coherent account of being constructed from the grammar of how people already speak about technology and power

## Notable Passages

> "My goal is to develop a way of thinking that would really be a way of speaking and writing that would dismantle and reassemble the utterances in which it participates."

> "The center doesn't 'speak,' but for the members of the group the center is repelling their desire—that is, a kind of intentionality is attributed to the center, and registered in the sign, but this intentionality cannot be given 'voice.'"

> "It's impossible to imagine the members of the group making requests without framing that request in terms of their response to a command, since doing so would be tantamount to placing themselves outside of the sign and community."

## Questions Raised

1. How does the imperative exchange model extend to distributed, algorithmic systems where the "center" is fragmented or invisible?

2. What would it look like to apply this originary grammar to understand digital platforms as organizing reward structures through imperative compliance rather than transparent command?

3. If all interactions with power structures recapitulate imperative exchange, what linguistic or institutional forms would resistance or genuine freedom require?
