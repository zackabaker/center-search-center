## Summary

This essay argues that the "digital humanities" allow us to recognize [[originarity|the original digitality]] of human culture: the sacred/profane binary as humanity's foundational 1/0 distinction. Drawing on Eric Gans, the author contends that digital abstraction exposes what conceptual art has always exemplified—that [[attention|attention]] to a work matters more than its content (the "MacGuffin"). Civilization itself rests on replacing honor/shame binaries with civility/guilt ones, a recoding completed during the Renaissance. The essay suggests that digital culture's current appeal lies partly in exhaustion with [[victimary|victimary discourse]], opening possibilities for post-victimary cultural politics.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Originarity]]** – the founding scene of human culture, where the sacred/profane distinction emerges as the first binary
- **[[Digitality]]** – the 1/0 structure underlying all cultural meaning-making
- **[[Sacred/Profane]]** – the original binary distinction that generates civilization and deferral
- **[[Victimary]]** – contemporary discourse centering victim status and grievance; increasingly exhausted in academia
- **[[Civilization]]** – founded on recoding material obligations (debt, violation, penalty) into abstract, polysemous terms
- **[[Honor/Shame]]** – the barbaric binary organized around reputation for vengeance and power
- **[[Civility/Guilt]]** – the civilizational binary organized around rule-following and internalized conscience

## Notable Passages

> "The digital humanities draw out what is essential to the project of abstract or conceptual art, which is that iconic, analog content is ultimately secondary to simply positing the work or text as an object worthy of attention."

> "Civilization, like any cultural form, is founded on a binary: civilized vs. uncivilized…which first of all distinguishes itself from barbarism—the honor/gift/shame culture."

> "Renaissance thinkers were both sharply aware of the cost and difficulty of constructing civilization out of barbarism…and distant enough from the culture that had been overcome to study it as a kind of originary model."

## Questions Raised

1. If the digital humanities exemplify the exhaustion of victimary discourse, what affirmative cultural and political forms emerge once victimary thinking is merely *avoided* rather than directly confronted?

2. The author suggests Renaissance greatness partly derived from acute awareness of civilization's cost—does post-victimary culture risk losing that critical consciousness, and if so, what replaces it?

3. How does the equation of digitality with the sacred/profane binary help us understand contemporary attention economies and the "MacGuffin" logic of digital content?
