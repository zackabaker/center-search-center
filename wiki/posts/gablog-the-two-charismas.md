## Summary

This essay distinguishes two forms of [[charisma]]: the classical Weberian sense—divine grace manifested through self-discipline that inspires others to transcendence—and the modern, post-Weberian sense of transgression against established order. Using a parable of ten people and food, the author argues that while both forms require discipline, they differ qualitatively in their orientation. The transgressive charismatic destabilizes norms by reversing scales of value, often empowering resentment; the disciplined charismatic models restraint. The essay probes how these modes blur in practice and suggests that the distinction between them is not quantitative but rooted in fundamentally different attitudes toward [[order]] itself.

## Key Concepts

- [[Charisma]] — perceived divine grace or transgressive power that restructures social norms through force of example or reversal of values
- [[Discipline]] — the restraint of compulsive desires; manifested differently depending on whether it serves tradition or transgression
- [[Transgression]] — the modern form of charisma; breaking established norms by empowering resentment and reversing hierarchies of value
- [[Resentment]] — the emotional fuel of transgressive charisma; the instigator's ability to resent the power of the disciplined
- [[Order]] — the established social structure that either contains or generates the seeds of its own transformation
- [[Mimetic desire]] — the underlying dynamic by which the nine are drawn to either the tenth's restraint or the instigator's reversal

## Notable Passages

> "Charisma, in its original sense, according to Philip Rieff: divine grace perceived in a person who has transcended desires that are compulsive to others."

> "The transgressive instigator must have been at least slightly more disciplined than his brethren—otherwise there is no way he could have extricated his mind...to resent the power the latter now deployed."

> "The difference we are looking for must be qualitative" (not merely a matter of quantity of discipline).

## Questions Raised

1. Can a single historical actor or movement embody both forms of charisma simultaneously, or does commitment to one necessarily exclude the other?

2. If the transgressive charismatic must possess qualitative discipline to perceive and resent the established order, what prevents that same discipline from becoming traditionalizing over time?

3. How do we evaluate which form of charisma serves human flourishing when both require real ascesis and both can inspire genuine devotion?
