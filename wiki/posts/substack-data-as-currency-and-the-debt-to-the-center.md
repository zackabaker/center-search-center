## Summary

This essay argues that humanity is constitutively indebted to the [[center]] from the originary event onward, an asymmetrical obligation that can never be fully repaid but only perpetually serviced. The author contends that [[originary indebtedness]] explains phenomena like guilt, devotion, and compulsion better than liberal egalitarian fantasies, which attempt to airbrush the center from view. The debt accumulates as the interval between issuance and discharge lengthens, and becomes increasingly unbearable in late modernity—yet without acknowledgment of this foundational obligation, heroism, ambition, and genuine generosity become unintelligible.

## Key Concepts

- [[Originary indebtedness]] – The foundational human obligation to the center that constitutes our humanity and can never be fully discharged
- [[Asymmetrical exchange]] – The center gives first; the periphery responds second—a structural inequality underlying all human relations
- [[The center]] – The sacred locus that organizes human meaning and to which all beings remain perpetually obligated
- [[Egalitarian fantasy]] – The modern illusion of symmetrical, instantaneous mutual exchange that obscures originary hierarchy
- [[The interval]] – The temporal gap between debt issuance and repayment, whose prolongation increases both debt and interest
- [[Imperative]] – The genuine compulsion (not mere choice) that drives human obligation and meaning-making activity

## Notable Passages

> "The originary debt can never be paid back, but we can also never leave off paying our regular installments—a genuine compulsion, which is to say, imperative, is in place here."

> "To sustain the fantasy, all that needs to be done is to airbrush out of sight the center...This is a fantasy of instantaneous, mutual, symmetrical, exchange."

> "From the standpoint of the egalitarian fantasy, originary indebtedness is bleak indeed, but how would any concept of heroism, greatness, ambition or...generosity make sense without us experiencing being held by some obligation from which we can never be free?"

## Questions Raised

1. If originary indebtedness is structural and universal, how do we distinguish between its authentic forms and the "pettier versions" the author mentions? What criteria determine when debt becomes properly formalized versus when it remains obscured?

2. Can modern institutions genuinely formalize originary debt, or does formalization necessarily domesticate and diminish it? What would an honest political economy of originary indebtedness actually look like?

3. How does the author's position differ from Girard's on human sacrifice if both acknowledge an escalating debt dynamic—is the difference purely chronological (animals before humans) or does it run deeper into the logic of obligation itself?
