## Summary

This essay argues that "anthropomorphics" names the ongoing dialectical process by which humans reciprocally confer humanity upon one another through interaction, rather than expressing a fixed human nature. Drawing on [[originary hypothesis|the originary hypothesis]], the author traces how [[mimesis]], [[signification]], and [[ritual]] ground human universals that manifest only through historical forms. The essay then tracks how the emergence of the [[Big Man]]—who breaks egalitarian reciprocity through accumulated power and imagination—generates [[resentment]] and establishes a new center of communal order, creating permanent tension between those who seek to displace him and those who wish to restore primitive equality.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Originary hypothesis]]**: The theory that human nature originates in a single founding event of [[signification]] that defers mimetic crisis through the sacred sign
- **[[Mimesis]]**: The fundamental human capacity for imitation that generates both rivalry and the possibility of crisis
- **[[Signification]]**: The deferral of violence through the establishment of a sacred sign that stabilizes the community
- **[[Anthropomorphization]]**: The reciprocal endowment of humanity through accumulated interactions and retrojected intentions
- **[[Big Man]]**: The figure who breaks egalitarian reciprocity by accumulating goods, power, and indebtedness beyond reciprocal gift relations
- **[[Resentment]]**: The distinctive emotion generated when the Big Man sets terms of communal interaction rather than merely possessing desired objects
- **[[Center]]**: Originally the shared object of desire; relocated to the Big Man who occupies the position of ritual and ancestral authority
- **[[Producer's desire]]**: The imagination and discipline to prevail over anticipated reception and accumulate power

## Questions Raised

1. How does the perpetual tension between displacing the Big Man and restoring egalitarianism manifest in modern political movements—and what determines whether reactionary or progressive impulses prevail?

2. If anthropomorphization is reciprocal and ongoing, can we identify historical moments where this process breaks down or reverses, dehumanizing rather than humanizing participants?

3. What distinguishes legitimate [[center|centers]] of authority from tyrannical Big Men who suppress rather than merely discipline potential rivals?
