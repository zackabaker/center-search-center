## Summary

This essay examines the poverty of contemporary immigration debate, noting that neither anti-immigration nor pro-immigration arguments rest on serious political theory or historical precedent. The author constructs a missing pro-immigration case grounded in economic benefit and intergenerational reciprocity, while acknowledging that the actual pro-immigration position relies on emotional blackmail and demonization of skeptics. The core tension emerges: keeping faith with ancestors who immigrated conflicts with keeping faith with descendants who inherit the consequences of current immigration policy.

## Key Concepts

- [[Immigration]] — demographic movement as political problem requiring coherent theoretical framework, not platitude
- [[Declarative culture]] — the way societies make binding claims about membership and belonging
- [[Victimary progressivism]] — moral positioning that uses emotional blackmail rather than argument
- [[Sovereignty]] — the right to determine membership and cultural boundaries
- [[Economic conditions]] — the material circumstances that determine whether immigration benefits or harms native populations
- [[Cultural reciprocity]] — the obligation to extend to future immigrants the faith predecessors extended to one's own ancestors

## Notable Passages

> "The pro-immigration position seems to rely completely on two things: first, a continual demonization of those skeptical of or opposed to immigration; second, a weird kind of emotional blackmail."

> "Insofar as keeping that faith comes into conflict with keeping faith with your own descendants, and leaving them a country at least as good as the one left to you, the latter faith must prevail."

> "The pathetic state of the ongoing debate over immigration" stems from historical forgetting—we don't invoke 1880s or 1905 arguments because we've lost the archive of serious political thinking on the question.
