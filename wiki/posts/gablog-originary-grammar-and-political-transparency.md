## Summary

This post develops a theory of how the three primary [[linguistic forms]]—ostensive, imperative, and declarative—correspond to distinct modes of political accountability rather than superseding one another in strict hierarchy. The author argues that higher linguistic forms *articulate* lower ones in new configurations, and proposes the concept of [[originary nihilism]] to explain how colliding or undermining imperatives create conditions for the emergence of declarative (liberal democratic) discourse as a deferral of impossible command.

## Key Concepts

- [[Ostensive]]: The linguistic form tied to unquestioned fealty to a shared sacred center; requires verification through pointing/reference.
- [[Imperative]]: The form of command and compliance; generates cascading sub-ostensives that complicate the original order.
- [[Declarative]]: The open, liberal form in which legitimacy requires all parties to have voice before decision.
- [[Originary nihilism]]: A condition where colliding imperatives erase the verifiable "object" needed to confirm compliance.
- [[Political accountability]]: The varying modes of justification and verification required in different linguistic/political regimes.
- [[Articulation]]: The process by which higher forms reorganize rather than eliminate lower ones in spatial-temporal configurations.

## Notable Passages

> "The higher linguistic forms do not supersede the lower ones but, rather, articulate them in new spatial and temporal configurations."

> "The declarative emerges as the deferral of the impossible imperative and the transcendence of originary nihilism."

> "The first 'claim' made by the declarative is that things can be 'otherwise' than proposed by the impossible imperative."
