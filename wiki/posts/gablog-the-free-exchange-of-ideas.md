## Summary

This post critiques a University of Wisconsin-Madison provost's defense of allowing a lecturer to teach 9/11 conspiracy theories as part of "free exchange of ideas." Gans argues that postmodern skepticism about truth is selectively applied: conspiracy theories reinforcing victimary grievances (antisemitism, anti-Americanism) are protected as "unpopular ideas," while other forms of absurdity would be rejected. The double standard reveals that institutional claims to neutrality mask ideological commitments, and that [[White Guilt]] cannot sustain a coherent philosophy.

## Key Concepts

- [[Victimary thought]]: A framework that privileges claims framed through victim status, granting protection to certain "ideas" based on their alignment with marginalized narratives
- [[Postmodern skepticism]]: Blanket doubt about objective truth, selectively deployed to protect some claims while dismissing others
- [[Free speech]]: Institutional rhetoric masking selective enforcement based on ideological alignment
- [[White Guilt]]: The incoherent philosophical position that undermines rational judgment in favor of self-abnegation
- [[Declarative culture]]: The power to define what counts as an acceptable "idea" or legitimate discourse
- [[Truth and relativism]]: The contradiction between claiming all ideas deserve equal consideration and actually protecting only those serving particular interests

## Notable Passages

> "Thus the most preposterous idiocy is considered an 'unpopular idea' if it comports with victimary thought."

> "An absurdity becomes an 'idea' only under such conditions; we cannot trust our 'objective' judgment in such cases because we might tend to favor ourselves."

> "We cannot allow political pressure from critics of unpopular ideas to inhibit the free exchange of ideas"—yet this principle applies selectively.
