## Summary

This piece introduces a method of "originary political thinking" by analyzing Gertrude Stein's 1946 reflections on the atomic bomb. Stein argues that weapons of total destruction cannot be genuinely "interesting" because interest requires something to remain—someone to notice change. The author develops this into a philosophical inquiry about what constitutes meaningful change in human life and how [[representation]] might account for existential threats that exceed our calculable interest. The essay proposes that political thinking must operate within recognizable parameters of the living, not abstractions of annihilation.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Deferral of sacrifice]]** — A political stance predicated on avoiding or postponing violent resolution; the basis of originary political thinking
- **[[Interest]]** — The philosophical category that requires both threshold conditions: something must remain, and something must meaningfully change within recognizable bounds
- **[[Representation]]** — The narrative and strategic frameworks (like detective stories) through which humans make sense of unpredictable human variety
- **[[Originary thinking]]** — Political philosophy that defers sacrifice and works within human parameters rather than apocalyptic abstractions
- **[[The living]]** — What remains after any destruction; the actual domain of human interest and concern
- **[[Mimetic crisis]]** — Implicit in discussions of total destruction; the collapse of recognizable difference necessary for interest

## Notable Passages

> "If there is nothing there nobody to be interested and nothing to be interested about... there are always lots left on this earth to be interested or to be willing and the thing that destroys is just one of the things that concerns the people inventing it."

> "Everybody gets so much information all day long that they lose their common sense. They listen so much that they forget to be natural."

> "Interest would seem to attach to events or phenomena that are, on the one hand, below the threshold above which 'interest' would be impossible because nothing recognizable would remain."

## Questions Raised

1. Can [[representation]] genuinely account for the possibility of total destruction without ceasing to be interested in the living—or does that possibility necessarily transform what "interest" means?

2. How does the distinction between "secret weapons" (dull) and revealed ones affect the political economy of deterrence and [[sovereignty]]?

3. What would an originary political thinking look like that incorporates the atomic bomb as a permanent feature of the calculable future without losing grip on what remains to be interested in?
