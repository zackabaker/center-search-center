## Summary

The center—the originary locus of shared human attention—is the fundamental basis for language and morality. Sustaining the center through [[linguistic presence]] is humanity's core ethical imperative, requiring recuperation rather than denunciation of distracted or competing claims to centrality. The text traces how breakdowns in [[linguistic presence]] generate transitions between [[ostensive]], [[imperative]], and [[declarative]] speech forms, each restoring shared reality when threatened by violence. Understanding this semiotic progression reveals how language itself is an engineering of human cooperation around attention.

## Key Concepts

- [[Center]] — The originary shared locus of human attention and the origin of language itself
- [[Linguistic presence]] — The maintenance of a common, linguistically constituted reality across a community
- [[Ostensive]] — The first speech act, pointing to present objects to establish shared reference
- [[Imperative]] — The speech act emerging when ostensive pointing fails, magically creating reality through command
- [[Declarative]] — The highest speech form, restoring presence through negative reference and inference about an external world
- [[Attention]] — The fundamental moral resource; evil originates in distraction from the center
- [[Semiotic engineering]] — The deliberate structuring of language and speech acts to maintain social coherence

## Notable Passages

> "If there's one thing we can say human beings 'should do,' i.e., an absolute moral imperative, it would be 'sustain the center.'"

> "You sustain the center by recuperating, not denouncing, distractions—if they are distractions, they are distractions from a particular center, and can therefore be treated as iterations of that centrality in a particular way."

> "Maintaining linguistic presence is the urgent imperative that takes us through the succession of speech acts, from the ostensive, through the imperative and to the declarative."

## Questions Raised

1. How do we adjudicate between competing legitimate claims to be sustaining "the" center, especially when historical traditions have transmitted different modes of attention over centuries?

2. If evil requires concentrated focus on a particular center (albeit the wrong one), what distinguishes morally between the attention structures of good and evil projects?

3. Can the semiotic progression from ostensive → imperative → declarative be reversed or interrupted, and what would linguistic or political collapse look like in these terms?
