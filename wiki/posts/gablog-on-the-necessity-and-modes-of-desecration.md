## Summary

This essay examines a controversy around conceptual artists Vanessa Place and Kenneth Goldsmith, whose works provoked accusations of racism from activist groups. Place's project of tweeting *Gone with the Wind* and Goldsmith's public reading of Michael Brown's autopsy report triggered campaigns against them by those claiming to defend people of color. The author argues these attacks reveal a structural double bind: artists are simultaneously accused of ignoring POC suffering and of appropriating their cultural forms—a trap designed to keep critics perpetually ahead morally. Rather than paralysis, the essay suggests artists might embrace their role as scapegoats, turning accusations into productive [[desecration]].

## Key Concepts

- [[White Guilt]] — the liberal artist's performative self-flagellation that makes them vulnerable to accusation
- [[Victimary progressivism]] — moral positioning that weaponizes victimhood to establish hierarchy and exploit double binds
- [[Declarative Culture]] — the establishment of ideological positions through declaration rather than reasoning
- [[Transgression]] — the Duchampian tradition of confusing boundaries between art and non-art
- [[Scapegoat]] — the role the artist accepts by volunteering for communal cleansing
- [[Double Bind]] — the structural trap where no position escapes accusation
- [[Desecration]] — the deliberate violation of sacred boundaries as artistic or political act

## Notable Passages

"One the one hand, innovative literature is formalistic play that ignores and, by implication, is complicit with, the oppression of POC; and, on the other hand, innovative aesthetic devices were invented by POC and stolen (and tamed) by white people."

"If you volunteer yourself as a scapegoat so as to cleanse the community, you shouldn't be surprised if others take you up on it."

"The kind of conceptual art Place and Goldsmith does is very much in the tradition of Duchamp's 'Fountain,' aimed at transgressing and confusing the boundary between what is art and what is not art."

## Questions Raised

1. Can an artist productively embrace the scapegoat role without being destroyed by it, or does acceptance of victimization merely legitimize the accusers' moral claim?

2. How does the [[double bind]] structure of victimary discourse differ from earlier forms of ideological entrapment, and what makes it specifically modern?

3. Is desecration still possible as transgression when the boundary between sacred and profane has already collapsed into [[declarative culture]]?
