## Summary

This post traces how the [[declarative]] mode evolved across oral, manuscript, and literate cultures as a way of deferring and supplementing the [[imperative]]. In oral cultures, declaratives stay close to [[ritual]] and serve mythology; in manuscript cultures, the gap between written scripture and spoken language generates [[transfer-translations]] that preserve continuity; in literate cultures modeled on classical prose, the declarative threatens to abolish the imperative altogether. The core problem is how to use declaratives without neutralizing the [[ostensive-imperative]] world that grounds all human meaning-making.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Declarative]]**: The grammatical mode that presents the existence of an object as its presence; invented to defer the imperative.
- **[[Ostensive-imperative]]**: The originary dyadic structure where designation and command are inseparable from the scene of the center.
- **[[Ritual]]**: An asymmetrical exchange with the [[center]] where participants fulfill commands while making requests.
- **[[Transfer-translations]]**: Formulas that translate written scripture into new spoken languages while claiming to preserve original meaning (following Marcel Jousse).
- **[[Center]]**: The sacred or authoritative locus around which ritual participants organize their exchanges and narratives.
- **[[Declarative culture]]**: A culture concerned with demonstrating equivalence between original formulas and their translations across linguistic change.
- **[[Mythology]]**: The declarative narratives that preserve ritual identity over time in oral cultures.

## Notable Passages

"The declarative was invented in the course of deferring the imperative, so it follows that one trajectory of the declarative is to imagine the abolition of the imperative."

"Ritual is an exchange with the center: the participant fulfills some command of the center while making a request of the center. It is an asymmetrical imperative exchange."

"The originary purpose of the declarative, to supplement a failed imperative with a 'real' that preserves the relations implicit in the failed imperative, is activated here."

## Questions Raised

1. How can a modern literate culture recover the grounding in [[ostensive-imperative]] exchange that oral ritual naturally preserved, without abandoning the gains of literacy and classical prose?

2. What is the relationship between Marcel Jousse's [[transfer-translations]] and contemporary debates over linguistic meaning, interpretation, and the possibility of translation itself?

3. If the [[declarative]] tends toward abolishing the [[imperative]], what prevents a hyper-literate culture from complete meaning-collapse, and what role does [[center|centering]] play in that prevention?
