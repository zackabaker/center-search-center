## Summary

This piece examines Israel's role as a pioneer in surveillance and governance technologies that become globally distributed, reframing what critics present as "repression" within a generative anthropological framework. Rather than condemning governance itself, the author suggests that centralized ordering and the technologies required to maintain it are inevitable features of human organization. The deeper question becomes whether resistance to governance is intrinsic to humanity, or whether perfectly efficient systems might eliminate the desire to resist altogether.

## Key Concepts

- [[Center]] – The occupied position that distributes resources, attracts resentments, and requires information-gathering mechanisms
- [[Governance]] – An unavoidable structural feature of human organization, not merely an imposed evil
- [[Resentment]] – The fundamental drive that centers must defer and manage through evolving technological means
- [[Surveillance]] – The informational infrastructure required for effective centralized distribution and order maintenance
- [[Anarchist anthropology]] – The assumption that inequality and governance are exogenous impositions rather than structural necessities
- [[Residual humanity]] – The irreducible desire to resist governance, foundational to liberal thought
- [[Primarchy]] – Rule organized around a central distributing authority ("Big Man revolution")

## Notable Passages

> "The center is occupied, the center distributes, the center attracts and must defer resentments, and the means of doing so and acquiring the information needed to do so effectively will change along with the means of interfering with or thwarting the fulfillment of such obligations."

> "An interesting trope of dystopian science fiction is the system that governs too well, so well that you don't even notice you're being governed and wouldn't be able to form the intention to resist."

> "That there is some, perhaps the most irreducible, ingredient of residual humanity, to be found in the inextinguishable desire to resist governance, is a non-negotiable component of all liberal thought."

## Questions Raised

1. Is the desire to resist governance truly "irreducible" to humanity, or can sufficiently efficient systems actually eliminate the capacity for such resistance?

2. How do we distinguish between governance technologies that maintain necessary order and those that exceed their [[center|centripetal]] function to become genuinely oppressive?

3. If conflicts between states serve as communicative and deterrent mechanisms, does this reframe international tensions as systemic features rather than aberrations requiring resolution?
