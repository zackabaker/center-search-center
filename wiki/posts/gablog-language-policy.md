## Summary

This essay argues that language fundamentally functions as the deferral of [[mimetic violence]], particularly [[scapegoating]] violence directed at central figures. All linguistic choices—from everyday speech to abstract concepts—implicitly manage the risk of catastrophic social collapse by either loading guilt onto individuals or constructing reasons to spare them. In post-sacrificial societies, language becomes saturated with ways of naming "impossible victims," yet this very saturation necessarily implies the possibility of victimization. The paradox of ethical language is that it must hold both poles—the impossibility and possibility of victimization—in tension, a practice Gans calls [[originary satire]].

## Key Concepts

- [[Sign-deferral]]: A sign postpones imminent or possible [[mimetic violence]] through nomination and symbolic representation
- [[Scapegoating]]: The collective violent expulsion directed at a central figure, the most feared form of violence in organized societies
- [[Central authority]]: The figure upon whom order depends and against whom [[centralizing violence]] is perpetually tempted
- [[Impossible victim]]: A person ritually protected through linguistic saturation, rendered symbolically off-limits to sacrificial violence
- [[Originary satire]]: The representation of transgressive temptation within the framework of victimization-impossibility; ethical language holding paradox
- [[Mimetic crisis]]: The threat of undifferentiated violence that deferral systems prevent
- [[Linguistic intuition]]: Our native ability to detect whether speech is prepping someone for or protecting them from sacrificial violence

## Notable Passages

> "A sign is the deferral of violence. The first sign deferred imminent violence; subsequent signs may also do so, or they may defer intimations of merely possible violence, raising the threshold of what would count as a cause for violence."

> "Always naming impossible victims is the most moral way of speaking and writing; such a practice eventually entails naming ways of determining when, where and how the impossible victim is to be named."

> "The representation of the temptation of transgression within the impossibilization of victimization is originary satire."

## Questions Raised

1. How do we linguistically distinguish between legitimate [[force]] against criminals and the illegitimate [[centralizing violence]] of [[scapegoating]]—where exactly is that threshold located in actual discourse?

2. If [[originary satire]] requires holding both victimization and impossible-victimization in constant tension, what happens when one pole collapses or becomes politically weaponized (as in victimary progressivism)?

3. Can [[linguistic intuition]] be reliably trusted to detect sacrificial prep, or is it itself subject to [[mimetic]] manipulation and group conformity pressure?
