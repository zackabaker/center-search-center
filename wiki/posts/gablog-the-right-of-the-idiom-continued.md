## Summary

This post argues that rights should be grounded not in pre-political property ownership (as classical liberalism claims) but in participation in the [[gift economy]]. Drawing on De Soto's work on formalizing informal economies, the author proposes that gift and market economies coexist, and that everyone participates in gift exchange through language and representation. This reframing dissolves the contradictions that plague liberal theory—which struggles to extend rights to the non-propertied—and establishes a foundational "right of the idiom": the right to self-representation and entry into symbolic exchange.

## Key Concepts

- [[Gift economy]]: The economy of symbolic and material exchange preceding or coexisting alongside capitalist commodity exchange
- [[Money economy]]: The formalized, legalized market system that requires property rights and state enforcement
- [[Right of the idiom]]: The fundamental right to distinctive self-representation and entry into language and social exchange
- [[Property rights]]: Not pre-political natural rights but contingent on participation in gift exchange and symbolic systems
- [[Representational exchange]]: The most basic form of gift economy, wherein signs and meanings circulate between participants
- [[Liberal fiction]]: The false assumption that rights-bearing individuals and property ownership exist in nature prior to law
- [[Bell jar]]: The legal order protecting only elite property while excluding the masses from formal economic recognition

## Notable Passages

> "Rights are rooted in one's participation in a gift economy. One's participation in a gift economy makes one capable of entering the money, market economy, because the possibility of converting gifts into commodities is inherent in the juxtaposition of the two economies."

> "Anyone who speaks can tell a joke, or laugh at one, in a way no one else can...occupy a place in the world of signs, in short."

> "The gift economy and the money economy exist side by side, [and] any idiom could conceivably cross over the boundary and become property."

## Questions Raised

1. How does the "right of the idiom" prevent the commodification of idioms themselves—doesn't the capacity to convert any gift into property threaten the autonomy of distinctive self-representation?

2. If everyone participates in gift economy through representation, does this dissolve meaningful distinctions between the included and excluded, or does it risk obscuring real forms of legal and economic marginalization?

3. What would a legal order founded on the right of the idiom actually look like in practice, and how would it relate to existing property law?
