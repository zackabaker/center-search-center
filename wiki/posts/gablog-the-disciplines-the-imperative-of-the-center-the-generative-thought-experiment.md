## Summary

This essay argues that the academic disciplines study representations of central social forces rather than those forces themselves, systematically deferring attention from the actual scene of scholarly representation. The [[originary scene]] establishes the [[center]] as the locus of human intentionality, yet disciplinary knowledge systematizes this centering through "nominalizations" that saturate imagined scenes while obscuring the reader-writer scene. Writing itself functions as a technology that keeps author and reader "sceneless," filling in represented scenes to defer recognition of how attention itself operates in the disciplinary field.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Center]]**: The originary focal point where human intentionality emerges; later borrowed and reimagined as "society," "ideology," and "the media"
- **[[Originary Scene]]**: The founding human moment where the sign inaugurates representation and central being becomes the mirror of group intentions
- **[[Anthropomorphics]]**: The study of how margins represent and relate to the center; the practice underlying all disciplines
- **[[Nominalizations]]**: Linguistic deposits (e.g., "social structures," "cognitive behavior") that simulate causality while deferring attention to the scene of representation itself
- **[[Scenelessness]]**: The condition of writer and reader kept deliberately divorced from the scene of their own engagement
- **[[Saturation]]**: The density of represented detail in prose aimed at closing differences between writer and reader while obscuring the disciplinary scene

## Notable Passages

"The central being is most fully intentional participant on the scene: he 'understands' the desires animating the members of the group, along with the ruin to which they lead, while, finally, repelling the violent approach to itself."

"Enhancing the density of the presumed causality is the way we avoid paying attention to our modes of attention."

"All the arguments within a discipline, then, concern the proper degree of saturation."

## Questions Raised

1. How can thought escape the very mechanism of [[saturation]] it describes—i.e., how does GA itself avoid becoming another discipline that defers the disciplinary scene?

2. If all disciplines operate through nominalizations that conceal the reader-writer scene, what specific practices or attentional shifts would constitute genuine access to that concealed scene?

3. Does the text suggest that literary or poetic writing, with its refusal of full saturation, offers a way out of disciplinary obfuscation, or does it remain entangled in the same deferral structure?
