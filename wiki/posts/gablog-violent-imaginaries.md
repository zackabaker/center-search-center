## Summary

This post challenges the literal plausibility of the originary scene's collapse into universal violence, arguing instead that what matters is the [[violent imaginary]]—the shared, constitutive perception of unbounded chaos that proto-humans must project onto their breakdown in hierarchy. Drawing on Collingwood's concept of imagination as perception-completing supplement, the author argues that all culture is shaped by complementary violent imaginaries that operate negatively: we never reach actual chaos, but every social order is defined against the imagined threat of total dissolution.

## Key Concepts

- [[violent imaginary]] — A shared perception of potential total chaos that structures social order without being literally true
- [[originary event]] — The proto-human moment when mimetic fervor threatens hierarchy collapse, prompting the first sign
- [[mimetic fervor]] — The heightened mutual imitation that intensifies proto-human confrontation
- [[imaginary]] (Collingwood) — A constitutive supplement to perception that completes and makes meaningful what is perceived
- [[sign]] — The symmetrical gesture that mirrors and controls the negative symmetry of imagined violence
- [[pecking order]] — The pre-symbolic hierarchy whose breakdown triggers the originary mechanism
- [[chaos]] — The theoretical limit-state toward which social breakdown is imaginatively projected but never reaches

## Notable Passages

> "What is extremely plausible, though, is that such an all-consuming storm of violence is what that breakdown would *look like* to the members of the proto-human group—and that it would need to look that way in order to prompt the invention of the sign."

> "The violent imaginary on the originary works in the same way—as soon as the beginnings of this new form of confrontation becomes visible to the participants on the scene, those participants must continue and complete it."

> "Society can be dissolved into clans, gangs, militias, tribes, but never into a 'war of all against all.' Again, though, this doesn't make the imaginary false."

## Questions Raised

1. If the violent imaginary need not be literally true to be constitutive, how do we distinguish between productive imaginaries (those that maintain order) and pathological ones (those that generate unnecessary violence)?

2. Can different cultures be said to have fundamentally different violent imaginaries, and if so, does this explain variation in their institutional forms and tolerance thresholds?

3. Does the concept of violent imaginary apply equally to modern, mediated societies where actual confrontation is rare—or does digitization create new forms of imaginary altogether?
