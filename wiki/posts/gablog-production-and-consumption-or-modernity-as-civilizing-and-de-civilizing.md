## Summary

This essay reopens the classical Marxist debate between production and consumption, arguing that while economically exhausted, the question remains politically vital. The author contends that consumption functions differently across class lines: the salaried classes use consumption to differentiate themselves, while the waged classes express productive identity and class solidarity through consumption. Rather than settling into consumer capitalism's triumph, the text explores how consumerism has paradoxically destabilized working-class self-discipline and communal cohesion, suggesting modernity operates simultaneously as civilizing and de-civilizing force.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Production vs. Consumption]]** – The tension between valuing labor/work identity versus consumer satisfaction as organizing principles of society
- **[[Salaried vs. Waged Classes]]** – Distinction between differentiation-through-consumption (salaried) and solidarity-through-consumption (waged)
- **[[Productivist Consumerism]]** – Working-class consumption that references physical labor and communality rather than status differentiation
- **[[Class Discipline]]** – The historical process of civilizing working populations through moral and behavioral restraint
- **[[Civilizing and De-civilizing]]** – Modernity's paradoxical movement toward both greater order and greater dissolution of traditional restraints
- **[[Resentment]]** – The emotional substrate of class consciousness and resistance to upper-class consumption patterns

## Notable Passages

> "We are not just consumers, George" but "workers and citizens. And 'worker' (producer) does seem to be far more conjoined to 'citizen' than does 'consumer.'"

> "The independence, self-respect and community participation that comes from having a job are 'goods' to be placed in the balance against those purchased at Wal-Mart."

> "The consumption of the waged resists the consumerism of the salaried aimed at generating differences, and references a productive identity grounded in physical labor, or physicality and communality more generally."

## Questions Raised

1. If working-class consumption inherently resists consumerism while remaining dependent on it, can any political intervention actually support "productivist consumerism" or is it structurally doomed?

2. Does the de-civilizing effect of consumer society on the waged class represent capitalism's internal contradiction, or merely the predictable consequence of removing the disciplinary structures (guild, church, community) that once contained consumption?

3. How might the distinction between consumption-as-differentiation and consumption-as-solidarity reshape our understanding of political messaging and class formation in the contemporary moment?
