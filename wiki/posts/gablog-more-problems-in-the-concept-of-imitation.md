## Summary

This text explores the dangerous escalation of [[imitation]] from simple motor copying to mimetic rivalry over objects. Gans distinguishes between attending to a model's *moves* versus attending to the model's *intentional relation* to an object—a shift that generates conflicting imperatives and potential violence. The resolution emerges through [[deferral]]: imitating the model's reverence toward the object rather than seeking to possess it, which requires not mere imitation but [[iteration]] and explicit signification. Even in highly mediated contexts (like intellectual apprenticeship), moments arise where distance collapses and one confronts the model directly, with ambiguous implications for originality and succession.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Imperative]]** — a directive force issuing from action or presence that compels response
- **[[Mimetic desire]]** — the escalating wish to possess what the model possesses, leading toward violence
- **[[Deferral]]** — the renunciation of possession in favor of guarding or honoring the object
- **[[Iteration]]** — the repeated signification of assent to a gesture or rule, distinguishing it from mere imitation
- **[[The scene]]** — the spatial-attentional frame within which model and object cannot simultaneously be seen
- **[[Mediation]]** — the layering of distance between imitator and model through rule, style, and convention
- **[[Signification]]** — the explicit gesture by which one announces participation in a communal stance

## Notable Passages

> "Insofar as you see the model you don't see the object and insofar as you see the object the model must be eliminated from the scene. So, there are conflicting imperatives: do this (move toward the object); look at that—but this second imperative, precisely in proportion to the model's interest, would better be formulated as look at me."

> "To destroy the other's relation to the object is to enter the entirety of his relation to the object, to inhabit his tacit and explicit tendency toward the object."

> "Even in the most mediated mimetic relations...there will be those occasions where I ask myself, 'what would x do,' right here and now—as if all distance were abolished and we occupied the same frame."

## Questions Raised

- How does the shift from motor imitation to intentional-object imitation correspond to stages in human development or historical emergence?
- If even "mediated" intellectual imitation can collapse back into the immediate frame where violence lurks, what practical or ethical resources prevent such collapse?
- Does the requirement for explicit signification (iteration) suggest that all human community depends fundamentally on linguistic self-consciousness about mimetic processes?
