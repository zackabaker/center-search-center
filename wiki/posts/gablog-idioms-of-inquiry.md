## Summary

This post develops a linguistic framework for [[generative anthropology]] by grounding all human discourse in four utterance modes: [[ostensive]], [[imperative]], [[interrogative]], and [[declarative]]. The author argues that sentences can be productively read as answers to anticipated questions, that imperatives are "softened" into questions, and that all utterances ultimately function as commands to iterate the sign itself. This idiom of inquiry offers a more rigorous alternative to postmodern "power relations" discourse by tracing linguistic acts back to their originary grounding in shared attention and deferral.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Ostensive]]**: The primitive sign pointing to a shared object; the foundation from which other utterance modes emerge
- **[[Imperative]]**: Commands that make things happen; "softened" into interrogatives when uncertain of obedience
- **[[Interrogative]]**: Questions as imperatives cognizant of possible non-compliance; the mode of genuine uncertainty
- **[[Declarative]]**: Statements presenting a reality while embedding hidden imperatives and desires
- **[[Originary hypothesis]]**: Eric Gans's account of language origin as constitutive of the human
- **[[Negative ostensive]]**: The first predication—deferral of the object—that structures all subsequent language
- **[[Sign iteration]]**: The fundamental imperative that any utterance performs: repeat me, operate within my space

## Notable Passages

"Any sentence might be answering more than one [question]—each word in the sentence could be read in terms of the question it is answering, of the anticipated follow up question it is answering."

"Only imperatives can make anything happen beyond the centered attention effected by the ostensive."

"If someone says, 'The door is open,' maybe they want me to close the door, maybe they want me to leave...the sentence is telling me to do something."

## Questions Raised

1. If all sentences embed imperatives, how do we distinguish between sentences that *intend* to command action versus those that primarily orient attention? Where is the boundary between declarative and imperative?

2. Does grounding discourse analysis in utterance modes genuinely avoid the postmodern trap of unfalsifiable "power relations," or does it simply displace the problem to a deeper level of linguistic analysis?

3. How would this framework account for utterances that seem genuinely non-functional—lyric poetry, metaphysics, pure aesthetic forms—or must all language ultimately be reducible to pragmatic iteration of signs?
