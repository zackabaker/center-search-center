## Summary

This essay examines the paradox of modern state power through the lens of James Scott's critique of technocratic rationality. The author argues that genuinely absolute power would be nearly invisible, while the modern state's obsessive standardization and intervention—justified by scientific expertise—actually reveals its insecurity. Drawing on absolutist theory, the text shows how the state's centralization of technological capacity combines with liberal individualism to fragment communities and intermediary institutions, creating a high-low alliance that bypasses the middle class entirely.

## Key Concepts

- [[Sovereignty]]: Power genuinely absolute would be barely noticeable; visibility indicates divided and insecure power
- [[High-low alliance]]: State mechanism that mobilizes the wealthy and the poor against middle-class institutions and communities
- [[Standardization]]: Erasure of tacit knowledge and local judgment in favor of expert-generated blueprints
- [[Intermediary institutions]]: Communities, neighborhoods, and associations demolished by insecure centralized power
- [[Absolutism]]: Political theory that critiques both the monstrous modern state and liberal fragmentation
- [[Scientism]]: The dialectic whereby states centralize technological development while fragmenting social bonds
- [[Tacit knowledge]]: The embedded wisdom of people dependent on actual social structures, erased by technocratic planning

## Notable Passages

"Genuinely absolute power would be barely noticeable, while the more pervasive, invasive and unavoidable the power, the more divided and unsecured."

"The state centralizes (beneficial) technological and scientific activity, it fragments institutions, communities and individuals."

"It's easy to see how difficult it would be to separate the centralization of knowledge from the fragmentation of people."

## Questions Raised

1. How can we distinguish between genuinely beneficial large-scale technological development (highways, agriculture, infrastructure) and the state's destructive fragmentation of communities—or are these necessarily inseparable under modern conditions?

2. If the high-low alliance is the mechanism by which insecure power operates, what would genuine sovereign security look like institutionally, and could it be recovered?

3. Does the tension between standardization and fragmentation admit of resolution, or is it constitutive of Enlightenment modernity itself?
