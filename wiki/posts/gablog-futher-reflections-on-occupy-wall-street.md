## Summary

This essay examines OWS through a generative anthropology lens, grounding the movement's appeal in humanity's originary sense of [[reciprocity]] and equality. The author traces how the Democratic Party has positioned itself as the party of wealth redistribution while Republicans defend free markets, yet argues that government redistribution cannot sustainably create wealth. The core tension is between [[equality]] of rights and equality of outcomes—inequality itself is not prima facie evidence of injustice.

## Key Concepts

- [[Reciprocity]]: The originary human intuition that contributions should be returned; violation triggers resentment without need for instruction
- [[Equality]]: Both an originary human intuition and the rhetorical foundation of modern political movements
- [[Originary hypothesis]]: Eric Gans's anthropological grounding of basic human moral intuitions in the origin of language
- [[Wealth redistribution]]: Government transfer of resources between citizens; creates inefficiency and unsustainable job growth
- [[Rights vs. outcomes]]: The distinction between equal rights (justice) and equal results (egalitarian fantasy)
- [[Market economy]]: The only sustainable mechanism for producing wealth; government role is protective, not generative

## Notable Passages

"We have a virtually instinctive sense of reciprocity; when our contribution is not reciprocated, we do not need anyone to teach us to feel upset."

"The problem is that the government does not itself produce wealth... All the government can really do, economically, is to take money from one pocket and put it in another."

"Inequality of wealth is in fact no evidence of injustice... equality of rights...is not the same thing as equality of outcomes."

## Questions Raised

1. How does the originary sense of reciprocity become weaponized by victimary politics into a demand for equality of outcomes rather than mere equality of rights?

2. Can government ever play a productive (rather than purely redistributive) economic role without violating the market's generative logic?

3. Does OWS's generational character suggest that younger cohorts have lost the distinction between reciprocal obligation and entitlements, or merely that they experience scarcity more acutely?
