## Summary

This post traces the genealogy of money and markets through the lens of sovereignty, arguing that markets are not natural phenomena but creations of absolutist states designed to enable bureaucratic control and military logistics. Drawing on David Graeber, the author contends that money's value derives from sovereign security rather than labor or desire, and that currency stabilizes through the tension between individual abstraction and hierarchical ordering. The liberal order's promise of "freedom" through abstraction has indeed generated wealth and technology, but at the cost of destabilizing traditional reciprocal relations and producing vast inequality. The fundamental problem for any absolutist order is managing the destabilizing effects of abstraction itself.

## Key Concepts

- [[sovereignty]] — the power to issue currency, collect taxes, and maintain order through the monopoly on legitimate violence
- [[money and capital]] — originally a tool for bureaucratic accounting and soldier payment, later abstracted into a measure of sovereign security
- [[abstraction]] — the progressive removal of individuals from embedded social roles and reciprocal obligations, which accelerates wealth but destabilizes hierarchy
- [[megamachine]] — Mumford's concept of mass organization originating in military structure, later applied to labor and technology
- [[liberal order]] — a system that promises freedom through individual abstraction but generates both unprecedented wealth and inequality
- [[reciprocity]] — pre-monetary gift-economy logic where roles and obligations are embedded in community relations
- [[de-abstraction]] — the reordering of abstracted individuals back into stable hierarchies to prevent destabilization

## Notable Passages

> "Money and markets therefore accelerate that system, abstracting individuals from their social relations, enhancing the power of the sovereign, while generating new power bases that might destabilize power."

> "What sustains the value of any currency, in that case, is the stability and reliability of the sovereign issuing and approving it. Rather than labor or subjective desire, currency reflects the 'value' of sovereign security."

> "The problem for an absolutist order is to re-embed individuals in ordered hierarchies, which is to say 'de-abstract' them."

## Questions Raised

1. If money's value truly depends on sovereign security rather than labor, what happens to currency in periods of genuine sovereign weakness or transition—do markets collapse, or do alternative ordering systems emerge?

2. The post describes liberal abstraction as both destabilizing and generative of wealth. Is there a stable equilibrium point, or is the liberal order necessarily caught in perpetual oscillation between abstraction and re-ordering?

3. Can de-abstraction occur without authoritarian violence, or is the reordering of abstracted individuals inherently coercive?
