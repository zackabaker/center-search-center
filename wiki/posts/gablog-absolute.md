# Absolute

## Summary

This essay argues that all peaceful activity necessarily depends on [[sovereignty]]—some concentrated authority capable of distinguishing between peaceable and predatory conduct within a territory. The author traces how communities delegate protection to specialists, eventually creating sovereign power, but shows that liberal attempts to limit sovereignty through rights and constitutions fail because whoever *enforces* those limits becomes sovereign. The "checks and balances" of liberal democracy are thus a shell game that obscures real power concentration while creating conditions for perpetual struggle.

## Key Concepts

- [[Sovereignty]]: Concentrated power to distinguish peaceable from predatory conduct and enforce that distinction within a territory.
- [[Comparative advantage]]: The principle that specialized peaceful activity requires delegating protection to specialists, creating hierarchy.
- [[Predation]]: Violent interruption of peaceful activity through robbery, coercion, revenge, or arbitrary force.
- [[Liberal limitation]]: The attempt to constrain sovereign power through rights and constitutions—which creates ambiguity about who enforces those constraints.
- [[Ultimate sovereignty]]: The final decision-making authority; in liberal theory situated with "the people," but practically always concentrated in some branch or militia.
- [[Peaceful activity]]: Any non-violent, productive conduct that requires protection from those who would disrupt it.

## Notable Passages

> "Once communities are sufficiently differentiated so that various non-ritual interactions take place regularly within a sovereign territory, the sovereign is primarily distinguishing between the peaceable and the predatory amongst his own people."

> "Whoever decides is, then, sovereign... the decision is made by some branch of the government itself, which means there's a kind of shell game going on here."

> "The more power that seems to be lying around unclaimed, the more people will try and figure out how to pick it up."

## Questions Raised

1. If sovereignty is *necessary* for peaceful activity, is the liberal dream of limited government fundamentally incoherent—or can some institutional mechanism genuinely distribute ultimate authority without concentrating it?

2. How does the author's analysis account for long periods of relative stability under divided powers (checks and balances)—are these merely temporary equilibria before power reconcentrates, or do they suggest something more durable?

3. If liberal democracy is a "shell game" that obscures real power concentration, what honest alternative framework for sovereignty does GA offer?
