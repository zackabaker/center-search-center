## Summary

This essay examines an "emergent mode of thinking" represented in a letter defending college safe spaces, arguing it constitutes a radical historical break in how language, morality, and psychology function. The author demonstrates how therapeutic and victimary categories have replaced classical concepts like truth, justice, and freedom with substitutes (open-minded growth, safety, empathy) that are logically unstable and self-contradicting. Unlike 1960s radicalism, this new victimary discourse cannot even articulate coherent ideas it opposes—it can only ritually denounce them. The piece identifies this shift as a genuine "sea change" in human thought, one seamlessly intersecting with contemporary institutional and cultural support systems.

## Key Concepts

- [[Victimary progressivism]]: A mode of thought organized around protecting victims from re-traumatization rather than pursuing abstract truth or justice
- [[Declarative culture]]: A system where ideas are deemed valid or invalid through collective proclamation rather than reasoned argument
- [[Safe spaces]]: Institutional zones defined by the exclusion of ideas deemed threatening to vulnerable groups
- [[Empathy]]: The replacement concept for justice; centers the affective state of victims over universal standards
- [[Moral/ethical governance]]: Rule based on therapeutic inclusion rather than abstract law or desert
- [[Generational thinking]]: The gnostic self-righteousness of youth claiming validity over inherited frameworks without articulating coherent alternatives
- [[Ritual denunciation]]: The performative rejection of "failed ideas" without actually engaging their arguments
- [[Double talk]]: Logical self-contradiction (e.g., "inclusion" that necessarily excludes dissenters)

## Notable Passages

"Instead of 'truth,' we have 'open-minded intellectual growth,' which is here virtually defined by the extent to which 'victims' (the most unstable among us) are protected from any ideas that would re-traumatize them."

"The gnostic, self-righteous deification of the new generation and complementary demonization of the old is familiar to us from the 1960s, but it's interesting that Meerwarth refers so vaguely and blandly to those 'failed ideas of the past.'"

"For the victimary thinker, they are more inbred, ontological features of the inherited social landscape—the possibility that there were ever ideas there...cannot even be imagined."
