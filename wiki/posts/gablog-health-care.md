## Summary

Health care as a modern phenomenon is inseparable from state power and expert authority. The author traces how medical metaphors have displaced moral categories (sin, guilt, good/evil) with therapeutic ones (healthy/sick, normal/pathological), allowing progressive politics to expand state reach into all dimensions of life that "indirectly" affect health. Even libertarian privatization schemes face irresolvable paradoxes: medicine cannot function like car insurance because bodily suffering has no ceiling, guaranteeing resentment and political capture. True market medicine would require a cultural capacity to accept life's unfairness—something our [[declarative culture]] may be unable to sustain.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Declarative Culture]]** — The modern order where expertise and official pronouncements structure reality and governance
- **[[Expert Power]]** — The apparatus of specialized knowledge that extends state control into all domains touching health
- **[[Therapeutic Displacement]]** — The substitution of moral categories (sin/evil) with medical ones (sick/healthy), enabling moral expansion of state authority
- **[[Victimocracy]]** — The political channeling of resentment through media saturation around suffering and denial of care
- **[[Moral Economy]]** — The underlying cultural assumptions about fairness, obligation, and acceptable sacrifice that constrain what systems can actually function
- **[[Resentment]]** — The inevitable emotional residue when market mechanisms collide with existential stakes

## Questions Raised

1. Is there any historical or cultural precedent for a society accepting the "basic unfairness of life chances and death" with equanimity, or is this requirement itself a fantasy that no system can meet?

2. If the therapeutic displacement of moral language is essentially permanent in modern consciousness, what structural alternatives to state-centralized health care remain theoretically viable?

3. How does the media's role in circulating images of suffering actually constitute a new form of political power that no insurance contract or market mechanism can bypass?
