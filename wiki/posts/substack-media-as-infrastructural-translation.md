## Summary

This post traces how [[ritual]], [[myth]], and [[juridical scenes]] form a generative sequence foundational to Western discourse and media. Beginning with ritual exchange with a sacred center, the author shows how myth emerges when ritual promises fail, generating resentment and introspection. The introduction of juridical structures creates new possibilities for narrative and justice, but opens a uniquely Western problem: once judges themselves become subject to judgment, we enter a meta-juridical "politics" that generates both Western achievement and pathology.

## Key Concepts

- [[Ritual]]: Originary imperative exchange with a sacred center, enacting sacrificial reciprocity with a deity or collectivity
- [[Myth]]: Narrative accounting for failures in ritual promise-fulfillment, generating interpersonal resentment and moral introspection
- [[Juridical scene]]: Institutional structures of judgment independent of but imitative of ritual, enabling new theological and literary discourse
- [[Resentment]]: Directional affect—toward ritual performers in sacred orders, toward unjust judges in juridical orders
- [[Justice]]: The central question animating all narratives and social orders: how to deal fairly with others, God, and oneself
- [[Meta-juridical politics]]: The Western problematic of judging the judge, creating recursive procedures for accountability that generate both progress and disorder

## Notable Passages

> "In a ritual order, resentment is directed at those who perform the ritual incorrectly; in a juridical order, resentment is directed at those who don't deal justly."

> "All of our narratives are attempts to answer that question, or to teach each others how to prepare themselves to do so"—on dealing justly.

> "This is really a form of disorder that I think is specific to the 'West,' and responsible for its glories as well as its deformities and pathologies."
