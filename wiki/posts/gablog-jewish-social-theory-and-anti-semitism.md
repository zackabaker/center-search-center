## Summary

This essay grounds social theory in the observable fact of [[tacit agreement]]—the unspoken dimensions that make explicit contracts intelligible. Rather than appealing to abstract principles like natural law, the author proposes we study how communities maintain and revise their agreements while welcoming newcomers without diluting their "density of the tacit." Judaism and Christianity, understood through a [[generative anthropology|GA]] lens, represent a historical repudiation of the [[emissary victim]] system and the empire-building logic of human sacrifice that depends on it. The text suggests anti-Semitism emerges as hostility toward the Jewish (and Christian) challenge to victimary imperial power.

## Key Concepts

- [[Tacit agreement]]: The unspoken, sedimented dimensions of social contracts that make explicit agreements comprehensible
- [[Explicit agreement]]: Formally articulated covenants and contracts that depend on tacit foundations
- [[Emissary victim]]: The Big Man who gathers community resentment and can be sacrificed on behalf of the collective
- [[Rooted openness]]: A community's capacity to generalize and include outsiders without dissolving its internal coherence
- [[Declarative culture]]: Culture organized around the [[declarative sentence]], the naming of the One God
- [[Human sacrifice]]: The imperial system of victimary substitution that follows the logic of asymmetric gifting
- [[Meta-agreement]]: A higher-order agreement about how to revise, discard, or generalize existing agreements

## Notable Passages

> "We are people with a certain relation to each other—we have agreed so far and no further."

## Questions Raised

1. How does the transition from the emissary victim to systematic substitute victims occur conceptually and historically? What cognitive leap enables empires to perceive victims as fungible?

2. If anti-Semitism is rooted in hostility to the repudiation of victimary logic, does this mean [[victimary progressivism]] represents a paradoxical return to the very system Judaism sought to overcome?

3. Can a community genuinely maintain "rooted openness"—welcoming outsiders while preserving tacit density—or does pluralism necessarily thin the sedimented agreements that hold it together?
