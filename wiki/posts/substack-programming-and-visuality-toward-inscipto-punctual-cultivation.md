## Summary

Technology and media function as mechanisms of governance that reshape human perception, cognition, and affect across successive innovations from writing to smartphones. The author argues that modern technology serves as the perfecting of the imperative, enabling capital-state complexes to mobilize populations through competing media strategies. Eric Gans's concept of the [[screenic]]—a medium that creates ontological otherness—helps explain how cinema and digital devices have replaced traditional scenic forms, creating a culture where spectators become co-creators of their own spectacle, fundamentally altering the conditions of human self-formation and political control.

## Key Concepts

- [[Screenic|The screenic]]: A medium creating an ontologically separate world of representation that captures total attention and replaces prior scenic forms like ritual and theater
- [[Media governance]]: Technology as a form of control that shapes cognition, affect, and the kinds of selves required for contemporary disciplines
- [[Scenic design]]: The arrangement of perceptual and representational space; modernly perfected through technology as an imperative form
- [[Capital-state articulation]]: The reciprocal relationship between capitalist accumulation and state power, competing through media and intelligence operations
- [[Mobilization]]: The strategic deployment of knowledge (whether true or false) to coordinate action within ranks while fragmenting opponent capacity
- [[Ontological otherness]]: The quality of representing a separate universe of human culture distinct from the appetitive-inhibitive animal world

## Notable Passages

"Technology is governance, and so is media." The study of successive media should be undertaken as studies in mass mobilization—which may often take the form of mass neutralization.

"The screenic universe was the first full-fledged duplication of the ontological otherness of the scene of representation...freed by its technological independence from the artifacts of worldly framing."

"Today there is a screenic culture that corresponds to a desire to be both spectator and creator of one's own spectacle."

## Questions Raised

- If the screenic medium has replaced the traditional scene as the primary locus of human representation, what happens to the [[originary scene]] itself—does it become merely another content for the screen rather than the grounding origin?
- How can effective mobilization resist fragmentation when capital-state competition over media necessarily distributes partial knowledge across competing sectors?
- What would constitute a counter-screenic practice, or is the screenic now inescapable as the condition of contemporary culture?
