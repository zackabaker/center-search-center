## Summary

Every political desire—"we should have Medicare for all," "someone should fix poverty"—implicitly imagines a [[sovereign imaginary]]: a unitary, competent central authority free from competing interests and capable of implementing one's vision. Gans argues this imaginative structure is unavoidable and built into language itself. Rather than debating competing policy visions, we should recognize this shared underlying agreement and ask what practices enable us to orient toward [[central authority]] that actually works—not through revolutionary faction-building, but through sustained disciplinary engagement in the spaces we inhabit.

## Key Concepts

- [[Sovereign Imaginary]]: The implicit assumption of a unitary, competent central authority embedded in any political desire or resentment
- [[Central Authority]]: Clear, undivided power capable of executing policy free from parasitic interest groups and bureaucratic factions
- [[Political Desire]]: The "we should" formulation that necessarily presupposes but also contradicts itself through the sovereign imaginary
- [[Disciplinary Spaces]]: Concrete communities and practices where agreement about central authority can be grounded and spread
- [[Anthropological Inquiry]]: Examining the deep assumptions (ontological, epistemological) underlying any imagined authority
- [[Interest Groups]]: The crisscrossing powers that corrupt the gap between policy intention and reality

## Notable Passages

> "Even if you say, yes, I know there is corruption and incompetence... you are still assuming that these are marginal to the sovereign power you imagine—if not, you wouldn't be able to say 'we should.'"

> "Implicit in your political desire is its cancellation... [the] sovereign power might have very different ideas... and freed of all the interfering powers would have very little reason to care what you think."

> "We can talk amicably about something upon which we have just found we agree: there 'should be' a central authority that can carry out policies unhindered by interest groups, nosy NGOs, bureaucratic factions."

## Questions Raised

1. If the sovereign imaginary is inescapable in language, how do we distinguish between more and less realistic paths toward clarified central authority without falling into revolutionary faction-splitting?

2. What specific disciplinary practices best maximize agreement about central authority while avoiding both utopianism and cynical abdication?

3. Can the anthropological inquiry into one's own sovereign assumptions genuinely change political behavior, or does recognizing the paradox leave us paralyzed?
