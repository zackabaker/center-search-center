## Summary

This essay synthesizes Hernando de Soto's *The Mystery of Capital* with generative anthropology, arguing that written legal documentation transforms possession into capital by deferring violence and creating economic generativity. De Soto's insight—that the poor worldwide hold vast undocumented assets that cannot function as capital—reveals that the West's economic success depends not on property rights per se but on a sign-system that makes those rights legible and exchangeable. The author suggests the West has never fully grasped this originary mechanism, leaving both development policy and contemporary property theory vulnerable to collapse.

## Key Concepts

- [[Writing]] — The sign-system that transforms physical possession into legally recognized property and enables capital circulation
- [[Property]] — Not merely individual possession but a deferred, documented claim that generates wealth through lending and exchange
- [[Capital]] — Property that can be borrowed against; generative wealth dependent on documented legibility
- [[Originary hypothesis]] — GA's founding theory of language emergence applied here to understand property systems as deferrals of violence
- [[Generativity]] — The productive capacity that arises when exchange is mediated by signs rather than immediate appropriation
- [[Medium run]] — The temporal space created when violence is deferred, allowing trust in expanding division of labor
- [[Idiom]] — The particular linguistic or systemic form through which a culture articulates its deepest commitments (here: property and right)

## Notable Passages

> "Writing...turns what is possessed into 'property,' by documenting all the features of those possessions deemed relevant by potential buyers, lenders, and the existing legal authorities."

> "Written legal documentation confers a new mode of generativity upon things by deferring violence in the short run and thereby creating a 'medium run'—that is, making it possible to rely upon the expanding division of labor stored in property."

> "Property rights defended on those traditional terms have proven vulnerable, because...the state is obliged to defend individual property rights while at the same time being viewed as the major threat to those rights."

## Questions Raised

1. If the West has unconsciously benefited from a property-documentation system it never fully theorized, what happens when that system is made explicit and contested—can it survive transparent scrutiny?

2. How might GA's account of deferral and violence illuminate the recurring modern tension between state protection of property and state predation upon it, beyond liberal theory's paralysis?

3. What would it mean to reformulate property rights not in Lockean individual terms but as originary deferrals—and would such a reformulation be politically sustainable or fatal?
