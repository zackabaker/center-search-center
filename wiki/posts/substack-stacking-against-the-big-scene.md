## Summary

This essay proposes "stacking"—the alignment of speech forms (ostensive, imperative, interrogative, declarative) to maximize internal referentiality—as a moral practice that dissolves the "Big Scene" illusion in social discourse. The Big Scene wrongly presupposes divisible agents (e.g., "blacks" and "whites") competing over finite social goods, a framework that perpetuates sacrificial thinking and resentment. Only named, chartered actors and individuals can genuinely narrate agency; attributing action to mystical collective entities erodes sovereignty and generates unfalsifiable conspiracy theories.

## Key Concepts

- [[Grammatical stack]]: Alignment of speech forms so imperatives map onto ostensive fields and declaratives generate new imperatives for other centers
- [[Big Scene]]: False representation of social reality as a single divisible scene with unified agents competing for central goods
- [[Sovereignty]]: The capacity to authorize action through named, chartered officials and individuals rather than mystical collective entities
- [[Agency]]: Narrativizable power restricted to sovereigns, chartered institutions, and named individuals, not abstract demographic categories
- [[Sacrificial thinking]]: Social logic modeled on exchange with a center, generating resentment when goods appear unequally distributed
- [[Resentment]]: The inevitable affect produced by Big Scene framings that presuppose inequality in a supposedly shared distribution
- [[Ostensive field]]: The perceptual/social ground from which imperatives emerge and which they serve to preserve

## Notable Passages

> "Big Scenic thinking can only generate resentment; moreover, it is an indication that we have not transcended sacrificial thinking, which can only be formulated into terms of exchanges with a center."

> "The most direct way to opt out of such narrative structures (in which whites and blacks fight, do injustices to each other, reconcile, try to work it out, etc.) is to treat only official names as narrativizable agents."

> "Those who are behind the scenes are ultimately named themselves—specific individuals, specific organizations, even if clandestine ones that are nevertheless following assignments from named actors."

## Questions Raised

1. How do we distinguish between legitimate institutional delegation of agency and the "behind the scenes" narratives that erode sovereignty—especially when clandestine actors do operate through named institutions?

2. If only named actors can narrativize, how do we account for emergent social phenomena (markets, epidemics, cultural shifts) that seem to arise without unified agents directing them?

3. Can the grammatical stack actually be taught or implemented, or does it require a prior transformation in how speakers relate to the [[ostensive field]]?
