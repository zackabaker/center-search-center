## Summary

This post explores the inescapability of [[irony]] in human discourse by extending the concept of "market" to all human activity—any presentation of possibility to competing desires. Drawing on Ian Dennis's *Lord Byron and the History of Desire*, the author argues that just as Gans saw economic and political markets as fundamentally interconnected, so too is all language vulnerable to ironic undermining. The Romantic hero's attempt to escape [[mimesis]] and achieve independence from others' desires inevitably fails, since any stance of freedom must be staged for an audience, creating the very irony it seeks to transcend.

## Key Concepts

- [[Market]] – Any presentation of competing possibilities or desires; extends beyond economics to all human activity and discourse
- [[Irony]] – The impossibility of language coinciding completely with intention; the gap between stance and its necessary social performance
- [[Mimesis]] – Desire rooted in imitation of others' desires; the structural bind preventing escape from social determination
- [[Romanticism]] – An aesthetic mode originating in the rejected subject's attempt at self-liberation through denial of recognition-seeking
- [[Desire of Others]] – Both one's own desires directed toward others and others' desires' effects on oneself; doubly binding
- [[Political Market]] – Gans's concept of liberal democracy as a market "superstructured" over the economic market, expressing and defusing resentments
- [[Heroic Stance]] – The impossible pose of independence and spontaneity that paradoxically requires an audience to be meaningful

## Notable Passages

> "Is there any human practice that can be situated outside of the desire for desire? If we follow Dennis's argument, the parallel question within discourse would be: is there anything, any imaginable individual or action, that is not vulnerable to irony?"

> "[T]o be free from the desire of others (in both senses—your desires for others, and their desire's effect on you) would be to be free from irony—everything you say would coincide completely with what you mean. But nothing could be more ironic than claiming such a stance."

> "Your stance could only be meaningful if you staged it for others—you could hardly embody heroic independence locked in your bedroom—thereby rendering yourself dependent on an audience."

## Questions Raised

1. If irony is truly inescapable in all discourse, does this render genuine communication—or genuine [[centering]]—impossible, or does GA offer a way to acknowledge irony while still achieving meaningful utterance?

2. Can Byron's *Don Juan* genuinely transcend the ironic trap by self-consciously deploying irony about irony, or does this merely create an infinite regress of nested ironies?

3. Is there a meaningful distinction between a rhetorical stance that acknowledges its own irony and one that remains naively trapped within it?
