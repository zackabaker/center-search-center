## Summary

This post traces the [[aesthetic]] back to the originary scene, where it emerges as an oscillation of attention between the [[sign]] (the aborted appropriative gesture) and the central [[sacred]] object. The aesthetic is subordinate to the sacred on the originary scene but becomes autonomous as "art" only later in cultural history. The author extends Gans's analysis by emphasizing that the sign is not merely pointing but involves the entire body in a novel [[equipoise]]—a balanced self-control that manages both threat and vulnerability in the face of rivals.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Aesthetic]]** — The human attention to the sign and fellow participants, distinct from sacred attention to the central object itself
- **[[Sacred]]** — Focused attention on the being and transcendence of the central prohibitive object
- **[[Sign]]** — The aborted gesture of appropriation that communicates non-advance toward the object
- **[[Originary scene]]** — The hypothetical first human scene where aesthetic and sacred oscillate in founding human consciousness
- **[[Form]]** — A repeatable, recognizable element that can be identified across different instantiations; the origin of formality in human culture
- **[[Equipoise]]** — The balanced bodily posture maintaining tension between recognizing the other as both threatening and vulnerable
- **[[Joint attention]]** — The coordinated focus of multiple participants on a common object (via Tomasello)

## Notable Passages

> "The aesthetic and sacred are interdependent, with the esthetic subordinate to the sacred, confirming the transcendence of the object."

> "The gesture is formal—indeed, this is the origin of 'form' in human existence, and by 'form' we can mean a part of an act or object we can single out and identify."

> "Equipoise suggests balance, in this case between the other as threatening, on the one hand, and as vulnerable, on the other."

## Questions Raised

1. If equipoise is "probably novel" to the originary scene, what precisely differentiates it from pre-human postural balancing? Is it the *intentional* management of ambivalence toward the rival?

2. How does the aesthetic oscillation between sign and object stabilize once we move beyond the originary scene? What prevents perpetual deferral of attention?

3. Can the body's equipoise itself become aestheticized—can we eventually attend to the grace of the balanced body as a sign in its own right, independent of the central object?
