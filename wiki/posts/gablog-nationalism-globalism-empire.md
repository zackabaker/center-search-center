## Summary

The text analyzes the ideological conflict between nationalism and globalism, examining how a transnational elite—rooted in corporations, media, finance, and diplomacy—develops interests structurally opposed to particular nations. While supra-national coordination is historically inevitable (diplomatic corps, scientific communities, multinational businesses), genuine antagonism emerges when imperial responsibility for global order becomes detached from national pride or necessity. The author argues that the nationalist core of any nation maintains tighter loyalty than its cosmopolitan "epidermis," and that the post-WWII American empire sustained nationalist support only through Cold War rivalry against a clear foe.

## Key Concepts

- [[Nationalism]]: commitment to the interests and values of a particular nation over universal or cosmopolitan principles
- [[Globalism]]: the worldview of elites seeking to reduce the world to a single economic and political unit, indifferent to national boundaries
- [[Empire]]: a form of power beyond the nation that requires ideological justification—either external threat or triumphal expansion
- [[National core and epidermis]]: the distinction between the nation's loyal interior and its cosmopolitan outer layer mediating global relations
- [[Supra-national order]]: inevitable institutional coordination (diplomacy, science, commerce) that doesn't necessarily create antagonistic elites
- [[Imperial responsibility]]: the burden assumed by dominant powers (post-WWII US) to govern broader zones; sustains nationalism only through clear opposition

## Notable Passages

"A very formidable enemy, to be sure." — On how global elites, by refusing to privilege any nation, ultimately become enemies to all nations.

"The globalized outer layer of the nation will certainly have attenuated loyalties compared to the core, but something else seems necessary for a genuine global elite, at odds with the nation, to emerge."

"In the case of the Cold War, which itself resulted from American inheritance of a world broken by two world wars, symmetrical rivalry silenced questions regarding what was essentially US governance of Western Europe and much of East Asia."

## Questions Raised

1. What happens to the ideological justification of empire once the unifying external threat (USSR) disappears? Does the post-Cold War American order face inevitable erosion of nationalist support?

2. Is the distinction between legitimate supra-national coordination (diplomacy, science, commerce) and antagonistic globalism merely one of degree, or does it rest on whether elites maintain ties to particular national cores?

3. How does the "national pride" mechanism sustain itself in imperial projects lacking both military victory and clear enemy?
