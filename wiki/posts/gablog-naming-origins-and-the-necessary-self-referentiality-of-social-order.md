## Summary

This essay argues that all meaning, truth, and legitimacy originate in irreducible founding events that cannot be reduced to prior causes or naturalistic explanation. Language itself—the capacity to refer to absent objects—proves that human emergence was an originary rupture. The author shows how the paradox of naming "God" (where referent and meaning coincide) structures all signification, and that social orders face an inescapable problem: their legitimacy depends entirely on origins, yet origins become contentious and difficult to justify once documented or scrutinized. The essay suggests that dismissing origins in favor of rationalistic critique merely displaces rather than resolves this fundamental problem.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Originary hypothesis]]**: Human language and culture emerged in a single, irreducible event rather than through gradual evolution
- **[[The originary scene]]**: The founding moment where the central object is simultaneously named and transcended, creating the first sign
- **[[Paradox of the sign]]**: The referent is both present (object of appetite) and absent (object of desire), making iterability possible
- **[[God as Name]]**: The word whose meaning and referent are identical; every utterance tacitly refers to this Name
- **[[Declarative sentence]]**: Speech capable of referring to absent objects; creates infinite regress when analyzed philosophically
- **[[Legitimation by origins]]**: The claim that all right, meaning, and authority derive solely from founding events
- **[[Self-referentiality of social order]]**: Social critique itself has origins in disciplinary events; cannot escape the originary problem

## Notable Passages

> "Everything is nothing but the unfolding of its event of origin. To know something is to know its origin; even more, it is to participate in its origin."

> "The paradox of the event replaces the infinite regress a philosopher might find... the 'referent' is both present and absent... this is what makes the sign iterable."

> "There is nothing else. Your critical, rationalistic, moralizing attack on revered origins has its own origins, in another, disciplinary, event."

## Questions Raised

1. If all legitimacy depends on origins and origins are inherently contentious or concealed, what grounds the authority of a social order to enforce norms in the present?

2. How can the paradox of the originary scene—that the central object "is already God" before it is named—be distinguished from circular reasoning or tautology?

3. Does the claim that every critique has its own originary disciplinary foundation make substantive political judgment possible, or does it collapse all claims to equivalent status?
