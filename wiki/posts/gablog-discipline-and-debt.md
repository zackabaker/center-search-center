# Discipline and Debt

## Summary

This essay traces the evolution from reciprocal obligation (the Big Man model) through sacred kingship to monetary systems as increasingly effective hedges against direct accountability. Gans argues that the Big Man's power rests on unrepayable gifts that obligate followers; sacred kingship attempts to make this permanent through cosmogonic mediation. However, as rulers delegate power and develop hedges against cosmic/political reversal, they shift from reciprocal to coercive obligation—most effectively through monetary debt. The introduction of money represents a decline in competence requirements for rulers; those who can't perform directly competently instead create debt systems to obscure accountability.

## Key Concepts

- [[Big Man]]: Archaic leader who achieves power through out-gifting competitors, creating unrepayable debts of obligation
- [[Sacred kingship]]: Attempt to make Big Man occupation of center permanent by mediating between cosmos and community
- [[Center]]: The position of power in social hierarchies, always precarious and requiring constant maintenance
- [[Competence]]: Asymmetrical reciprocal obligation; direct accountability to hierarchical superiors
- [[Hedges]]: Structural insulations from accountability; mechanisms that protect rulers from being "refuted" by circumstances
- [[Debt]]: A coercive obligation enforced monetarily; substitute for reciprocal obligation when competence fails
- [[Money]]: Imperial technology for binding local communities into hierarchical order; marker of insecurity in central authority
- [[Jubilee]]: Periodic debt forgiveness in ancient world; restores competency and clarity in chains of command

## Notable Passages

> "The Big Man renders everyone dependent upon him, entirely for 'merit-based' reasons, and this is a debt which can never be paid back."

> "Those who can't do, issue money and create debts. Of course, then, managing money and debts becomes something one can become competent in."

> "Competence in this field is in that case a marker of the insecurity of the central authority."

## Questions Raised

1. If monetary competence emerges precisely as a marker of central authority insecurity, can we identify historical moments where ruling classes transition from reciprocal to monetary obligation—and what triggers those transitions?

2. What would it mean to restore "competency and clarity" in contemporary chains of command, given that we've now centuries into debt-based systems? Is such restoration conceivable?

3. Does the digital economy represent a new hedge technology beyond money, or simply a more abstract version of the same accountability-avoidance mechanism?
