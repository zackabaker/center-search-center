## Summary

This essay traces how the Axial Age's moral innovations—monotheism, philosophy, justice systems—created an unsolved structural problem: the recognition of a "higher law" above the monarch's authority. The rationalization of sacred kingship into god-imperial rule paradoxically enabled both the abstraction of individuals into anonymous masses and the emergence of individualized justice. The appearance of exemplary victims who embody sacrificial injustice within ostensibly rational systems generates moral revolutions, but leaves unresolved the tension between [[imperium in imperio]] (divided sovereignty) and absolute rule.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Sacral kingship]]**: Rule legitimized by cosmic and ritual connection, where the position itself is secure though its occupant remains vulnerable.
- **[[God-imperial rule]]**: Rationalized sovereignty where the monarch becomes more secure but the sacred function thins, reducing mystical efficacy to instrumental governance.
- **[[Imperium in imperio]]**: Divided sovereignty or competing centers of authority that create structural instability.
- **[[Sacrificial crisis]]**: The tendency of exchange systems to escalate toward human sacrifice as the ultimate commensurate return.
- **[[Victimary logic]]**: The articulation of injustice through the exemplary victim, whose suffering indicts the justice system itself.
- **[[Abstraction]]**: The dispossession of individuals from communal/ritual embeddedness into anonymous economic and legal systems (via coinage, debt, slavery).

## Notable Passages

> "The greater the benefit, the greater the obligation, which means that the sacrificial is always tending towards human sacrifice as its telos."

> "The emergence of exemplary victims of sacrificial injustice would lead to the clarification of this appearance, and its articulation in legal, political and sacral discourses."

> "It is now possible to imagine a law that is 'higher' than the law presided over by the monarch, and therefore a sacrality that supersedes that of the God-Emperor."

## Questions Raised

1. Why have no stable political solutions emerged to the problem of higher law versus absolute sovereignty since the Axial Age? Is this an inherent paradox or a failure of institutional imagination?

2. Does the modern [[victimocracy]] represent a final intensification of the victimary logic born in the Axial Age, or a fundamentally new configuration?

3. Can the "laboratory" referenced in the title—presumably modernity itself—escape the sacrificial structure that enabled both justice systems and their moral critique?
