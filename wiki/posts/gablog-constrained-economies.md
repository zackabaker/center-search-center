## Summary

Markets function not as collections of atomized rational actors but as embedded [[network|networks]] of relationships with varying degrees of trust and affiliation. Rather than switching between vendors based on pure price optimization, individuals remain "always already" bound to webs of social, geographic, and personal ties that constrain and shape economic behavior. The author argues that understanding markets as networks—rather than abstract systems—reframes both why government regulation often fails (it ignores network "tissues") and why the introduction of marketized relationships into established networks represents strategic power struggles between competing networks.

## Key Concepts

- [[Market]] – understood not as price discovery among isolated agents, but as embedded networks of relationship and trust
- [[Network]] – dense webs of social, economic, and affective ties that determine access, behavior, and opportunity
- [[Gift economy]] – kin and gift-based relationships that shade gradually into more abstracted market relations
- [[Power]] – the ability of ascending networks to usurp established ones through marketization and "diversity" disruption
- [[Institution]] – organizational apparatus that either maintains network order or destabilizes it through intervention
- [[Diversity]] – presented here as a deliberate strategy to fragment established networks and remake institutional terrain
- [[Constraint]] – the binding force of existing relationships that prevents the frictionless rational choice model

## Notable Passages

"You are 'always already' tied into a certain set of connections, you have a built in 'bias,' and if you want to make a change it involves wrenching yourself away from all that."

"The introduction of more marketized relationships into more networky ones are just strategies by some up and coming network aiming to usurp power from a more established one."

"The government looks at one part of a dense network, in accord with a narrow purpose...without understanding how all the 'tissues' of the network fit together."

## Questions Raised

1. If all markets are networks, what distinguishes purely extractive or predatory network relationships from mutually beneficial ones—or is that distinction itself an artifact of our [[declarative culture]]?

2. How can we distinguish between legitimate network consolidation and what the text calls "self-destruction" when institutions deliberately fragment established networks?

3. Does the networkization of markets necessarily imply that [[sovereignty]] itself must be rethought as distributed through multiple overlapping networks rather than concentrated in state or corporate entities?
