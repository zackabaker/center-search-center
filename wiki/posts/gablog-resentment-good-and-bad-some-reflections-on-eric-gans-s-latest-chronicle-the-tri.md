## Summary

This essay examines resentment as a fundamental human problem, exploring both quantitative approaches (minimizing total resentment) and qualitative approaches (distinguishing legitimate from illegitimate forms). The author argues that while absolutist solutions—concentrating power in a single undisputed center—may reduce resentment quantitatively, a qualitative approach proves more nuanced. The key insight is that resentments anchored to stable institutional norms (monarchy, property, church) are self-limiting, whereas "unrestricted, unqualified resentment" (resentment at lack of reciprocity in general) escalates infinitely and cannot be addressed.

## Key Concepts

- [[Resentment]] — foundational human passion rooted in mimetic rivalry and the desire for reciprocity; the basis of both legitimation and social disorder
- [[Center]] — the locus of sovereignty and authority; contested centrality generates resentment
- [[Mimetic desire]] — unlimited, envious, rivalrous desire constitutive of the human (after Girard)
- [[Sovereignty]] — undisputed, unified locus of power proposed as the only basis for stable order
- [[Institutional order]] — stable disciplinary frameworks (monarchy, monogamy, property) that channel and limit resentment
- [[Transcendent resentment]] — a higher-order resentment directed at resentment itself; exemplified by the Old Testament God
- [[Reciprocity]] — the standard against which social relations are measured; unrestricted demands for reciprocity fuel escalating resentment

## Notable Passages

> "If all resentment is resentment at another's centrality, the way to eliminate resentment, or, at least reduce it to manageable proportions, would be to establish a single, uncontested, efficient center that no one could resent effectively."

> "Resentments on behalf of some historically established mode of discipline…are intrinsically limited, since resentment of breaches of the institutional norms will subside with the re-secured stability of the institution."

> "The transcendence of resentment would be a transcendent resentment, which does seem a fairly accurate description of the Old Testament God."

## Questions Raised

1. Can we genuinely occupy a "non-resentful position" from which to measure or judge resentment, or is all moral judgment itself a form of transcendent resentment?

2. Is the distinction between quantitative reduction and qualitative channeling of resentment ultimately sustainable, or does channeling resentment through institutions merely displace rather than resolve it?

3. How do we prevent institutionalized resentment (resentment on behalf of established norms) from calcifying into oppressive force?
