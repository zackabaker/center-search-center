## Summary

This post argues that classical liberalism—premised on inviolable individual rights and open debate—has exhausted its historical function. Liberalism succeeded in defeating feudalism, slavery, absolutism, and totalitarianism, but now faces a social order where its foundational assumptions no longer apply. The author observes that contemporary progressivism operates on a fundamentally illiberal logic: that personal freedoms require universal celebration and unanimous support, not merely toleration. With pre-modern alternatives thoroughly routed, liberalism offers no framework for today's actual disagreements, suggesting we have entered a post-liberal era.

## Key Concepts

- [[Liberalism]]: Political tradition grounding rights in pre-political individual inviolability; now historically exhausted
- [[Victimary progressivism]]: Logic demanding not tolerance but universal affirmation and celebration of one's choices
- [[Declarative culture]]: Regime where what is declared true becomes binding on all, replacing liberal debate
- [[Center and periphery]]: Liberalism succeeded when fighting pre-modern (peripheral) forces; fails in modern (centered) disputes
- [[Technocratic governance]]: Post-liberal management addressing symptoms rather than engaging fundamental disagreements
- [[Pre-modernity]]: Feudalism, absolutism, and traditional forms now thoroughly defeated, leaving liberalism without its defining enemy

## Notable Passages

> "I am only allowed to do something only if everyone supports and celebrates my doing it. And that is not, in fact, illogical at all. Only liberalism finds it outrageous."

> "Liberalism's victory in these struggles enabled it to be sold as a set of eternal principles (and to disguise its basic emptiness), but maybe these enemies were very contingent and time bound."

> "Once all the pre-modern forms have been abolished and the more genocidal forms of totalitarian rule discredited, what, exactly, remains of liberalism?"

## Questions Raised

1. If liberalism's enemies were historically contingent rather than eternal, what made its premises seem universal for so long—and how does recognizing this contingency reshape our understanding of rights and freedom?

2. Can any coherent post-liberal political order emerge from victimary progressivism's demand for universal affirmation, or does this logic inevitably collapse into either authoritarianism or exhausted technocracy?

3. What would a genuinely centered (rather than periphery-focused) political tradition look like, and could it address the disagreements liberalism now obscures rather than resolves?
