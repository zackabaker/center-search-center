## Summary

This essay examines Eric Gans's fourfold taxonomy of freedom—[[ostensive freedom|ostensive]], [[imperative freedom|imperative]], [[declarative freedom|declarative]], and [[discursive freedom|discursive]]—arguing that modern political order emerges from the interdependency of imperative and declarative modes. The author proposes that [[ostensive freedom]] serves as the hidden ground enabling imperatives to be checked by declarations, and that public commemoration of imperative "excesses" generates new political centers. Rather than treating imperativity as merely a necessary evil, the essay reframes it as intrinsically bound to responsibility and the capacity for self-governance.

## Key Concepts

- [[Ostensive freedom]]: The freedom to intend an object, generating the capacity to become an object of intention for others
- [[Imperative freedom]]: Freedom from another's intention (negative freedom); the capacity to impose will
- [[Declarative freedom]]: The freedom to help choose the imperatives by which one will be governed
- [[Discursive freedom]]: The capacity to speak about and contest the imperatives governing society
- [[Imperativity]]: The exercise of imperative power, historically checked through ritual, gift-exchange, and modern deliberation
- [[Personal artworks]]: Self-constituting objects of attention that others may likewise appreciate and encourage
- [[Founding events]]: Public commemorations of imperative excess that generate new political spaces and centers

## Notable Passages

"The freedom to intend an object in principle generates for each member of society the discursive freedom to become an object of intention."

"Modern politics emerges as a result of a series of events in which the 'excesses' of imperativity have been witnessed, recorded and commemorated publically."

"Imperativity is certainly associable with responsibility, but also in the more 'positive' sense in which its limits can be strictly set in terms of what one must have complete or partial control over."

## Questions Raised

1. What is the precise mechanism by which declaratives actually check imperativity in practice? Does the author adequately answer whether declaratives possess "inherent checking power," or does the answer necessarily depend on ostensive freedom's operation?

2. How does the distinction between imperativity as "necessary evil" versus as positively bound to responsibility change our understanding of authority and obligation in modern democracies?

3. Can founding events—even "trivial" ones like the Boston Massacre—truly generate new public centers, or does the essay's framework require a more rigorous account of what constitutes a genuinely transformative political commemoration?
