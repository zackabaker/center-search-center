## Summary

This post explores how Judaism's founding principle—human equality grounded in creation in God's image—establishes a paradox: a universal truth proclaimed only by a small, particular people. The author traces how God's direct presence to humanity produces violent emulation and fails, necessitating [[covenant]] and [[law]] as means of maintaining [[egalitarianism]] while preserving distance between divine and human. The essay questions whether Judaism, Christianity, or some future model can adequately resolve the tension between universal equality and the particular people chosen to represent it.

## Key Concepts

- [[Originary scene]] – The foundational moment of human meaning-making and collective order
- [[Covenant]] – God's binding agreement with a people, establishing law as mediator between divine and human
- [[Egalitarianism]] – The principle that all humans possess equal dignity as bearers of God's image
- [[Divinity]] – The problem of human beings divinizing themselves (Big Man, patriarch, emperor) versus God's transcendence
- [[Law]] – The public, shared code that enables humans to live together without violence or tyrannical emulation
- [[Noachide code]] – The seven laws given to Noah as a universal, minimal framework for human covenanting
- [[Presence]] – God's immediate availability to humans, which paradoxically generates murderous envy and disorder

## Notable Passages

> "God cannot walk among men: His presence becomes the source of murderous envy, and tyrannical and mob-like emulation, until God is finally utterly repudiated."

> "The universal principle of human equality under God can only be proclaimed and instantiated in a single, small, weak people, in the midst of imperial orders predicated upon man creating himself as God."

> "The Jews would still be at the center of this monotheistic world… any success they enjoyed would be taken as a sign that they were still god's privileged children."

## Questions Raised

1. Can Judaism's model of universal equality through particular law-observance actually escape the anti-Semitic trap of being perpetually central and responsible—or is this structural inescapability inherent to any monotheistic framework?

2. If Christianity "re-divinized" humanity (through incarnation) and thus reopened the God-man distance problem, what would a third model look like that is sufficiently "originary"?

3. Does the author's invocation of "egalitarian ancestors" suggest that pre-monotheistic or pre-covenantal human orders possessed resources for thinking equality that later systems obscured?
