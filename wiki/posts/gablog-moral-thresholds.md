## Summary

Morality, understood through generative anthropology, is fundamentally about maintaining and extending [[linguistic presence]] across scales of human interaction. Rather than appealing to liberal abstractions like dignity or equality, a GA-based moral theory examines how successive linguistic forms—ostensive, imperative, interrogative, declarative—structure action and attention. All action can be read as ostensive sign-making that throws off imperatives requiring response; moral behavior emerges when one finds ways to sustain the [[center]] and its compelling presence even when obedience fails. The task is perpetually deferring linguistic collapse through recursive iteration of founding structures.

## Key Concepts

- [[Linguistic presence]] — The sustained attention and communicative coherence that makes social meaning possible
- [[Ostensive sign]] — Any action emitted in response to perceived crisis; the iconic, foundational linguistic form
- [[Imperative exchange]] — The reciprocal demand-and-obedience structure linking desirer to center
- [[Center]] — The locus of compelling attention that actions orbit and either confirm or destabilize
- [[Originary iteration]] — The recursive, non-linear repetition of founding structures at increasing complexity
- [[Declarative sentence]] — The linguistic form that settles contested centers by conveying the imperative grounding an action
- [[Linguistic collapse]] — The dissolution of shared presence; the ultimate moral danger to defer

## Notable Passages

"If morality entails maintaining linguistic presence, then a further exploration of morality in these terms would look into the strengthening and extension of linguistic presence."

"All desires can be constructed as imperatives directed to some center, a center from which the desirer has or will receive corresponding imperatives upon obedience to which compliance with the petitioner's request will be contingent."

"If the other disobeys, linguistic presence is put in danger, and the moral thing to do is find some other way of maintaining it."

## Questions Raised

1. How does one distinguish morally between different strategies for restoring linguistic presence after disobedience—when is the "new center" a genuine moral response versus mere strategic repositioning?

2. If all action is ostensive sign-making, does this framework risk collapsing moral agency into mere causality, or does the iterative structure of imperatives and declaratives preserve meaningful human choice?

3. How does this account of moral thresholds apply to large-scale institutional or civilizational crises where the center itself becomes radically contested across incompatible linguistic communities?
