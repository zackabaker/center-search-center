## Summary

This post reconnects the long-abandoned problem of [[originary grammar]]—the project of dissolving any utterance into its ostensive, imperative, interrogative, and declarative components—with contemporary media and technology. Gans proposes that articulating originary grammar is fundamentally a computational and technological problem, one that requires mapping hierarchically organized imperatives across different [[media]] scenes. Drawing on [[David Olson]]'s literacy model, he argues that all media work by creating [[potential ostensives]] to supplement absent scenes, and that this supplementary function is itself grammatically structured. The insight reframes media not as representations of a non-mediated baseline, but as systems generating virtual presence within an environment where each medium relates to all others.

## Key Concepts

- [[Originary Grammar]] — The articulation of any utterance into ostensive, imperative, interrogative, and declarative modes
- [[Declarative Sentence]] — Creates virtual or potential ostensives to supplement blocked desire or imperatives
- [[Potential Ostensive]] — A virtual object created by language to make absent parties seem present on the same scene
- [[Imperative Hierarchy]] — The deferred chain of commands organized through the creation of shared referents
- [[Media as Supplementarity]] — Media supplement what they cannot supply—absent scenes, missing mimetic cues, blocked presence
- [[Scenic Stack]] — The layered media environments (conversation, film, social media, blockchain) that enable utterance
- [[Mimetic Clothing]] — The non-verbal, embodied dimension available only in face-to-face encounter
- [[Supplemental Speech]] — Even originary speech supplements blocked desire, making all communication fundamentally supplementary

## Questions Raised

1. How would a computational mapping of originary grammar across contemporary media actually function? What would it look like to expose the imperative chains embedded in a social media feed or algorithmic feed?

2. If all speech—even "originary" face-to-face speech—is supplementary to blocked desire, what changes about how we think the originary scene itself? Does this dissolve the boundary between origin and mediation?

3. How do different media scenes (livestream vs. printed text vs. blockchain) create different grammatical forms for positioning disparate people as if they occupy the same scene?
