## Summary

This essay traces the historical movement from [[ostensive]] and [[imperative]] signs toward the [[declarative]], showing how digital civilization represents a qualitative shift in our relationship to ostensivity itself. Rather than merely automating existing practices, digitization has relocated vast domains of social interaction—from driving to justice—into algorithmic systems that bypass traditional human deliberation. The author argues that while we gain factual precision through DNA testing and surveillance, we paradoxically lose the meaningful human judgment that once accompanied evidence-gathering, replacing it with probabilistic prediction divorced from guilt or innocence.

## Key Concepts

- [[Ostensive]]: The direct pointing gesture; the originary sign anchoring all human meaning
- [[Declarative]]: Language that makes statements about possible worlds rather than pointing at immediate objects
- [[Imperative]]: The demand or command that initiates the sign exchange
- [[Declarative culture]]: Civilization organized around complex linguistic statements rather than material objects
- [[Semiotic history]]: The account of how human signs evolve from primitive to complex forms
- [[Algorithmic governance]]: Rule by statistical pattern-matching rather than case-by-case judgment
- [[Ostensivity]]: The quality of being available for pointing; directness of reference

## Notable Passages

> "A semiotic history of the world would trace the gradual process of distancing from the ostensive and imperative via the declarative, along with the efforts, within new forms of declarative culture, to re-embed ostensives and imperatives."

> "To understand a sentence is to understand the probability of one possible world amongst a range of other possible worlds, and to be capable of identifying sufficient distinctive features of that world."

> "If we once moved from barbaric means of determining guilt by 'trial by ordeal' to the method we have now...we are now likely to move to the use of algorithms to search and analyze the official records of anyone."

## Questions Raised

1. If algorithmic prediction becomes perfectly accurate at forecasting behavior, does the concept of human responsibility or [[agency]] become obsolete—and what replaces the moral framework that depends on it?

2. How can we preserve meaningful [[ostensive]] anchors (moments of shared recognition) in a civilization increasingly mediated by probabilistic systems that eliminate the need for human judgment?

3. Does digital mediation represent genuine progress over older forms of justice and governance, or merely the concealment of the same power dynamics under a veneer of mathematical objectivity?
