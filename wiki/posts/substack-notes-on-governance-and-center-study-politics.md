## Summary

This post develops a minimal theory of obedience and disobedience by framing loyalty as a bet on the continuance of the [[pointman]] versus the emergence of an [[outside option]]. The author argues that degrees of compliance constitute calculated hedges between existing and prospective sovereigns, complicated by non-utilitarian factors like [[credit]], dignity, and professionalism. Center Study politics uniquely proposes collapsing the oscillation between central bank and central intelligence by internalizing all power through [[succession practices]], thereby eliminating the outside spread entirely.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Pointman]]**: The sovereign or center figure whose hold on power is constantly measured by subordinate compliance patterns.
- **[[Outside Option]]**: A prospective replacement regime; the possibility of regime change shapes present obedience.
- **[[Credit]]**: A non-reducible measure of credibility and trustworthiness that transfers across regimes, particularly for high-ranking officials.
- **[[Succession Practices]]**: Formalized rotation of power through internal mechanisms rather than external revolution.
- **[[Outside Spread]]**: The gap between competing power centers (central bank vs. central intelligence) that enables modern oscillation.
- **[[Sunk Capital]]**: Investment in the existing regime that complicates switching loyalty to a successor.

## Notable Passages

> "Complete and devoted obedience would indicate no possibility (whether it be desire for or fear of) an outside option; complete disobedience, meanwhile, indicates reliance upon and proactive obedience toward, some outside option."

> "Credibility irreducible to the likelihood of predicting one's next action will be more important the higher up the hierarchy one goes."

> "Center study implies what I think is a unique politics: placing all the bets on a center or prospective or type of pointman so inside as to turn the central bank and central intelligence into a single entity, thereby abolishing both."

## Questions Raised

1. How can a center study politics practically internalize the outside option through succession without simply recreating the oscillation it claims to transcend?

2. What distinguishes the "credit" that transfers across regimes from mere reputation or fear-based compliance—and is it actually available to those with genuine ideological commitments?

3. If all modern investment is ultimately a bet on the amplitude of credit-wrecking within power rotation, does this theory adequately account for transformative moments when the outside spread genuinely collapses?
