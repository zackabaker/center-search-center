## Summary

This text argues that GA must place [[the center]] back at the center of analysis, rejecting Gans's liberal humanist vision of omnicentrism where no social center exists. The author contends that understanding how power actually maintains centrality—through what he calls "singularized succession in perpetuity"—allows us to make implicit mechanisms of governance explicit and thereby transform them. The neglect of the state and technology in orthodox GA flows directly from this foundational aporia: if there is no center, there is nothing structural to say about either. Restoring the center as the organizing problem of human civilization enables permanent critique without nostalgic retreat.

## Key Concepts

- [[The Center]] – The locus of social consensus and power; what post-sacral societies have failed to establish satisfactorily
- [[Singularized Succession in Perpetuity]] – The implicit mechanism by which power maintains itself through controlled leadership transitions
- [[Post-Sacral Governance]] – The problem of legitimacy and order after the breakdown of sacred kingship; includes state and technology as central problems
- [[Omnicentrism]] – Gans's vision of liberal society where each individual is a center; masks the reality of actual centralization
- [[Aporia]] – The theoretical blockage that prevents recognition of structural power and the state in orthodox GA
- [[Making the Implicit Explicit]] – The method of understanding existing conditions so as to make transformation possible

## Notable Passages

> "The more thorough the study of existing conditions, the more visible and intelligible the way to transform them."

> "We must have a center, but we've never had a really satisfactory one: keeping this in mind enables us to be unremittingly critical of the existing order without nostalgia for any earlier one."

> "If you can't say anything about post-sacral governance, technology can only be smart people inventing things that make life easier but can be 'misused.' It's not really part of the human."

## Questions Raised

1. If the center is the fundamental human problem, what would a "satisfactory" center look like in contemporary conditions—one that avoids both the vulnerability of egalitarian consensus and the cynicism bred by sacral kingship?

2. How does technology relate to the maintenance or destabilization of the center in post-sacral societies, and what role should it play in a rethought GA framework?

3. Can "singularized succession in perpetuity" operate transparently (made fully explicit) without collapsing the very mechanisms it describes, or is some degree of implicit operation necessary to any center's stability?
