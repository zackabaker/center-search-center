## Summary

This post analyzes Barack Obama's Cairo speech through the lens of his rhetorical habit of constructing [[symmetries]] across political and cultural divides. The author argues that Obama's discourse systematically saturates contested issues with balanced equivalences—ranging from sensible to provocative to obscene—as a strategy for establishing a shared representational ground. Rather than adjudicating historical truth, Obama's symmetries function as [[declarative culture|declarative]] gestures that invite both sides to "sign on" to a provisional framework, deferring factual disputes in favor of pragmatic mutual commitment.

## Key Concepts

- [[Symmetry]] — A rhetorical strategy of constructing balanced equivalences between opposed positions to enable negotiation and representation.
- [[Declarative culture]] — The establishment of shared frameworks through speech acts rather than prior agreement on facts or values.
- [[Originary scene]] — The foundational moment of human meaning-making; used here to analyze how representation supersedes historical causation.
- [[Representation]] — The act of bringing an issue into proper form so it can be addressed; requires saturation with symmetries.
- [[Modernity]] — The contested site of Western imposition and Muslim ambivalence; central to Obama's Cairo negotiations.
- [[Cold War]] — Historical framing device used to establish temporal equivalence between colonial and contemporary grievances.

## Notable Passages

> "What is certainly most interesting about Obama's rhetoric is his sense no issue has been properly represented until it has been saturated with symmetries."

> "If we set aside all questions of truth and fairness, and just think in originary terms... we must judge them as follows: can acknowledged representatives of both 'sides' sign onto these provisions as a starting point?"

> "Their truth need not be determined until after we have tried to live up to them."

## Questions Raised

1. Does the deferral of truth-claims in favor of pragmatic symmetry ultimately strengthen or weaken political commitment, and at what point do asymmetries reassert themselves?

2. How does the originary understanding of representation—where the sign supersedes causation—account for genuine power differentials and material consequences that resist rhetorical framing?

3. What happens when one party refuses to "sign on" to the symmetry, and how does that refusal itself become representable within this framework?
