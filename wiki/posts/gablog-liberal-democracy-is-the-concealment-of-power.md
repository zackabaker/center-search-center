## Summary

This post argues that "liberal democracy" is an incoherent and ultimately hollow concept that masks the exercise of arbitrary [[power]]. The author contends that guaranteeing vague abstract rights inevitably generates exceptions, meaning citizens possess only those rights governments don't find urgent to violate. Recent electoral battles—from 2016 onward—reveal that even the procedural legitimacy of voting itself has become a contested political arena, exposing the absence of any real limits on governmental power.

## Key Concepts

- [[Liberal Democracy]]: A claimed system of limited government and guaranteed rights plus elections; in practice, a fiction masking power's arbitrary exercise
- [[Power]]: The capacity to determine which rights will be enforced and which violated based on perceived urgency
- [[Vague Abstract Rights]]: Rights formulated so broadly they necessarily generate exceptions and are only secure during periods of elite stability
- [[Electoral Legitimacy]]: The procedural authority granted by voting; now itself contested and weaponized as a political battleground
- [[Declarative Culture]]: The sphere where systems are announced and justified (here: democracy's self-presentation vs. reality)
- [[Victimary Progressivism]]: The left's use of scapegoating and delegitimation (e.g., "Russians hacked the election") to mobilize power

## Questions Raised

1. If liberal democracy's legitimacy depends entirely on rotating elites' willingness to accept electoral results, what replaces it once that consensus breaks down—and do we already see that breakdown?

2. Can a [[declarative]] system (one that announces its values) survive long-term when its practice systematically contradicts its proclamations, or does the contradiction eventually become conscious and collapse the system?

3. How does the transformation of electoral mechanics themselves into a "contested arena" differ fundamentally from pre-democratic systems of power transfer, and does it constitute a genuine epochal shift?
