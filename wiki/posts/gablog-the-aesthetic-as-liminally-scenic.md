## Summary

The aesthetic in GA emerges from oscillation between attention to the [[sign]] and the central object on the [[originary scene]]. Crucially, art only becomes possible after the [[sacred center]] loses its monopoly on communal attention—the aesthetic supplements and demonstrates the provisionality of centrality itself. By exposing the practices that construct shared centers, art enacts deritualization, and the history of aesthetics parallels the increasing uncertainty about who/what can occupy the center. The permeable boundary between artwork and audience reflects this shared inquiry into the consequences of centering.

## Key Concepts

- [[Originary scene]]: The founding moment of human culture where attention oscillates between sign and central object
- [[Sacred center]]: The unified focal point of communal attention that art displaces and demystifies
- [[Aesthetic]]: Pre-sacral oscillation that supplements the center by demonstrating its provisional nature
- [[Deritualization]]: Art's practice of exposing the preliminary ritualizing practices that construct centrality
- [[Sign]]: The ostensive gesture pointing away from the central object, foundational to language
- [[Classical aesthetic]]: Direct, unquestioned centering (Greek tragedy); audience extension of the scene
- [[Neoclassical aesthetic]]: Self-aware staging of the scene within itself (Shakespeare); audience represented within
- [[Kitsch]]: Cynical simulation of the sacral that art explicitly refuses

## Notable Passages

"The aesthetic, in that case, supplements the center, first of all by providing something 'like' a rite both in its own structure and for those participating as spectators."

"If art is a practice of deritualization by exposing the preliminary practices of ritualization...then it makes sense that the boundary between art work and public would itself become more visible and permeable."

"The resistance to figural art and straightforward representations of beauty...is that they are cynical attempts to simulate the sacral scene, which is precisely what art does not and cannot do."

## Questions Raised

1. If art necessarily demystifies centrality, can there be genuinely "sacred" art after the loss of monopoly, or only art *about* the sacred's erosion?

2. How does the increasing permeability of the artwork-audience boundary reshape the artist's relation to the [[Romantic]] gesture of scapegoated resistance?

3. Does digital/participatory art fundamentally alter the deritualization process, or merely accelerate the visibility of boundaries already at work?
