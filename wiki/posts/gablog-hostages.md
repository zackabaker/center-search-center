## Summary

This essay applies originary analysis to Israel's 2006 campaign to secure the release of captured soldier Gilad Shalit, identifying a puzzling mismatch between military means and political ends. The author argues that Israel's large-scale military operation—neither a targeted rescue nor a full invasion—represents an attempt to use overwhelming force as intimidation rather than as a solution to the hostage crisis itself. This approach reveals how [[white guilt]] constrains contemporary sovereigns from pursuing classical imperialist logic while simultaneously preventing them from accepting the asymmetrical costs of modern asymmetrical conflict.

## Key Concepts

- **[[White Guilt]]** — The post-Auschwitz, post-Hiroshima political paradigm that makes unrestrained force morally illegitimate for Western democracies
- **[[Asymmetry]]** — The structural imbalance where weak actors (terrorists, non-state groups) exploit the moral constraints binding strong actors
- **[[Originary Analysis]]** — Method of interpreting historical events through GA's scenic and resentment-based frameworks
- **[[Sovereignty]]** — The capacity to determine one's own course of action without external constraint; here, compromised by guilt
- **[[Means and Ends]]** — The strategic mismatch when military force exceeds what is necessary to achieve a specific political objective
- **[[Intimidation as Policy]]** — Using superior force to coerce compliance rather than to achieve territorial or existential goals

## Questions Raised

1. Can a sovereign actor ever fully escape the constraints of white guilt once they have been culturally internalized, or does this represent a permanent structural change to how Western power operates?
