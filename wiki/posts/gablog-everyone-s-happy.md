## Summary

This post analyzes the curious political consensus that emerged around Obama's centrist cabinet appointments, wherein both left and right claim victory while potentially supporting identical policies. The author argues that Bush-hatred may have functioned as pure [[scapegoating]] rather than substantive political disagreement, with the "content" of opposition reducing to ritual denunciation of Iraq rather than genuine policy alternatives. If this reading holds, politics has become primarily a matter of [[representation]] and [[declarative culture]]—who gets to claim moral authority—rather than actual governance.

## Key Concepts

- [[Scapegoating]] – the mechanism by which a community achieves temporary unity through collective blame of an arbitrary or overdetermined target
- [[Representation]] – the claim to speak for or embody a constituency, increasingly divorced from policy content
- [[Declarative culture]] – the assertion of meaning through speech acts and symbolic positioning rather than material action
- [[Victimary politics]] – the mobilization of moral authority through claims of injury and historical wrong
- [[Center]] – the locus of decision-making and legitimate authority that both sides compete to occupy
- [[Verbal commitment]] – the gap between ritualized speech and actual behavioral intention

## Notable Passages

> "If Obama's transcendent political persona is predicated upon a messianic negation of the scapegoated Bush, then it might actually be that policy and political realities have become irrelevant."

> "The differences concerned little more than details of implementation, and even those couldn't be clarified since they regarded an unknown future... It seemed to come down to nothing more than a mere verbal commitment to the claim that the invasion of Iraq was the worst foreign policy disaster ever."

> "The question comes down to representation: on one side, a battered, tainted, tiresome administration, on the other side a fresh face."

## Questions Raised

1. Can [[scapegoating]] ever be purely arbitrary, or does the target always carry some meaningful content—even if that content is subsequently obscured or mythologized?

2. At what point does the dominance of [[declarative culture]] in politics make actual policy outcomes irrelevant, and what prevents this from becoming total?

3. If both sides can claim victory through different narratives about identical policies, what mechanism could restore politics to substantive disagreement over real alternatives?
