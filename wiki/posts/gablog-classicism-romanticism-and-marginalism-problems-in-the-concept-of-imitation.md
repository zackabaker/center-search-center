## Summary

This post develops a genealogy of three orientations toward [[model|models]] and imitation in cultural life. [[Classicism]] demands adherence to the exemplary model; [[Romanticism]] reflexively rejects all models as tyrannical constraints; [[Marginalism]] resolves this antinomy by treating the model as an inexhaustible source of [[rule|rules]] that directs attention toward [[object|objects]] of desire rather than toward itself. The tension between finite objects and intangible values creates an inevitable inversion in civilized orders: models must embody abstract norms while pointing beyond themselves to invisible legitimating sources. This framework illuminates contemporary left-wing pedagogies that provide unambiguous models, enemies, and imperatives.

## Key Concepts

- [[Classicism]]: The imperative to refer work to the best model, anchored in permanent human excellence and divine order
- [[Romanticism]]: The compulsive rejection of all particular models as arbitrary constraints on experience and discovery
- [[Marginalism]]: A middle position that treats models as instituting inexhaustible [[rule|rules]] while keeping [[object|objects]] central
- [[Mimetic desire]]: The circular process by which one model supplants another by offering access to more attractive objects
- [[Intangible object]]: Goods like honor, goodness, respect, and divine blessing that cannot be possessed but only embodied through models
- [[Cultural pedagogy]]: The necessary inversion where models direct attention from themselves toward abstract norms they supposedly embody
- [[Self-presentation]]: The ritualized modes of comportment and speech that models enforce through social approval

## Notable Passages

> "They tell you exactly what you have to say on every question, they model the appropriate modes of self-presentation in great detail, they provide a pantheon of heroes, they tell you exactly who the enemy is."

## Questions Raised

1. How can a civilization maintain the pedagogical inversion (where models point beyond themselves) without collapsing into either slavish imitation of the model or cynical rejection of all models?

2. What distinguishes healthy [[marginalism|marginalist]] imitation—where models institute rules—from the "unproblematic self-confidence" of contemporary cults of the Left that the author criticizes?

3. Can [[intangible object|intangible objects]] like God's blessing be transmitted through [[model|models]] at all, or does the attempt necessarily corrupt both the model and the object it purports to serve?
