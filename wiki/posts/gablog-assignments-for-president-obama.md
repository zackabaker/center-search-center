## Summary

This essay analyzes Obama's political rise as fundamentally rooted in his role as the [[scapegoat|anti-Bush]] figure—a transcendent political celebrity whose quasi-divine status derives from completing the public ritual of scapegoating George W. Bush. The author argues that Obama's studied distance from the radical Left and tentative overtures to conservatives mask a deeper strategy: using incremental policy shifts and international legal frameworks to gradually transform American governance into a "bureaucratic, quasi-feudal order" based on transnational law. The essay proposes that progressive actors function as sophisticated anthropologists, strategically deploying events and transitional figures to reframe national interests within an emerging international human rights apparatus.

## Key Concepts

- [[Scapegoating]]: The public ritual mechanism through which Obama's transcendence is constituted by opposition to Bush
- [[Transcendence]]: Political celebrity status cultivated and carefully preserved for strategic deployment
- [[Victimary progressivism]]: Postmodern internationalism leveraging human rights discourse to shift power toward unaccountable bureaucracies
- [[Declarative culture]]: The "proper lighting" or reframing of actions within legitimating international legal frameworks
- [[Transnational bureaucracy]]: Supranational institutions (war crimes tribunals, human rights forums) that supersede national sovereignty
- [[Transitional figures]]: Dissident ancien régime actors used temporarily then discarded in systemic transformation

## Notable Passages

> "Nobody speaks about Obama without an explicit or implicit gesture toward Bush ('we finally have a President who…'). Such an analysis sufficed to understand Obama's rise."

> "If you want to shift more power in society to unaccountable bureaucracies... you cannot try to install such an order all at one time. You must pay token homage to the reality in front of you."

> "One works with events, events which make visible the boundary line between nationalistic, bourgeois, imperialistic motives and actions, and the legitimating frame which now christens those actions anew."

## Questions Raised

1. How does the [[scapegoating]] mechanism sustain itself once the scapegoated figure (Bush) exits office—what redirects the [[transcendence]] that was constituted through opposition?

2. Can transnational progressive actors genuinely function as "better anthropologists" if their own framework (postmodern international law) obscures the [[originary]] scene of political order?

3. If [[declarative culture]] succeeds in reframing national action through international legal legitimation, what prevents competing transnational powers from weaponizing the same apparatus against American interests?
