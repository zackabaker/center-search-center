# The Rhythm of Civilization

## Summary

Civilization emerges as a break from the [[hostage-taking]] logic that characterizes barbarism, enabling third-party [[adjudication]] and shared procedural order. The transition requires a sovereign power strong enough to suppress rivals and impose peace, but genuine neutralization of resentment demands more than fear—it requires a [[sacred interdiction]] (like monotheistic revelation) that internalizes restraint as divine law rather than mere command. The deepening discovery of hidden forms of hostage-taking in ritual, politics, and family life suggests civilization is perpetually unfolding, with vanguards of sacred reform continually extending the interdiction against coercion.

## Key Concepts

- [[Hostage-taking]]: The one-on-one threat logic binding barbarism; holding what the other cherishes to enforce compliance
- [[Sacred interdiction]]: Divine prohibition on coercion that internalizes restraint as moral law rather than terror
- [[Sovereignty]]: The emergent third-party power that establishes pre-eminence and institutes procedural order
- [[Adjudication]]: Agreement to let a neutral authority resolve disputes, requiring belief in shared substance and space
- [[Impartial spectator]]: Adam Smith's concept of internalized fairness; the "third person" each subject carries
- [[Procedural order]]: Systems of fairness and ritual that replace simple repression with legitimate governance
- [[Vanguard of the sacred]]: Those who propagate and enforce evolving interdictions against coercion

## Notable Passages

"Hostage-taking is radically one-on-one—something dear to you is held by the other, who is ready to destroy it."

"Only obedience to a sacred imperative, to a divine interdiction on hostage-taking, can account for that sense of fairness."

"When this humble blogger hypothesizes the end of our civilization, is he really saying anything else [than 'Vengeance is mine, sayeth the Lord']?"

## Questions Raised

1. Does every civilization require an explicit [[sacred interdiction]] (religious or philosophical), or can procedural fairness alone sustain order absent the internal "impartial spectator"?

2. If civilization perpetually discovers new forms of hidden [[hostage-taking]], what prevents this recursive process from collapsing into infinite resentment rather than progressive reform?

3. What conditions determine whether a [[vanguard of the sacred]] successfully propagates new interdictions, versus being rejected by communities whose sacred orders remain stable?
