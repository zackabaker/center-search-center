# Incorporation

## Summary

This post explores the radical reframing of government as a [[corporation]] chartered by sovereign authority rather than a contractual entity. Drawing on David Ciepley's historical argument, the author shows how the U.S. Constitution was deliberately designed as a corporate charter limiting government powers to those enumerated—a model the Founders understood perfectly well, contrary to liberal mythology about "social contracts." The paradox emerges: if government is a chartered corporation, who grants the charter? The answer—that "the people" must be sovereign—smuggles popular sovereignty back in as a pure negating force, a cipher without positive power, creating the structural tension of [[imperium in imperio]].

## Key Concepts

- **[[Corporation]]** – A public-private institution established by sovereign authority with enumerated powers, not a contractual arrangement between individuals
- **[[Charter]]** – A foundational document that limits and defines institutional powers; the Founders used "charter" and "constitution" interchangeably
- **[[Popular Sovereignty]]** – The retroactive claim that "the people" constitute the sovereign authority; functionally a negating cipher
- **[[Imperium in Imperio]]** – Power within power; the structural paradox of sovereignty delegating authority while remaining the ultimate negator
- **[[Sovereign]]** – The original source of legitimacy and basis for criticizing governmental acts; cannot itself govern directly
- **[[Social Contract Theory]]** – The liberal myth the Founders rejected, though it sneaks back in through the legitimation problem
- **[[Enumerated Powers]]** – The specific authorities granted to a chartered government; all others revert to the sovereign

## Notable Passages

> "They were trying to establish a charter for a government, a corporate 'person,' that, like a corporation, would have powers limited to those enumerated in the charter."

> "If the government was a chartered corporation, who chartered it? Corporations are chartered by the sovereign—but the sovereign, the British Parliament, had just been overthrown."

> "Popular sovereignty is essentially a cipher—the sovereign is the original source of legitimacy...but can't actually do anything. It's pure negation."

## Questions Raised

1. If popular sovereignty functions only as negation (rejecting violations of the charter), what positive constitutional act could ever legitimately establish new fundamental orders without resorting to the very social contract theory the Founders rejected?

2. Does the corporate model of government—with enumerated powers and no direct democratic participation in governance—actually address the legitimacy crisis, or merely defer it to an ever-receding "people"?

3. How does this chartering logic apply to the contemporary crisis of [[sovereignty]] when governments claim powers far beyond their enumerated scope?
