## Summary

The collapse of ritual orders means models and aspirants no longer fit harmoniously; modern culture emerges from this fundamental [[mistakenness]]. The secular disciplines arose as attempts to make explicit—in purely human terms—how we have gone wrong. Anomalies within sacred systems (unincorporable groups like plebeians) forced the creation of secular terminology defined through opposition and conflict. GA inherits this abstractive tradition but aims toward renewed [[naming]] practices that acknowledge what remains nameless in sacred orders.

## Key Concepts

- [[Ritual order]] – The pre-modern systems where models, initiatory practices, and social membership formed seamless wholes
- [[Mistakenness]] – The fundamental condition of modern culture; the misfit between available models and aspirants seeking membership
- [[Center]] – The originary locus from which all names and procedures are generated in stable communities
- [[Secular disciplines]] – Metalanguages of literacy that diagnose human alienation by naming what cannot fit the sacred system
- [[Anomaly]] – Groups or figures that cannot be incorporated into the sacred structure (plebeians, younger sons) and thus generate resentment
- [[Naming]] – The core practice GA seeks to renew; pointing toward minimal, post-abstract modes of designation
- [[Mimesis]], [[desire]], [[resentment]] – GA's secular terms for what remains nameless within any sacred order

## Notable Passages

> "All of modern culture is a result of the misfit of models and aspirants, which is to say our fundamental mistakenness."

> "All secular social scientific terms are essentially versions of 'that which has been expelled from the center and takes on its meaning through antagonism to other expelled non-members.'"

> "GA's ambition is to do what can only be done through the secular disciplines—point the way to a renewed practice of naming."

## Questions Raised

1. If all secular terminology emerges from expelled anomalies defined in opposition, can GA itself escape this antagonistic structure, or does it merely make the problem more explicit?

2. What would "renewed naming practices" actually look like—more minimal and de-mythicized than current secular language, yet avoiding return to sacred closure?

3. How do we distinguish between necessary abstractions that enable diagnosis of a "shattered system" and those that reinforce the very conflicts they purport to solve?
