## Summary

Political movements craft detailed doctrines and programs ostensibly to clarify their aims, but these canonical models actually serve to manage internal power conflicts by providing grounds to accuse leaders of betraying "the real project." The author argues that this problem stems from **[[declarative culture]]**—a mode of thought that privileges logical consistency and grammatical relations while banishing the [[imperative]] and [[ostensive]] that precede and exceed doctrine. Escaping this trap requires recognizing that power, not procedure, determines outcomes, and that the insider who understands actual power dynamics will always outmaneuver the doctrinal purist.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Declarative Culture]]**: A system of meaning predicated on logical clarity, dictionary-established word usage, and grammatical relations, which attempts to banish imperatives and ostensives.
- **[[Imperative]]**: Commands and obligations that cannot be derived from declarative logic alone; essential to actual governance but erased by declarative frameworks.
- **[[Ostensive]]**: Pointing, gesture, and presence—the originary means of designation that declarative culture systematically suppresses.
- **[[Political Doctrine]]**: A written canonical model designed to manage internal movement conflict by claiming fidelity to "true" principles.
- **[[Absolutism]]**: Rule based on personal authority and non-procedural decision-making, which avoids the trap of doctrinal contradiction.
- **[[Power Dynamics]]**: The actual mechanisms of control that supersede formal programs; the insider's knowledge of these determines political victory.

## Notable Passages

> "Once a political project has a canonical model, filled with procedures, organization structures, required policies, and so on, it also has a permanent basis for political conflict based on the claim that the actual leadership is not in conformity with the 'real' project."

> "The insider who knows where all the pieces are will generally win out over the one who has best mastered the doctrine and program."

> "Once you have banished imperatives (in particular, because if the imperatives go, the ostensives go with them), you are wiping the slate clean and setting all prior obligations, commitments and loyalties aside."

## Questions Raised

1. If declarative culture inevitably creates space for doctrinal conflict, can any large-scale political movement escape this trap, or is the conflict itself the price of coordination?

2. How does the banishment of the [[ostensive]] in favor of pure declarative logic alter the phenomenology of political commitment—what are we losing when we move from presence to procedure?

3. Is the recognition that power, not doctrine, determines outcomes sufficient grounds for a post-declarative politics, or does that recognition itself become another doctrine?
