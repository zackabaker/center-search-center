## Summary

This essay diagnoses liberalism's paradox: it projects the "denuded individual" as foundational, yet that individual is liberalism's own product. Drawing on Gans and Graeber, the author argues that individualism requires perpetual negation of institutional dependencies—a "negative gnostic theology" that feeds a market economy of hostage-taking rather than exchange. The path forward requires [[absolutism]] to replace sacrificial order with a [[concentrative process]] that clarifies responsibilities and eliminates abstract targets for victimary scapegoating.

## Key Concepts

- [[Individualism]]: Self-definition through perpetual mutiny against one's institutional dependencies; a negative identity requiring constant negation
- [[Market economy]]: A system of "hostage exchange" where human selves circulate as commodities, disguising violence through habituation
- [[Constitutive hypocrisy of romanticism]]: Gans's term for the "anti-social" self-representation that paradoxically enables market participation
- [[Absolutism]]: Political order rejecting divided power (imperium in imperio), assigning clear responsibilities without interference
- [[Concentrative process]]: Alternative to liberal abstraction; organizing society through defined orders and explicit accountability
- [[Sacral kingship]]: Ancient [[sacrifice]]-based order now defunct, leaving absolutism to solve recentering without sacrificial victims
- [[Onomastician]]: One who names; the sovereign as chief namer, determining what and whom circulate within the social order

## Notable Passages

> "To be an individual is to be in a perpetual state of mutiny against whatever form of order most directly threatens to define one."

> "What if we were actually to define ourselves constantly, indiscriminately, against every social dependency—friends, families, colleagues, acquaintances, etc.? Such behavior would be psychopathic."

> "The central problem for absolutism is a non-sacrificial recentering" after the collapse of sacral order's restricted economy.

## Questions Raised

1. If absolutism requires clarity of responsibility without interference, how does it prevent the concentration of power from regenerating new forms of hidden sacrifice or scapegoating?

2. What would a "concentrative" educational and institutional apparatus look like in practice—how does one name individuals into order without returning to the very dependencies they've learned to resent?

3. Can the sovereign function as genuine "onomastician-in-chief" in a world where [[declarative culture]] has already been fractured by mass media and competing naming authorities?
