## Summary

All human centrality involves [[resentment]], which emerges immediately after the [[originary sign]] secures the center by preventing access to the desired object. [[Resentment]] operates as a "power imaginary"—a fantasy of recognition that is ultimately directed at whatever [[center]] or [[power]] structure prevents one's own centrality. The author argues that the responsibility of any power [[center]] is not to eliminate [[resentment]] but to contain and shape it through institutions like the justice system. The most effective framing unites [[power]] and [[accountability]], creating what the author calls "absolutist" structures where [[resentment]] can be productively directed toward repairable misalignments.

## Key Concepts

- [[Originary sign]]: The linguistic gesture that both secures the center and generates [[resentment]] by forbidding access to the central object
- [[Mimetic desire]]: The competitive craving for the same object that triggers the originary crisis
- [[Big Man]]: The first occupant of the [[center]], whose position becomes the template for all subsequent human [[centrality]]
- [[Power imaginary]]: A representation of the "good center" that would provide deserved recognition; the fantasied alternative to current power structures
- [[Center]]: The locus of [[power]], recognition, and decision-making authority
- [[Accountability]]: The obligation corresponding to [[power]]; ideally unified with it
- [[Sacramental kingdom]]: Medieval mode of social organization where multiple power centers collaborate without claiming sovereign monopoly on violence

## Notable Passages

> "All resentment is directed at someone who has usurped, or prevented us from occupying, 'our' center."

> "Resentment constitutes a 'power imaginary': a representation of the 'good center' that would provide me with the recognition due me."

> "The best framing is the one that unites power and accountability, that gives everyone the power to do what they are obliged to do—in other words, absolutist framing."

## Questions Raised

1. If [[resentment]] is always shaped by the power structures that frame it, how can a [[center]] introduce new institutional frames without simply generating new forms of [[resentment]] directed at the new system itself?

2. Does the "absolutist" model that unites [[power]] and [[accountability]] escape the problem of [[resentment]], or does it merely make [[resentment]] more transparent and thus more workable?

3. What is the difference between a [[center]] that productively contains [[resentment]] and one that merely suppresses it through force?
