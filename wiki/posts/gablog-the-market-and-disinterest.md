## Summary

This essay explores a fundamental paradox: the free market is theoretically the consensus principle holding together the post-Cold War free world, yet almost no one genuinely supports it when market verdicts threaten their own interests. Unlike defense of civil rights, which has clear spatial and institutional sites of commitment, the market lacks a localized "where" to defend it. The author argues that this crisis of market disinterest becomes newly visible in victimary financial crises—where the market's failures themselves become victimary absolutes demanding state intervention, undermining the system's theoretical coherence.

## Key Concepts

- [[Free Market]]: An economic system requiring genuine commitment to market verdicts even when they harm constituencies
- [[Victimary]]: A mode of claiming injury and demanding redress that colonizes ever-wider domains, including financial systems
- [[Disinterest]]: The capacity to maintain principle without immediate self-interest—absent in market defense
- [[Sovereignty]]: The political capacity to defend freedom and sustain the conditions markets require
- [[Market Verdicts]]: The outcomes of market competition that, when politically reversed, reveal the market's fragility
- [[Originary Thinking]]: GA methodology for analyzing crisis moments with clarity about causation

## Notable Passages

> "The one article of consensus holding together the free world in the post-Cold War era is meaningfully adhered to by almost no one."

> "We would know 'where' to stand in defense of, say, civil rights... But where does one stand in defense of the market—where 'is' the market for that matter."

> "We actually have a victimary financial crisis that, according to some accounts at least, imperils the entire capitalist system. This certainly situates the whole discussion of the victimary on new terrain!"

## Questions Raised

1. How can the market survive as a system if its theoretical defenders abandon it the moment it becomes personally costly—and is this abandonment itself the market's deepest truth?

2. Does the victimary colonization of financial systems represent a fundamentally new crisis mode, or merely the exposure of longstanding contradictions between market theory and political practice?

3. What would genuine market disinterest look like institutionally, and is it even possible in a democratic order where constituencies can demand reversal of market verdicts?
