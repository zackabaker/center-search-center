## Summary

The aesthetic emerges from the oscillation of attention between a deferring sign and a central object on the originary scene. Rather than residing in either element alone, the aesthetic inhabits the oscillation itself—the mediated, socially protected experience of desire. Art history traces the progressive incorporation of the representational scene within the work itself, from ritual's immersive participation through Greek tragedy to modern theater's inclusion of peripheral figures. Yet this boundary-dissolving impulse creates a paradox: to undo the life/art separation requires representing both the resistant practices of life and the segregating conventions of art as critical objects.

## Key Concepts

- [[Originary scene]]: The foundational moment where a sign deferring access to a central object generates meaning and culture
- [[Sign]]: The aborted gesture of appropriation that bars access while enhancing desirability
- [[Oscillation]]: The movement of attention between sign and object constituting aesthetic experience itself
- [[Ritual]]: Institutionally separated immersive experience presupposing taken-for-granted social hierarchy
- [[Representation]]: The formal inclusion of the scene of representation within the artwork, progressing historically
- [[Center and periphery]]: The hierarchical structure wherein certain figures command intrinsic interest, later supplemented by peripheral voices
- [[Resentment]]: The affect purged through identification with the central figure in tragic form
- [[Life/art boundary]]: The ontological separation that modern aesthetics progressively undoes and re-represents

## Notable Passages

"The aesthetic must lie in the oscillation itself—in some object of desire as seen through the gesture, which is to say the constitution of the scene, which makes it a 'formal' rather than 'material' object."

"It may be considered an astonishing testament to the institutional power of artistic representation that not only did it take so long for the idea to emerge that the creative primacy of the artist is ultimately a mere adjunct to the experience of the 'recipient.'"

"Trying to undo the life/art boundary requires that the practices of 'life' that resist participation in 'art' must be represented; and those artistic conventions that 'segregate' the audience from the work must also be represented."

## Questions Raised

1. How can artistic representation authentically incorporate audience participation without replicating the very hierarchical structures it seeks to dissolve? Does the critical representation of conventions inevitably become its own form of domination?

2. What is the practical limit of undoing the life/art boundary—can avant-garde aesthetics ever escape the institutional separation that defines art, or is that separation constitutive of aesthetic experience itself?

3. If the aesthetic oscillation requires both sign and object, center and periphery, does a fully inclusive or participatory art dissolve the conditions necessary for aesthetic experience altogether?
