## Summary

This essay traces the metaphor of divine or natural law "inscribed" on the heart from Hebrew scripture through modernity, arguing that writing fundamentally transformed humanity's relationship to knowledge and memory. The shift from oral to literate culture required internalization of transcendent words through memorization, a discipline that persists even as electronic media now eliminate the need to remember. The author explores how scholarly competence once depended on inscribing texts mentally to enable dialogue across sources—a practice increasingly obsolete in digital culture where information is externally stored and instantly retrievable.

## Key Concepts

- [[Inscription]] — The internalization of written or divine law through memorization and repetition into consciousness
- [[Oral culture]] — Society organized around memorizing names and genealogies with ostensive, imperative knowledge
- [[Transcendent voice]] — The impersonal, universally addressed authority that writing makes possible
- [[Declarative culture]] — Culture mediated by written texts that can be checked objectively and transmitted universally
- [[Attention]] — Acts of pure concentration that constitute discovery and knowledge-making
- [[Memory]] — The bridge between external texts and internal comprehension, now displaced by electronic storage

## Notable Passages

> "In an oral culture one's primary obligation is to know all the names (of ancestors and divine beings) constitutive of the web of existence, or to know who knows them."

> "God's word is then written on the heart through constant oral repetition, and is embedded in culture through its transformation of the language."

> "What marks one as a worthy scholar is being able...to take a written text, available to all, as a prompt for a dialogue with others or an internal dialogue with oneself that, in the end, would produce a new text."

## Questions Raised

1. If electronic culture eliminates the need for memorization, does this represent genuine cognitive liberation or the atrophy of capacities essential to scholarly thought and cross-textual understanding?

2. What replaces [[inscription]] as the mechanism by which cultural values and knowledge become internalized when external storage systems make memorization unnecessary?

3. How does the disappearance of memory-dependent scholarship alter the relationship between individual consciousness and the [[declarative culture]] that once required active internalization to transmit?
