## Summary

This essay identifies two competing modernities emerging from Christianity's exposure of scapegoating. [[Victimary modernity]] inherits Christ's model of the innocent victim, making persecution-resistance universal but trapping itself in perpetual resentment. [[Covenantal modernity]], by contrast, answers how social order becomes possible once hierarchical authority loses legitimacy—through freely entered agreements that generate sustainable community. The two modernities wage an asymmetrical war: the victimary can endlessly stage grievances and exploit resentment, while the covenantal requires fragile consensus and faith in norms that cannot be proven in advance.

## Key Concepts

- [[Victimary modernity]]: Modernity as perpetual denunciation of persecution, borrowing Christianity's scapegoat logic while rejecting hierarchy
- [[Covenantal modernity]]: Modernity based on freely-entered voluntary agreements that sustain a shared "public thing" between people
- [[Scapegoat mechanism]]: The anthropological system of directed violence against an innocent that Christianity exposed and delegitimized
- [[Resentment]]: The affective engine of victimary politics, always finding new inequalities to denounce
- [[Social norms]]: Habitual restraints (decency, politeness, sexual propriety) requiring near-unanimity to sustain but vulnerable to victimary critique
- [[The double bind]]: Victimary modernity's paradox of denouncing the Church using tools it borrowed from Christianity

## Notable Passages

> "This victimary modernity is caught up in a never-ending double bind of both denouncing the Church for its 'hypocrisy' while borrowing, for the purposes of the denunciation, the basic anthropological model preserved by Christianity."

> "The victimary can stage events almost at will, as any equality can, upon close examination, be seen to conceal some unbearable inequality...and no satisfactory answer can ever be given to such complaints."

> "The covenantal requires near unanimity on all kinds of issues, often without any proof...that a little transgression or variation here not only won't lead to devastation, but will even liven things up a bit."

## Questions Raised

1. Can covenantal modernity ever recover sufficient cultural authority to compete with victimary modernity's structural advantages in mobilizing resentment and staging grievance?

2. What empirical markers would distinguish genuine covenant-building from victimary appropriation of covenantal language for resentment-generating purposes?

3. If norms require "near unanimity" but victimary critique always finds hidden inequalities, is the victimary-covenantal conflict inherently unresolvable, or does resolution require a shift in the anthropological model itself?
