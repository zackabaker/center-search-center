## Summary

This essay examines how we imagine restoration of social order through the lens of absurdity (like "food insurance" during collapse). The author argues that political hopes often rest on the false assumption that parasitic evils can be excised while leaving the healthy social body intact—ignoring that marginality is *constitutive* of the center. Drawing on [[originary hypothesis]] and deconstruction, the text suggests that civilized order necessarily occludes self-knowledge, and that attempting to expose the misfit between signs and reality leaves us better prepared than those clinging to illusions of a priori fit.

## Key Concepts

- [[Originary hypothesis]]: The GA founding narrative that explains how meaning and community emerge from the sacred center through deferral of violence.
- [[Center and margin]]: The paradox that what appears marginal or parasitic is actually constitutive of the center itself.
- [[Declarative culture]]: Society's reliance on signs and designations that construct reality rather than simply reflecting it.
- [[Founding event]]: The primal scene that inaugurates social order; nostalgia for "restoration" attempts to forget this necessary violence.
- [[Sign-world fit]]: The perpetual misalignment between our linguistic/symbolic systems and material reality.
- [[Social knowledge]]: What we can and cannot know about ourselves within a civilized order; self-occluding by design.

## Notable Passages

> "What is almost always wrong is to assume the evils, the parasitic, the sick, can be excised in such a way as to leave the original body intact and restored to its natural form."

> "Part of the structure of a civilized order is to occlude such knowledge. Part of the morbid fascination with exceptional regimes...is that we find out who people 'really are' behind the 'façade.'"

> "It is those who attempt the latter [clinging to illusions of a priori fit] who are most severely disabled when signs can no longer be stuck on the world."

## Questions Raised

1. If the misfit between signs and reality is constitutive of civilized order, can we ever genuinely "expose" this gap without destabilizing the entire social fabric—or is exposure itself already incorporated into the system?

2. What distinguishes those capable of creating "alternate economies" and new communities in crisis from those paralyzed by the collapse of old signs? Is it prior practice in acknowledging sign-world misfit?

3. How does Hayek's superior knowledge-generating capacity of decentralized markets relate to the author's claim that civilized order necessarily occludes self-knowledge?
