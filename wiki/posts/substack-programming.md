# Programming

## Summary

This essay proposes that a fundamental "programming language" underlies human meaning-making, articulated through three basic phrases: "before and after will have been the same," "the part of all is the same," and "doing and happening are the same." Rather than mere abstraction, these phrases function as prayers and tools for engaging perplexity. The author argues that programming language and natural language increasingly converge, with code functioning as a "transfer idiom" embedded in conversation itself, allowing us to map reality by identifying what remains invariant across historical deferral and scenic transformation.

## Notable Passages

> "Before and after will have been the same poses the question of succession and options, and also our debt to the center; the part of the all is the same pertains to the decision settling a dispute retrieving the nomos or originary distribution."

> "Everything in reality is like everything else in reality in innumerable and ever new ways... everything, with each thing presented to us through the grace of a historically extended deferral, is an articulation of the phrases."

> "Learning to code will eventually converge with learning a language, or an idiom... as a kind of magnetic center of conversation."

## Questions Raised

1. How does the "programming string" become long enough to convert mere likeness into genuine difference—and what determines that threshold temporally and spatially?

2. If programming language and natural language are converging as transfer idioms, what pedagogical and political consequences follow for those who learn code versus those who don't?

3. Can the three foundational phrases actually function as a unified method for analyzing any discourse, or do certain domains of meaning resist this kind of algorithmic translation?
