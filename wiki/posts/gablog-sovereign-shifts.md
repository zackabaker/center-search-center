## Summary

This essay traces the dissolution of liberal-democratic sovereignty through the contradiction between its ethnic-particular foundations and universalist claims. As globalizing elites abandoned responsibility for domestic populations in favor of transnational agendas, sovereignty itself hollowed out, generating resentment among displaced working classes. Contemporary nationalist identity politics represents an attempt to reconstitute a more compact form of sovereignty, yet no coherent formalization of this new sovereign structure has emerged—raising questions about whether such reformation is even feasible.

## Key Concepts

- [[Sovereignty]]: The capacity to centralize and defuse resentments generated by central power; now contested between liberal-global and nationalist-local models
- [[Identity politics]]: The shift from ideological to identity-based political organization, with identity preceding culture preceding politics
- [[Nationalism vs globalism]]: The fundamental antagonism between rooted ethnic-national interests and universalist transnational governance
- [[Liberal state]]: A majority-ethnic polity claiming transcendence of ethnic interest through universal rights, now torn by this contradiction
- [[Resentment]]: The generative force of contemporary politics; produced by elites' abandonment of displaced populations
- [[Biopolitics]]: Immigration and anti-discrimination policies deployed to weaken or humiliate native populations
- [[Center and periphery]]: The globalizing center's indifference to peripheral populations whose labor and lives sustain it

## Notable Passages

> "The sovereign must centralize and defuse the resentments generated by central power, but the globalizing state lost interest in attending to the resentments of wide swaths of the population."

> "Liberal anti-communism always contained the germ of the global liberal crusade."

> "Contemporary nationalist identity politics is an attempt, as yet groping, to retrench to a compressed form of sovereignty to replace the one based on 'citizenship' and which has been evacuated."

## Questions Raised

1. Can a new, more compact form of sovereignty be formally theorized and institutionalized, or is the nationalist attempt to reconstitute sovereignty inherently unstable?

2. How does the transition from Cold War sovereignty (capital-labor balance + anti-communism) to post-Cold War liberal universalism explain the specific timing and velocity of contemporary resentment?

3. If mainstream and radical nationalist approaches both fail to formalize genuine sovereign reconstitution, what third possibility—if any—remains available?
