# Narrative

## Summary

This essay examines how [[narrative]] functions as the primary framework through which humans explore and represent [[mimetic desire]]. While "narrative" has become common political language—replacing cruder concepts like ideology and propaganda—the term remains loosely applied. The author argues that effective narratives operate according to structural laws: they require beginnings, middles, ends, characters in hierarchical relationship, and conflicts that propel action forward. Crucially, narratives "hook" us by recruiting us to take sides, thereby generating the very desires and goals that sustain our investment in the narrative outcome. This mechanism reveals how narratives can both illuminate and delude us about our actual motivations.

## Key Concepts

- [[Narrative structure]] — bounded sequences with beginnings, middles, ends; characters; events; and resolutions
- [[Mimetic desire]] — the fundamental human capacity to desire through imitation of rivals' desires
- [[Character hierarchy]] — ordering of agents by importance within the narrative frame
- [[Antagonism]] — the structural opposition between agents that sustains narrative interest and momentum
- [[MacGuffin]] — the nominal object of pursuit; less important than the structure of rivalry itself
- [[Narrative framing]] — the way events are selectively rendered visible through narrative constraints and options
- [[Side-taking]] — the mechanism by which narratives recruit audience investment and generate new, previously unimportant goals
- [[Counter-narrative]] — alternative narrative that can infiltrate and contest dominant frames

## Notable Passages

> "Narratives, by definition, have beginnings, middles and ends. They have characters, or agents—usually in some hierarchy of importance...The things that happen propel the narrative forward."

> "A narrative 'hooks' us by getting us to take sides, to see the agent's actions and goals as our own. But, looked at this way, narratives generate delusions by inflaming and providing new pretexts for our mimetic desires."

> "Hitchcock's dismissive reference to the goal sought by the protagonist as the 'MacGuffin' is correct, because the object is less important than the structure of rivalry itself."

## Questions Raised

1. **How do we distinguish between narratives that illuminate genuine social dynamics and those that primarily inflame mimetic desire?** The essay suggests narratives inevitably generate delusions—but are some narrative frames more epistemically honest than others?

2. **Can counter-narratives "infiltrate" dominant frames without themselves becoming subject to the same mimetic recruitment mechanisms?** Or does contestation necessarily replicate the structure it opposes?

3. **What would it mean to construct narrative awareness itself—to recognize we are being "hooked" into side-taking without abandoning the narrative altogether?** Is this even possible, given that narrative is fundamental to human meaning-making?
