## Summary

This essay argues that naming precedes both truth and ethics in human culture. Drawing on Eric Gans's originary hypothesis, the author demonstrates that shared pointing-at-objects establishes the foundation for all subsequent knowledge claims. Truth emerges only within a framework of prior ethical deferral and collective memory; the paradoxes embedded in truth-claims are resolved through the originary act of naming, which simultaneously binds a community together and preserves its shared attention. Names are thus sovereign acts that stabilize meaning while remaining perpetually contestable.

## Key Concepts

- [[Originary scene]]: The primal human event where collective deferral of violence through representation establishes language and community
- [[Representation]]: The cultural order ensuring shared successful identification of objects between participants
- [[Ethics precedes cognition]]: The principle that ethical deferral of the desired object makes truth-claims and knowledge possible
- [[Naming]]: The conferral of a shared name through collective pointing, which preserves memory and establishes meaning
- [[Collective memory]]: Shared, language-mediated memory that precedes and constitutes individual memory
- [[Paradox]]: The logical contradiction at the heart of truth—we agree on truth only by already having agreed on truth
- [[Sovereignty]]: The authority to name and the responsibility to maintain naming against contestation

## Notable Passages

> "Ethics precedes cognition: we have deferred violence by representing the desired object and thereby letting it be; only then can we talk 'about' the object, and say true or false things about it."

> "Before truth, before agreement and disagreement, before arguments, there are names. The first human word, on the originary scene, was 'God'—the name of the object that saved and established the community."

> "Names can be changed and disputed—this happens all the time and is a well-worn means of waging cultural and ideological warfare—but only some names, while we continue to rely on the unquestioned reliability of yet other names."

## Questions Raised

1. If naming is always sovereignly contestable, how do communities maintain sufficient stability in meaning to operate functionally without constant semiotic warfare?

2. Does the author's claim that "you can't speak while suspending every word's meaning" entail that radical critique or deconstruction is necessarily parasitic on the very naming conventions it seeks to undermine?

3. How does the distinction between names we rely on unquestioningly versus those we wage ideological battles over map onto contemporary struggles over terminology and identity labels?
