## Summary

This piece argues that prioritizing **meaning** over **truth** as a filter for discourse is more productive than the typical left-right binary of fact-checking. Drawing on Peirce's pragmatism, the author shows that meaning emerges from the practical effects a sign generates within a specific [[disciplinary space]]. Rather than getting trapped in accusations of lying—a rhetorical move that forces binary qualification—we can instead ask what a statement *means* by examining its conceivable practical bearings. This approach allows us to both comprehend positions generously and critique them rigorously depending on context.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Sign]]**: A representational unit whose meaning depends on practical effects, not inherent truthfulness
- **[[Meaning]]**: The conceived practical effects that an object or statement would have (Peirce's definition)
- **[[Disciplinary space]]**: A shared frame of reference where certain meanings are regional, relative, and contextually valid
- **[[Truth vs. Meaning]]**: Truth claims require agreed criteria; meaning can be conferred even on ostensibly false statements
- **[[Pragmatism]]**: The philosophical method of determining meaning through practical bearings rather than abstract essence
- **[[Declarative culture]]**: The impulse to declare winners/losers via fact-checking rather than understanding effects

## Notable Passages

> "A sign has to have meaning before it can be true or false, and so setting one's filters for discourse ingestion on meaningful/meaningless should be more effective than setting them for true/false."

> "Distinguishing between truth and falsehood is a waste of time unless we are situated within a disciplinary space with shared frames and criteria."

> "Within one space, 'dogs are devoted' retains its significance; within another, it is meaningless, and is replaced by 'dogs respond to cues of dominance.'"
