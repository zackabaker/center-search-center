## Summary

Gans examines the conservative euphoria surrounding Rand Paul's filibuster on presidential drone strikes, tracing it to multiple sources: novelty of spectacle, hunger for confrontation with Obama, libertarian constitutional fetishization, and genuine concern about executive power. However, he argues the substantive issue—whether a president can kill American citizens during armed rebellion—has an obvious answer that makes the entire exercise largely performative. The real roots of the excitement, Gans suggests, lie in deeper psychological and political currents that the text only begins to excavate.

## Key Concepts

- [[Victimary logic]]: A self-righteous stance that prevents honest hypothetical reasoning about threats and power
- [[Constitutional fetishization]]: The belief that fidelity to the Constitution (rather than pragmatic governance) defines political imperatives
- [[Sovereignty]]: The legitimate power to wage war and eliminate threats, including against citizens in rebellion
- [[Political catharsis]]: Emotional release from spectacle that obscures substantive analysis
- [[Legitimacy vs. misuse]]: The distinction between whether a power is rightly possessed versus how it might be abused
- [[Left-wing paranoia]]: Projection of worst-case scenarios onto political opponents without addressing underlying principles

## Notable Passages

> "Whether the government might misuse the powers at its disposal tells us nothing about whether it legitimately possesses those powers."

> "The President would have to have the power to put down a rebellion organized on American soil; such a rebellion, by definition, would exceed the powers of law enforcement and put us on a war footing."

> "Victimary thinking would exclude even the hypothetical construction of such a scenario, since one must in some way 'stigmatize' some specific group in order to do so."
