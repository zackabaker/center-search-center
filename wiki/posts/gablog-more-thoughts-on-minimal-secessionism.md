## Summary

This post argues that systemic collapse of the contemporary market-democratic order is a real possibility worth planning for, and that the deepening factional divide in American life may require institutional separation rather than forced coexistence. The author identifies five key observations: progressivism has exhausted its capacity to recirculate resentments, its adherents cannot accept alternative orders, a majority wants current benefits without expansion, progressivism cannot stabilize, and a growing minority rejects it entirely. This polarity—rather than any viable center—now drives events toward potential civil conflict.

## Key Concepts

- [[Progressivism]]: The assumption that modern complexity requires scientific expert management rather than spontaneous order or private agencies
- [[Systemic collapse]]: The breakdown of current market-democratic arrangements, after which economic and political relations would necessarily reconstitute
- [[Center]]: A potential political middle ground between hardcore progressives (15-20%) and those rejecting progressivism (20-25%), though increasingly unlikely to form
- [[Minimal secessionism]]: Thinking on the margins of the current system to prepare for its possible failure while simultaneously making that failure less likely
- [[Resentment recirculation]]: The capacity of a system to absorb and manage the frustrations it generates; progressivism's declining ability here signals systemic limits
- [[Polarity]]: The bifurcation of American factions into irreconcilable camps, replacing any possibility of centrist mediation

## Notable Passages

"Progressivism has reached its limits, that is, it generates more resentments than it can recirculate, and it now threatens to swallow up the market order upon which it has been merely parasitic so far."

"Some kind of separation, embedded in differing ways of life, of the factions from each other, might be the best alternative to civil war."

"If the majority interested in some version of current arrangements could constitute a genuine center, those rough edges could be smoothed out; but if they can't, then it is the polarity that will drive events."

## Questions Raised

1. If a genuine center cannot form and polarity drives events, what institutional mechanisms could enable peaceful separation without triggering the centralized suppression the author fears?

2. How does one distinguish between "minimal secessionism" as prudent preparation and rhetoric that accelerates the very collapse it claims to prevent?

3. What would "restored normalcy" look like after systemic collapse—and on what [[originary]] grounds would new political and economic relations establish legitimacy?
