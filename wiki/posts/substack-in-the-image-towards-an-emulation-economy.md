## Summary

This text proposes that [[mimesis]]—the fundamental human drive to imitate others—should be the explicit foundation for economic and moral systems, rather than the fiction of self-interest that liberalism promotes. The author argues we are always modeling ourselves on models, even when reasoning, and that a well-designed social order would channel this mimetic desire rather than deny it. Against both Girard's hope for universal imitation of Christ and Gans's vision of an omnicentric market, the text suggests transitional programs toward a post-money economy grounded in making mimetic motivation transparent and politically leverageable.

## Key Concepts

- [[Mimesis]] — The originary human drive to imitate others; the basis of desire, morality, and motivation beneath all claims to autonomous reason
- [[Internal mediation]] — Girard's term for modern mimetic crisis where models are accessible, creating rivalry and conflict
- [[External mediation]] — The pre-modern condition where models (aristocracy, monarchy) were distant and inaccessible, limiting mimetic conflict
- [[Self-interest]] — The liberal substitution for mimesis; a concealment of our fundamentally imitative nature
- [[Omnicentric market]] — Gans's vision of distributed centers generating local deferrals and contained mimesis
- [[Center]] — The institutional locus from which tokens and models are distributed
- [[Declarative culture]] — Culture built on explicit principles rather than concealed motivations

## Notable Passages

"Everything everyone does is in order to be like someone else, and the entire social order would therefore best be designed so as to leverage and channel this desire."

"Liberalism's privileging of the individual is a way of denying, concealing and opposing our basic mimetic nature—anything about us that can be traced back to imitation of another is inauthentic and discredited."

"Even if those models are especially good reasoners"—one is always modeling oneself on models, never escaping [[mimesis]].

## Questions Raised

- If mimetic desire is universal and acknowledged, how do we prevent the escalation into [[internal mediation]] and crisis without returning to aristocratic or monarchical distance?
- What would an "emulation economy" actually look like institutionally—what mechanisms replace both money and Girard's universal Christ-imitation?
- Does making mimesis explicit and declarative change its operation fundamentally, or does transparency itself become another form of concealment?
