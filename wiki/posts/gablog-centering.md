# Centering

## Summary

Power is fundamentally the occupation of [[center]] and the subsequent direction of attention toward another center. Drawing on Eric Gans's originary history, the text traces power from primitive egalitarianism through the emergence of the "Big Man" to monotheistic constraints on centrality. True power requires not merely coercion but sustained respect; sovereigns must defer conflicts by modeling order and avoiding uncontrollable resentment. Ultimately, centering is absolute power within any given [[attentional]] space, whether wielded by torturers or kings.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Center]]** — The occupational locus of power; what individuals and systems must control to direct attention and maintain sovereignty
- **[[Attention]]** — The fundamental medium through which power operates; directing others' focus is the mechanism of control
- **[[Big Man]]** — Gans's originary figure who first seized permanent occupancy of the sacred center and control of distribution
- **[[Resentment]]** — The uncontrollable force that destabilizes power; sovereigns must contain it while avoiding its escalation
- **[[Sacred center]]** — The permanent, transcendent reference point (especially in monotheism) that constrains and legitimizes individual occupancy of power
- **[[Absolute power]]** — Authority that is total within its bounded sphere of attention, whether microscopic (torturer-victim) or macroscopic (state)
- **[[Centering (verb)]]** — The act of positioning oneself at the center while deferring conflicts through respect and reference to permanent order

## Notable Passages

> "Centering is power. Power entails, first, occupying the center and, second, using that occupation to direct attention to another center."

> "Whoever occupies the place of power must not be generating resentments more uncontrollable than those his presence in power contains. He must, in centering himself, be deferring conflicts by directing attention to a more permanent center, a model of order."

> "The tortured has the absolute power to satisfy or not the torturer's desire and to that extent 'over' the torturer. Obviously the scope of this power is extremely limited, in space and time, but within those limitations, it is absolute."

## Questions Raised

1. How does the distinction between coercive power (sustained by fear) and legitimate power (sustained by respect) map onto different media or technological epochs? Does [[declarative culture]] alter which form dominates?

2. If absolute power is always relative to its sphere of [[attention]], does the fragmentation of attention in digital spaces create a proliferation of micro-sovereignties, or does it dissolve sovereignty altogether?

3. What prevents a sovereign from becoming aware that he is generating the very resentments he seeks to contain—and what role does denial play in the stability of centering itself?
