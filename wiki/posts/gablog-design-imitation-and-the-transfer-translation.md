## Summary

This essay investigates how ideas, beliefs, and opinions translate into institutional change by reframing the problem through [[practice]] rather than abstract "ideas." The author argues that social knowledge operates through iterative practices that can be recognized as "the same" across different contexts and media. Drawing on Marcel Jousse's concept of "transfer translation," the text proposes that cultural knowledge survives through repeated, embedded practices that must be preserved as language and meaning shift—making the identification of what remains "the same" across different media the central problem of social life.

## Key Concepts

- [[Practice]]: A doing that can be iterated and recognized as the same thing by others; the mark of order distinguishing human action from mere events
- [[Translation]]: The production of something in one medium that preserves identity with an original in another medium; all social practices are translations of other translations
- [[Transfer Translation]]: The written residue of the most repeated and embedded oral traditions, transferred to new media when original language intelligibility diminishes
- [[Medium]]: The specific domain (oral, written, gestural) in which practices occur; identity across media is always in question
- [[Knowledge]]: The ability to say that two things are the same—whether across time, participants, or as a sample to population relationship
- [[Iterability]]: The capacity for a saying or doing to be repeated by others such that it remains recognizable as "the same thing"
- [[Proportionality]]: The structural commensurability between parts and wholes; knowledge as recognizing identical proportions across scales

## Notable Passages

> "A practice is a doing in the middle of things that are happening. Part of the practice, then, is marking the difference between what you are doing and what is happening."

> "A translation produces something in one medium that is the same as some original in another medium. So, all social practices are translations of all the other translations, with the question always being, what makes it a translation, or the same, in this new medium as all the others."

> "The parts of the oral tradition that must be preserved and therefore cannot be allowed to dissipate with the loss of or diminishing intelligibility of the language in which they have been articulated."

## Questions Raised

1. If all social practices are translations of translations, what breaks the infinite regress—what is the "original" that anchors identity claims across media?

2. How do we determine what constitutes "the same" when transfer translation necessarily loses elements in crossing from one medium to another? What criteria of identity hold?

3. Does the problem of recognizing transfer translation differ fundamentally from the [[mimesis]] problem in generative anthropology—i.e., how is cultural continuity preserved through radical difference?
