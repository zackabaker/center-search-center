## Summary

This post applies [[Goodhart's Law]]—once a measure becomes a target it ceases to be useful—to linguistics, politics, and education, arguing that abstract concepts like "cognition" and "critical thinking" inevitably drift from measures into bureaucratic targets. The author proposes [[idiom]] as the foundational protective concept, ensuring we remember that all measures are linguistic designations dependent on those who iterate them. Rather than escaping into metalanguage, GA must make idiom itself generative, keeping measurement grounded in the [[scenicity]] of actual discourse and the disciplines that produce it.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Goodhart's Law]]**: Once a measure becomes a target, it loses its utility as a measure; the logic of bureaucracy
- **[[Idiom]]**: A naming or designation that depends on those who recognize and iterate it; the foundation of all discourse
- **[[Scenicity]]**: The scene within which every sign is used and meaning is disciplined; the concrete context of utterance
- **[[Linguification]]**: The reduction of all phenomena to linguistic and semiotic elements rather than metalinguistic abstractions
- **[[Natural Semantic Primes]]** (Wierzbicka): Primitive semantic elements underlying all language; alternatives to abstract concepts
- **[[Measure]]**: Any designation that depends on iterability and communal recognition; vulnerable to becoming a target
- **[[Pedagogical Outcome]]**: Knowledge judged by those competent in specific circumstances; resists bureaucratic abstraction

## Notable Passages

> "Every single word, expression or sentence is or contains measures that are liable to become targets. This is the reason for my hostility to words like 'cognition,' 'consciousness,' 'belief.'"

> "The most important measure I have taken in this regard is centering the concept of idiom as the condition of all discourse: the protection offered by 'idiom' is that it ensures that we don't forget that any measure is an idiom."

## Questions Raised

1. Can idiom be made sufficiently generative to replace metalinguistic concepts like "cognition" without collapsing back into vagueness or scenicity-blindness?

2. How do we teach or transmit idiom iteratively without turning pedagogical outcomes into Goodhart targets—what would competence in idiom-making look like?

3. Is the distinction between measure and target itself stable, or does every attempt to distinguish them linguistically risk creating a new measure-become-target?
