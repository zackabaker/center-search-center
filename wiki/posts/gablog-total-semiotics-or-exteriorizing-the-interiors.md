## Summary

This post argues that our reliance on metaphors of interiority—"inside," "deep down," "beliefs"—is an artifact of literate, declarative culture rather than a necessity of human thought. Drawing on Olson's analysis of how literacy introduced accountability markers like "belief," the author traces how nouns derived from these markers create the illusion of internal psychological containers. By contrast, a Peircean semiotic framework treats everything as signs mediating signs, eliminating the need for interior mental spaces. In an omnicentric social order, what matters is not hidden beliefs but observable conduct and the work of [[self-centering]] through [[attention|attentional exchanges]].

## Key Concepts

- [[Interiority]]: The metaphorical "inside" where we locate thoughts, feelings, and beliefs—a by-product of literacy, not necessity
- [[Declarative culture]]: The culture of written, literate discourse that supplements reported speech and creates accountability structures
- [[Semiotics]]: Peirce's framework that all knowledge is mediated by signs; nothing escapes semiotic mediation
- [[Omnicentric order]]: Post-sacrificial social arrangements where everyone is constituted as a center with institutional anchors (names, IDs, histories)
- [[Self-centering]]: The work of constituting oneself as a center through managing attention and reciprocal confirmation
- [[Attentional exchange]]: Reciprocal words and gestures through which people confirm and compete over each other's centrality
- [[Icon, index, symbol]]: Peirce's triadic semiotics, roughly corresponding to GA's [[ostensive]], [[imperative]], [[declarative]]

## Notable Passages

> "Interiorization is metaphysics, or the declarative culture of literacy: all the interiorizing concepts are drawn from the supplementations to the reported speech scene David Olson identifies in 'classic prose.'"

> "Everything that we know is a sign. Things that are not signs can be known because they generate effects that are registered by signs, and from those signs we can infer their causes."

> "What makes someone a someone in the first place is that he constitutes himself as a center among centers, and it is this self-constitution as a center that will enable us to do all the work of the interiorizations I recommend replacing."

## Questions Raised

1. If we eliminate the metaphor of interiority entirely, how do we account for the genuine phenomenological experience of inner conflict or deliberation—or is that too a linguistic artifact we mistake for experience?

2. Does the omnicentric order (with its ID numbers, credit cards, institutional histories) actually constitute centers in the way pre-literate societies might have, or does it merely simulate centering while obscuring deeper power asymmetries?

3. How does the move from "what someone believes" to "what someone does" avoid collapsing ethics into pure behaviorism, especially when conduct itself requires interpretation through signs?
