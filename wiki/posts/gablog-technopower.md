## Summary

This post examines the fundamental question of whether [[sovereignty]] precedes and determines technological/economic power, or vice versa. The author engages Nick Land's materialist argument that technological innovation inherently disrupts centralized power, against Reactionary Futures' claim that unified sovereignty can persist. The text proposes three futures: uncontrolled techno-political spiraling, technologically fortified private domains, or recovered [[sovereignty]] that maintains commensurability between speech and action. The author ultimately argues that a competent sovereign can manage complexity without micromanagement, and that contemporary tech giants show no inclination toward Land's decentralized utopia.

## Key Concepts

- [[Sovereignty]] — power that says what it does, does what it says, and maintains commensurability between utterance and action
- [[Technopower]] — the intersection of technological innovation and political control; the question of who directs technological change
- [[Market logic]] — the materialist claim that complex systems cannot be centrally controlled without self-destruction
- [[Capitalism]] — born from European fragmentation; sustained by keeping the world "broken" for capital intensification
- [[Private property]] — technologically fortified domains that might allow escape from broader political disruption
- [[Complexity]] — the management problem that challenges both sovereignty and market-driven systems
- [[Decentralization]] — Land's utopia of distributed technological power beyond any sovereign reach

## Notable Passages

> "Capitalism hatched in Europe because Europe was broken. Keeping the world broken seems similarly indissociable from the survival of capitalistic historical momentum."

> "The capable sovereign will seek to make as little [management] as possible... management of everything that needs to be managed."

> "Today's new technological powers... are all too eager to converge with existing forms of (self-subverting) sovereignty."

## Questions Raised

1. Can a truly competent sovereign manage technological complexity without attempting micromanagement, or does the feedback loop between innovation and control inevitably produce self-subversion?

2. Why do contemporary tech giants consistently converge with state power rather than pursuing Land's vision of technological independence—is this structural inevitability or merely contingent choice?

3. Does the distinction between "management of everything that needs managing" and Land's "uncontrollable complexity" rest on a coherent principle, or does it simply relocate the problem of definition to whoever claims authority to decide what "needs" managing?
