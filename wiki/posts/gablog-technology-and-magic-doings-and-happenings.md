## Summary

This essay traces the origin of predication through Eric Gans's analysis of the [[declarative sentence]] as emerging from the [[operator of negation]]—a counter-imperative that halts action rather than commanding it. The first predication, paradoxically, involves negating the presence of a desired object while affirming it imaginatively through the [[negative ostensive]]. This move creates a horizontal (person-to-person) and vertical (group-to-center) dimension that must be resolved for true predication—ultimately requiring the emergence of verbs to fully mediate between imperative force and linguistic representation.

## Key Concepts

- [[Operator of negation]]: An imperative commanding *not* to do something, requiring ongoing "normative awaiting" rather than terminal completion
- [[Negative ostensive]]: A paradoxical linguistic move confirming the object's presence in imagination while denying its actual availability
- [[Declarative sentence]]: The linguistic form that emerges when predication fully mediates between imperative and ostensive dimensions
- [[Normative awaiting]]: The sustained attention and expectation-setting required when one commands abstention rather than action
- [[Originary scene]]: The primordial moment of linguistic emergence where desire, prohibition, and naming converge
- [[Imperative]]: The original linguistic force: commands to do or (derivatively) not to do
- [[Predication]]: The attribution of properties to objects, requiring both horizontal and vertical (social and sacred) dimensions
- [[Verb]]: The grammatical form that finally resolves predication by mediating action and attribution

## Notable Passages

> "The operator of negation must originate in the original prohibition, created on the originary scene, directed toward the group's desire, converging on the central object."

> "There is no greater mark of charisma than superior self-control" — the authority to issue a counter-command derives from demonstrated restraint.

> "The first predicate simply modifies the name of the object as not present or not available—but, even more, as rendering itself absent or unavailable in obedience to a higher command."
