## Summary

This essay contests anti-civilization thinkers like John Zerzan by arguing that the [[originary hypothesis]] does not require us to accept their nostalgic primitivism. While Zerzan claims pre-civilizational life was Edenic—characterized by presence, minimal conflict, and unlimited desire—the originary perspective suggests the pre-human state may have been one of "perpetual delight" driven by heightened [[mimetic capacity]] rather than simple abundance. The [[originary event]] itself, far from being inevitable, introduced new [[deferral of desire]] and social complexity, but this need not vindicate civilization's necessity or virtue. The text opens space for a civilizing politics that neither romanticizes the past nor assumes progress is inevitable.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Originary hypothesis]]**: The founding assumption that human consciousness emerged from a specific event of linguistic and social origin
- **[[Mimetic capacity]]**: The heightened ability to imitate and desire what others desire, driving both community and conflict
- **[[Originary event]]**: The foundational moment when convergence on a central object injected fear and created language and social order
- **[[Deferral of desire]]**: The civilizational mechanism requiring individuals to postpone immediate satisfaction, enabling social structure
- **[[Presence]]**: Pre-civilizational human existence characterized by immediate experience, absence of abstract thought, and temporal suspension
- **[[Mimetic proclivities]]**: The innate drive toward imitative behavior in advanced hominids preceding the originary event
- **[[Central object]]**: The locus of collective attention and desire around which the originary event crystallizes

## Notable Passages

> "This transitional period might have been one best characterized by perpetual delight... the desire of everyone around you multiplying your own might have given objects a kind of halo."

> "The originary hypothesis assumes an event in which the general convergence upon the central object injected a new kind of fear into the proto-human community, but it does not assume that this fear was justified."

> "Nothing in the originary hypothesis is affected by the anti-civilization creed."

## Questions Raised

1. If pre-human mimetic existence was indeed characterized by "perpetual delight" rather than scarcity, what grounds justify the originary event's inevitability, and does acknowledging this contingency change our evaluation of civilization's value?

2. How does the distinction between justified and unjustified fear at the originary event reshape our understanding of [[deferral of desire]]—is it an imposed constraint or an emergent necessity?

3. Can a coherent "civilizing politics" exist that neither defends civilization apologetically nor accepts Zerzan's primitivist critique, and what would such a politics demand of us?
