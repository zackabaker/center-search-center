## Summary

The text traces how modern individuality emerged through a Rousseauian "Romantic stance" where one claims victim status to attract attention, exploiting persecution narratives as proof of authenticity. However, this stance has become pathological when appropriated by collective movements. True modern individuality instead involves risking scapegoating by openly defending others—a secularization of Christian witness. Yet the victimary apparatus has inverted this: by scapegoating the "normal" citizen, it paradoxically confirms its own claims, trapping bourgeois institutions in a post-Auschwitz crisis where they cannot produce new martyrs or witnesses.

## Key Concepts

- [[Romantic stance]]: Rousseau's invention where individuals construct themselves as victims of universal persecution to gain centrality and attention
- [[scapegoat mechanism]]: The arbitrary exclusion of a figure to restore social order; rendered non-arbitrary after Christ's exposure of it
- [[victimary]]: Collective movements that claim exclusion from the marketplace and weaponize persecution narratives against the normal/bourgeois
- [[Christian event]]: The crucifixion that exposed scapegoating as arbitrary and established new reciprocity norms
- [[witness]]: The modern individual who risks scapegoating by openly defending others in danger
- [[Auschwitz theology]]: The interpretive horizon that imprints institutional complicity on all bourgeois institutions, preventing new martyrs
- [[modernity]]: The expansion of Christian practices (witness, imitation) beyond strictly liturgical realms into secular society

## Notable Passages

> "To be a modern individual is to court, if only in the most distant, mild, or simulated way, the risk of being scapegoated."

> "Once Jesus has exposed the arbitrariness of the scapegoat function, that function can no longer be arbitrary: it is now he or she who confronts whosoever claims to be today's 'big man' with a broader mode of reciprocity."

> "The victimary scapegoating return[s] the normal citizen to his or her originary (modern) vocation as witness to the modern as the 'secularization' of the Christian event"—yet this inversion traps the bourgeoisie in complicity.

## Questions Raised

1. Can modern institutions produce new, non-ironic martyrs before "another event comes to supplant the Holocaust"—or is post-Auschwitz culture permanently trapped in irony?

2. If the victimary mechanism inverts Christian witness by scapegoating the normal citizen, at what point does the inversion itself become transparent enough to break its spell?

3. How do we distinguish between authentic risk-taking witness (defending actual scapegoats) and simulated Romantic victimhood when both operate within the same symbolic structure?
