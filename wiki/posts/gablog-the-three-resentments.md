## Summary

This essay synthesizes [[absolutism]] and generative anthropology to theorize social order through the lens of [[resentment]] rather than pre-social factors like genetics or natural rights. The author argues that [[resentment]] is fundamentally an index of the [[center]]'s security and that all stable social order depends on the strategic management and "donation" of resentment *toward* the center rather than against it. Using the originary scene as a model, the text shows how the [[center]] simultaneously creates and restrains desire, generating dual resentments that can either destabilize or stabilize society depending on their relative intensity.

## Key Concepts

- [[Center]] — The sacred object around which the community gathers; both creates and perpetually bars possession of itself
- [[Resentment]] — The affective index of whether the center can secure obedience; originally directed at the center's prohibition of desire
- [[Originary scene]] — The primordial moment of human emergence where the center stabilizes the group through sign-mediated deferral
- [[Absolutism]] — Political theory that explains governance through the sovereign's need to secure itself, rather than from pre-social human natures
- [[Donation of resentment]] — The mechanism by which resentment toward the center is neutralized by redirecting it toward those who might transgress the center's authority
- [[Object (capital O)]] — The sacred center; distinct from objects (small "o") which can only be possessed through the center's signs and permissions

## Notable Passages

> "Resentment is an index of the degree of security or certainty of the central power. It is above all resentment that needs to be governed and managed by the sovereign."

> "The power of the buffalo ancestor is secure insofar as the latter resentment [toward other group members] outweighs the former [toward the center itself]."

> "Objects (small 'o') can now only be possessed under the aegis of some sign, a sign that guarantees the protection and permission of the Object."

## Questions Raised

1. How does the "donation of resentment" function in modern mass societies where the [[center]] is abstract or institutional rather than embodied? Can this ancient mechanism still operate effectively?

2. If [[resentment]] toward the center must be continually managed and never fully resolved, what distinguishes stable from unstable orders—is it merely a quantitative threshold of redirected resentment, or are there qualitative differences?

3. Does absolutism's explanatory power depend on treating all governance as ultimately insecure, or can a sovereign ever genuinely stabilize itself such that [[resentment management]] becomes secondary to other concerns?
