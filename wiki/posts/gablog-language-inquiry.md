## Summary

This text develops a rigorous account of meaning by distinguishing between the [[scene of presencing]] (direct sign exchange among participants) and the [[scene of representation]] (signs exchanged with outsiders/strangers). The author argues that all meaning is fundamentally [[ostensive]], yet the distinction between [[signified]] and [[referent]] became necessary for language to move beyond immediate pointing to portable, reassemblable signs. Understanding occurs when signified and referent coincide, allowing shared acknowledgment of deferral and power in a given scene.

## Key Concepts

- [[Ostensivity]]: The capacity of a sign to direct attention to something present in the here-and-now; the originary mode of all meaning
- [[Signified]]: The sign's capacity to articulate an emerging scene by composing elements on the model of previous scenes; the object as deferring and powerful
- [[Referent]]: The sign as directing attention to an object already present on an established scene; the object as available for orderly appropriation
- [[Scene of presencing]]: The exchange of signs among participants sharing a scene
- [[Scene of representation]]: The exchange of signs between a participant and a stranger to the scene; the purpose of providing necessary information
- [[Sparagmos]]: The chaotic distribution of a sacred object, mediated by the repetition of the sign to prevent violent overreach
- [[Declarative]] speech: One of the four originary speech forms through which meaning progressively emerges

## Notable Passages

> "Ultimately every word (but also, then, sentence, discourse, etc.) is the name of God, then the indistinguishability of signified and referent is definitive or constitutive of meaning as such."

> "The signified is the object as deferring and powerful; the referent is the object as available for orderly appropriation."

> "When we 'understand' each other, what happens, then, is that the signified and referent coincide for us."

## Questions Raised

1. How does the distinction between [[scene of presencing]] and [[scene of representation]] map onto the difference between [[imperative]] and [[declarative]] speech forms? Does one scene privilege certain speech forms over others?

2. If understanding requires coincidence of signified and referent, what accounts for persistent miscommunication or resentment—is it always a failure to achieve this coincidence, or can conflict arise even when it is achieved?

3. Does the author's three-fold scene (presencing, representation, and constitutive) suggest that all utterances necessarily involve all three scenes simultaneously, or do some utterances remain primarily within one scene?
