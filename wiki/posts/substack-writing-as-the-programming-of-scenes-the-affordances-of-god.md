## Summary

The author critiques the "Big Scenic Imaginary"—the media-inflected tendency to represent social reality as a unified stage where demographic groups act as agents with unified emotions like resentment. Against this residual orality within literacy, he argues that writing functions not as shared scene-presentation but as *programming* of possible scenes: declarative sentences are algorithms that singularize ostensive gestures and remain open to any speaker. Drawing on Biblical wisdom literature, he positions God's self-naming ("I AM THAT I AM") as the model for anonymous yet intimate declarative utterance that transcends particular ritual sites.

## Key Concepts

- [[Big Scenic Imaginary]]: Media-constructed representation treating demographic groups as unified agents capable of shared emotions
- [[Declarative sentence]]: Algorithm figuring possible ostensives at the end of a sequence of imperatives; best understood as programming rather than presentation
- [[Residual orality]]: Illiterate persistence of oral scene-presence within literate media culture
- [[Ritual/juridical/disciplinary scenes]]: The only real arenas where actual agency is constructed
- [[Anyoneness]]: Quality of anonymous utterance that could be voiced by anyone positioned to recognize its ostensive singularization
- [[Ostensive gesture]]: The pointing act singularized by declarative language; the endpoint of programmed scenes
- [[Declarative naming]]: God's "I AM THAT I AM" as model for untethered, portable presence transcending fixed ritual place

## Notable Passages

> "Writing is less a presentation of a shared scene than a programming of possible scenes: if someone were to write X then some implicated set of persons might be expected do Y range of things."

> "The God whose name is the declarative sentence is therefore a mode of generating scenes (which ultimately must 'take place')."

> "Only under such conditions could God always be with you, untethered to any specific ritual place or gesture."

## Questions Raised

2. If the declarative sentence models God's portable presence, what are the political and theological implications of reducing declarative utterance to algorithmic calculation rather than revelation?

3. Can actual juridical and ritual scenes ever truly escape the "Big Scenic Imaginary," or does mass mediation necessarily contaminate their singularity?
