## Summary

This essay argues that our access to reality is fundamentally mediated through signs, grounded in the [[originary hypothesis]]. The author uses the Iraq War as a case study: the most "realist" strategic argument for invasion—that U.S. forces were trapped between staying in Saudi Arabia (unsustainable) and leaving (appearing weak after 9/11)—became culturally "unthinkable," replaced instead by abstract legalistic debates about UN authorization. This disconnect reveals how a "late [[victimary order]]" constructs reality through [[declarative culture]] rather than through [[ostensive]] acknowledgment of actual geopolitical situations.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Ostensive sign]]**: The first human sign-form, pointing to a shared object; the foundation of all reality-access
- **[[Declarative sentence]]**: Speech form generating independent representable reality, enabling mimetic discourse
- **[[Victimary order]]**: A social configuration where victimhood becomes the primary currency of legitimacy and reality-construction
- **[[Scenic/mimetic]]**: The intrinsically theatrical and imitative character of human reality and discourse
- **[[Reality construction]]**: The mediated, sign-dependent nature of what "counts" as real in a given culture
- **[[Imperative/interrogative reality]]**: Reality as it "answers" questions or "forbids" violations; the layer between ostensive and declarative

## Notable Passages

> "Our access to reality is always through signs: the originary hypothesis discredits empiricist claims about the unmediated access to reality through the senses more thoroughly than all the idealisms."

> "We couldn't stay and we couldn't go: invading Iraq and removing the source of the crisis cut the Gordian knot and enabled the history of our involvement in the Middle East to start up again."

> "The most 'realist' argument possible simply didn't 'register' across the entire culture, which was completely caught up in what were essentially contrived debates centered on points of international law."
