## Summary

This essay uses the figure of the serial killer in contemporary TV culture—particularly *Dexter*—as a lens for understanding mimetic crisis and the problem of "lastness" on the originary scene. The protagonist's emotional autism and need to strategically simulate human responses reveal a deeper cultural condition: the difficulty of maintaining genuine ethical commitment in a postmodern age where pathological compulsion appears inseparable from moral action. By analyzing how the "last" participant on the originary scene experiences the sign objectively but not subjectively, Gans illuminates how cultural mimesis breaks down when participants can exploit the system without being "taken in" by it.

## Key Concepts

- [[Mimesis]]: The mutual imitation and desire-modeling that shapes human culture and social bonding
- [[Originary scene]]: The anthropological moment of human origin when the first sign emerges to prevent mimetic violence
- [[Lastness]]: The structural position of those who arrive after the sign has already organized the group, creating asymmetrical access to meaning
- [[Declarative culture]]: Culture sustained through explicit rules and performances rather than tacit knowing
- [[Ethical autism]]: The inability to feel or intuitively grasp others' emotional states; requiring conscious strategic simulation
- [[Postmodern relativism]]: The cultural condition where dedication to the good appears necessarily pathological

## Notable Passages

> "It has become extremely difficult to imagine such dedication to the good in contemporary culture as anything other than the result of a pathological compulsion subjected to equally pathological self-control."

> "Everyday life becomes a never ending series of strategy sessions. At the same time, though, Dexter is very good at strategizing, and to that extent knows the semiotics of everyday life better than anyone else."

> "For the last, the sign is objective but has no subjective component—he sees that it works, but it doesn't work for him."

## Questions Raised

1. If contemporary culture consistently renders ethical dedication as pathological, what structural conditions have made genuine (non-strategic) moral commitment culturally unintelligible?

2. Can the "last" position—where one understands the sign's mechanics without participating in its meaning—ever be transcended, or is it endemic to any hierarchically organized scene?

3. What distinguishes Dexter's strategic mastery from the tacit competence of ordinary social actors, and does this distinction collapse under scrutiny?
