## Summary

This essay applies J.L. Austin's concept of "felicity conditions" in speech act theory to generative anthropology, arguing that all language should be understood as performative rather than constative (truth-conditional). Drawing on Gans's originary hypothesis, the author demonstrates that even declarative sentences—traditionally defined by truth conditions—function performatively by answering questions within historically constituted disciplinary spaces. The piece reveals how sentences enact meaning not through correspondence to reality but through their efficacy in specific social contexts.

## Key Concepts

- [[performative speech acts]] — utterances that effect change in the world rather than describe it (e.g., "I do" in marriage)
- [[felicity conditions]] — the proper ritual, ceremonial, sanctioned circumstances required for a performative to "work"
- [[constative speech acts]] — referential utterances judged by truth or falsity; treated as limiting case of performative
- [[originary hypothesis]] — Gans's theory that the first speech act was performative, creating humans, God, and objects
- [[declarative]] — truth-conditional sentences that function performatively by answering questions within a disciplinary space
- [[disciplinary space]] — the historical community of language users whose tacit questions inform utterances
- [[ostensive and imperative]] — lower speech acts that obviously do something through their saying

## Notable Passages

> "Within the disciplinary space created by the originary hypothesis, the first speech act was undeniably performative, creating humans, God, and a world of objects that could be referred to, the decision is an easy one: all uses of language are to be understood as performative."

> "A good sentence is one that maximizes the questions it elicits and answers... Our sentences are informed by tacit, unasked questions."

> "What makes a question answerable, and which questions a sentence might be answering, depends upon the space, ultimately a disciplinary space of historical language users, within which the sentence is uttered."

## Questions Raised

1. If all language is performative, how do we distinguish between sentences that successfully constitute shared reality versus those that fail to establish [[felicity conditions]]—and who adjudicates that boundary?

2. What is the relationship between the "tacit, unasked questions" that inform our sentences and the collective resentment or desire that generates them in the first place?

3. How does understanding the [[declarative]] as performative rather than truth-conditional reshape our understanding of intellectual authority and institutional power?
