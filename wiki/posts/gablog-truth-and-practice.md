## Summary

Truth is defined not metaphysically but pragmatically: whatever enables the perfection of practice. A practice emerges when intentional action produces repeatable, identifiable effects that can be isolated and recorded. The author traces how practices evolve through retrospective reframing, where past accidents become recognized as imperfect versions of later refined activities. Crucially, human practices—especially those involving speech and social interaction—differ fundamentally from physical ones: they require construction of appropriate social conditions, authority structures, and shared [[declarative culture|declarative spaces]]. The paradox is that we recognize ourselves as practicing only after the fact, through the anomalies and mistakes that reveal what we were "really" doing all along.

## Key Concepts

- [[Practice]]: Intentional activity producing repeatable, isolable effects that can be pointed to and shared
- [[Truth]]: Whatever enables further perfection of a practice; correlation between action and identifiable outcome
- [[Attention]]: The partly hidden intentional structure of present actions, only fully revealed through future practices
- [[Declarative space]]: The social conditions and authority structures necessary for human practices to exist
- [[Anomaly]]: The starting point of practice recognition; what doesn't fit existing frames of expectation
- [[Retrospective reframing]]: The capacity to recognize past actions as proto-versions of present refined practices
- [[Mimetic mediation]]: How practices are made "routine and practicable for anyone" through design and demonstration

## Notable Passages

> "You have a practice when you can point to something that happens that could only have been a result of something you did."

> "The more inclusive of past practices and the more anticipatory of future ones they are, the more truth your present practices generate."

> "The starting point is always an anomaly or a mistake—someone does or says something that doesn't fit the frame of expectations that enable us to make sense of so[cial reality]."

## Questions Raised

1. How does the retrospective recognition of practice differ between physical domains (glass-breaking) and linguistic/social ones (conversation)? Can we always trace back what we were "really doing"?

2. If truth is relative to practice-perfection, what prevents competing or incommensurable practices from each claiming their own truth? Where is the [[center]] that adjudicates?

3. Does the requirement for "social space," "minimized violence," and "constructed authority" mean that certain human practices can never achieve the clarity of scientific ones?
