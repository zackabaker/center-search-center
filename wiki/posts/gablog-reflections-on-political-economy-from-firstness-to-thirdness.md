## Summary

This essay traces how market economies manage the inherent [[asymmetry]] created when innovation disrupts the existing [[division of labor]]. New products create new desires and breach established value systems, forcing responses: entrepreneurship operates as [[firstness]] (the asymmetrical gesture), while government intervention via fiat money and regulation represents [[secondness]] (attempts to restore symmetry through resentment management). The author argues that both entrepreneurial disruption and state intervention must be recognized as economic facts, with [[money]] serving as the medium through which these competing forces take definite shape.

## Key Concepts

- [[Firstness]]: The asymmetrical entrepreneurial gesture that introduces unprecedented products and desires into the market
- [[Secondness]]: State and regulatory responses (fiat money, legislation) that attempt to restore symmetry and address resentments disrupted by innovation
- [[Asymmetry of exchange]]: The destabilizing gap created when new value emerges outside ritual or tradition
- [[Division of labor]]: The existing social-economic order disrupted by innovation and new product introduction
- [[Fiat money]]: State-created currency that privileges early recipients, functioning like a Ponzi scheme structurally
- [[Entrepreneurship]]: A mode of firstness; the creation and market introduction of unprecedented goods
- [[Resentment]]: The neglected social feeling that secondness draws upon to legitimize intervention and restore order

## Notable Passages

> "All exchanges end symmetrically, but, once they are no longer bound by ritual, they must begin asymmetrically."

> "The one who brings the new product to market can't know whether it will, in fact, create the corresponding desire...that too is part of the asymmetry, but also the basis for converting the asymmetry into a new symmetry."

> "Those who are able to make the conversion first have the advantage—not only are they...the only players, but, as partners with the government in the new regulatory regime, they can shape future regulation."

## Questions Raised

1. If secondness inevitably emerges to manage entrepreneurial asymmetry, is there a [[thirdness]] that transcends this oscillation—or does capitalism perpetually cycle between disruption and regulatory capture?

2. How does the [[resentment]] that justifies government intervention itself become a mechanism of new asymmetry, creating captive markets and regulatory privilege?

3. Can [[money]] function as a genuinely neutral medium of deferral, or does its state-created nature necessarily embed secondness (and resentment management) into the economic foundation itself?
