## Summary

This essay examines Zionism as a fundamentally incoherent political project riddled with internal contradictions, yet one that becomes intelligible through the originary anthropological lens. The author acknowledges his own Zionist commitment while cataloging the theoretical impossibilities of the doctrine: the arbitrary claim to Jewish nationhood, the disdain for diaspora Jews, the erasure of existing inhabitants, and most critically, the structural dependence on perpetual Jewish vulnerability elsewhere. Rather than dismiss these paradoxes, the piece suggests they reveal something essential about how [[declarative culture]] constructs and sustains political entities through [[victimary]] logic.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Zionism]]**: Political movement claiming scattered Jewish diaspora constitutes a nation requiring territorial consolidation in ancient homeland
- **[[Victimary logic]]**: Political framework dependent on the perpetuation of victimhood to justify institutional power
- **[[Galut]]**: Jewish diaspora; exile condition that Zionism seeks to cure through return
- **[[Declarative culture]]**: Systems of meaning and identity constructed through linguistic assertion rather than natural necessity
- **[[Self-hatred]]**: Embedded in the Zionist demand that diaspora Jews be remade as "new Jews," implying their current existence is deficient
- **[[Center and periphery]]**: The relationship between Israel as imagined homeland and diaspora communities whose insecurity paradoxically legitimates the center

## Notable Passages

> "Zionism is quite a mess—there's nothing like a shared, coherent version of it... The idea that Jews scattered throughout the world constitute a single 'nation' in anything like the modern sense is a stretch, to say the least."

> "The worse things are for Jews elsewhere the better for Zionism... the movement to make Jews safe depends upon increased dangers for Jews."

> "If they are so hated everywhere, won't they be just as hated all gathered together in one place?"
