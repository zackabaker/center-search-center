# Barbarian Within

## Summary

This essay traces how [[mimesis]] and the [[originary scene]] establish the foundational logic of civilized order as a *suspension* rather than elimination of barbarism. Gans argues that civilization works by internalizing rules that mediate mimetic violence—converting potential group-on-group retaliation into state-administered justice. However, true civilization remains impossible; we can only defer our barbaric impulses through reliable legal structures. The essay concludes by locating barbarism and savagery within a developmental schema rooted in [[generative anthropology]], suggesting that civilized order perpetually contains within itself the very forms of violence it claims to have transcended.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Mimesis]]**: The foundational law that what another does, you do; what is done to you, you do to them
- **[[Originary scene]]**: The primordial moment where the [[originary sign]] interrupts mimetic escalation, deferring unimaginable violence
- **[[Civilization]]**: The generalization and internalization of rules that contain local violence to prevent larger outbreaks
- **[[Barbarism]]**: An order organized around competing "Big Men" and group-on-group retaliation
- **[[Savagery]]**: Egalitarian, collective society prior to the emergence of Big Men
- **[[Law]]**: The mediated response to transgression; the state's "controlled, more reliable barbarism"
- **[[Honor society]]**: Pre-civilized order where violence against one group member triggers counter-violence against the offending group

## Notable Passages

> "To be thoroughly civilized would be to forego satisfaction for the offense committed against us individually in favor of the invention and dissemination of practices that would reduce the level of offense more generally."

> "We can only suspend our barbarian inclinations in deference to the controlled, more reliable barbarism of the state."

> "We can never be thoroughly civilized: we can only suspend our barbarism, which also means we must preserve it."

## Questions Raised

1. If civilization requires perpetually suspending rather than eliminating barbarism, does this mean that societies claiming highest civilization are merely those most effective at *hiding* their barbaric core—and what happens when that concealment fails?

2. How does the essay's historical schema (savagery → barbarism → civilization) map onto contemporary identity politics and [[victimocracy]], where groups increasingly demand direct retaliation rather than faith in mediating institutions?

3. If the legal system's legitimacy depends on being "more reliable" than private vengeance, what occurs when citizens lose confidence in that reliability—does civilization automatically revert to honor-society logic?
