## Summary

This essay examines why powerful figures—particularly billionaires and political leaders—almost never speak explicitly from their position of power, using Michael Bloomberg's unusually candid rhetoric as a foil. The author argues that maintaining power requires obscuring its sources and mechanisms; explicit disclosure would render power illegible to those who exercise it and unimaginable to everyone else. The absence of a "serious rhetoric of power" from modern discourse reflects a fundamental structural problem: the powerful cannot speak truthfully about what they know without destabilizing the plural order that sustains them.

## Key Concepts

- [[Power]] — the capacity to influence outcomes; best preserved through obscurity rather than declaration
- [[Leverage]] — the hidden mechanisms through which power operates in plural, unstable orders
- [[Declarative Culture]] — the tendency to speak as if from egalitarian premises rather than from actual position
- [[Center and Periphery]] — the spatial relation between those with knowledge/power and those without it
- [[Mimesis]] — the human need to identify with recognizable characters and narratives
- [[Sovereignty]] — the position of decision-making that cannot be fully articulated without losing efficacy

## Notable Passages

"Wouldn't detailed, honest, accounts of everything that goes into their decision-making by the most powerful people in the world be the most informative disclosures we could imagine?"

"The less others know about the precise sources of his power, the better—except for when he wants to bring a very precise quantum of power to bear in a particular instance."

"Were Trump, or any powerful figure, to speak so explicitly about the sources and purposes of his power, he would be presenting an image of the world most of us would be incapable of reproducing or picturing for ourselves."

## Questions Raised

1. Is there a structural difference between Bloomberg's "petty" explicit power-speech and Trump's strategic opacity, or are both ultimately failures of the same imperative to remain illegible?

2. If the powerful cannot speak truthfully about what they know without destabilizing order, does this create an insurmountable gap between power and language itself—and what are the consequences for democratic legitimacy?

3. What would a "serious rhetoric of power" that *could* work actually sound like, if traditional declarative forms necessarily fail?
