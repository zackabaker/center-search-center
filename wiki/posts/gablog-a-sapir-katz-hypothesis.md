## Summary

This post critiques the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis as it's typically deployed in cultural theory, arguing that obsession with cross-linguistic differences misses the real generative relationship between language and thought. Katz proposes instead a focus on [[idiom|idioms]]—novel linguistic formulations that create new ways of thinking within a single language before normalization occurs. The argument shifts emphasis from structural linguistic determinism to the [[linguistic invention]] that emerges through desire, resentment, and cultural pressure, making language an engine of thought rather than a constraint on it.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Sapir-Whorf hypothesis]]**: The claim that grammatical structures determine or heavily influence thought and cultural perception
- **[[Idiom]]**: A novel linguistic formation that creates new possibilities for thought before being normalized into standard usage
- **[[Linguistic invention]]**: The creative generation of new idioms demanded by emerging desires, resentments, and social pressures
- **[[Victimary thinking]]**: The liberal intellectual obsession with asymmetrical cultural differences rooted in Romanticism and anti-imperialism
- **[[Normalization]]**: The process by which innovative idioms become standard, creating space for new linguistic creativity
- **[[Semiotic vocabulary]]**: The tools needed to describe thought and culture through linguistic and sign-based analysis

## Notable Passages

> "Language must be bound up with thought and culture, and we must be able to describe thought and culture with linguistic and semiotic vocabularies—what else are thought and culture comprised of if not words, sentences and signs?"

> "The point is not the inherent properties of language; rather, it is...the possibilities for invention inherent in language, and the certainty that new desires, resentments and loves will demand new idioms of expression."

> "Language...normalizes idioms and idiomizes norms, thereby creating new resources for expression."

## Questions Raised

1. How can we empirically distinguish between an [[idiom]] that generates genuinely new thought versus one that merely repackages existing concepts in novel form?

2. If [[normalization]] necessarily follows [[linguistic invention]], is there a predictable rhythm or pattern to when cultures produce intense bursts of neologism versus periods of linguistic stability?

3. Does the speed of [[idiom]] proliferation (e.g., internet slang) change the relationship between language and thought compared to slower historical periods of linguistic evolution?
