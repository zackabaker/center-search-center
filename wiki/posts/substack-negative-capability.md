## Summary

The [[originary hypothesis]] poses a genuine explanatory problem—accounting for the qualitative emergence of human language—yet no academic discipline can accept its answer because doing so would eliminate their future research agendas. Drawing on John Keats's concept of "negative capability," the author suggests that GA's mode of thinking operates as a distinct form of scientific inquiry: one that requires sustained intellectual scrutiny without the promise of empirical verification. This paradox points toward a recognition of limits within modern disciplinary knowledge-production and hints at connections between Romantic aesthetics and originary thinking.

## Key Concepts

- [[originary hypothesis]] — The claim that human language emerged in a singular event rather than through gradual genetic accumulation
- [[negative capability]] — Keats's ability to remain in uncertainty and mystery without irritable reaching after fact or reason
- [[joint attention]] — Tomasello's concept of shared intentionality; a continuity often used to minimize human discontinuity
- [[tertiary retention]] — Stiegler's semiotic category; another tool for flattening animal-human communication differences
- [[human discontinuity]] — The qualitative break between human and animal communication that makes the species unique
- [[disciplinary closure]] — How academic fields structurally reject answers that would eliminate their own future work

## Notable Passages

> "In saying, 'human language emerged in an event, not through a gradual accumulation of genetic mutations tied to signaling behaviors,' the originary hypothesis is answering a real question."

> "The answer can't be accepted precisely because it gives people in these disciplines nothing to do—no future dig will come up with some mixture of bones and pottery that will make the originary event more probable."

> "The originary hypothesis is 'too' scientific, or another kind of scientific. But what kind?"
