## Summary

The declarative sentence is fundamentally a mechanism for exposing the embedded [[ostensives]] and [[imperatives]] within other declarations. Every sentence rests upon vast layers of witnessed events and deferred demands, ultimately tracing back to the [[originary scene]]. Language preserves peace by allowing shared attention to referents rather than contention for them, and discourse functions as a complex system of "buffers" that prevent civilizational crisis. The proper use of the declarative is therefore political: to navigate and preserve the precarious scaffolding that keeps society from collapse into [[reciprocal violence]].

## Key Concepts

- [[Originary scene]]: The primordial moment when the first sign nominates a central object that repels appropriation, establishing shared attention over contention
- [[Ostensives]]: Gestures or utterances that point to things; the foundational layer of all declarative sentences
- [[Imperatives]]: Commands or demands embedded in discourse; the deferred violence that language suspends
- [[Referent]]: The object of attention in language; retains traces of the original repellent force that made language necessary
- [[Deferral]]: The postponement of conflict through layered buffers of discourse and ritual; the mechanism of civilization
- [[Declarative sentence]]: An utterance that exposes hidden ostensives and imperatives in order to redirect attention and preserve social order
- [[Civilization]]: An extended system of buffers—layered, graduated topics and rituals—that defer irreconcilable struggle over distribution of goods and power
- [[Buffer]]: Any discourse, practice, or institution that prevents the escalation of local conflict into general crisis

## Notable Passages

> "The sign, in referring to the thing, lets it be, and in letting it be, lets us share attention devoted to the thing, rather than contend for it."

> "There are layers of deferral here, and this is much of what we mean by 'civilization.'"

> "Our discoursing is always concerned with preserving and enhancing the general buffering system, if not necessarily any particular buffer."

## Questions Raised

1. How do we identify which "buffers" are genuinely preserving order versus which are merely deferring catastrophe to a larger scale?

2. When a declarative sentence exposes the ostensives and imperatives in another, does it necessarily strengthen the buffering system, or can such exposure itself trigger the very crisis it aims to prevent?

3. Can the theory of graduated deferral explain contemporary phenomena where multiple incompatible civilizational orders (with conflicting referential hierarchies) occupy the same space?
