## Summary

This essay traces the aesthetic back to the [[originary scene]], where the oscillation between [[sign]] and [[object]] creates a space for self-inspection. Using a "Us vs. Them" T-shirt as a case study, the author argues that art functions by mirroring our [[resentments]] back to us in formalized, "bare" ways that allow distance and critique. The aesthetic emerges whenever participants in a scene can withdraw from competitive self-promotion to inspect the signs presented by others—a capacity fundamental to human scenes generally, and to art specifically.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Originary Scene]]** – The moment of human origin where the aborted gesture of appropriation becomes a sign, establishing representation itself.
- **[[Sign and Object]]** – The oscillation between the formal sign (aborted gesture) and the desired center object that consolidates the aesthetic dimension.
- **[[Mimetic Desire]]** – The symmetrical alignment of desire with others that sustained conflict intensifies, creating the need for ironic distance.
- **[[Resentment]]** – The emotional substrate that art exhibits back to us in formal dress, allowing inspection and curtailment.
- **[[Aesthetic Scene]]** – A potential scene within any human event where participants withdraw from self-promotion to judge signs as well-formed or unformed.
- **[[Self-Disarmament and Deterrence]]** – The dual balance visible in the aesthetic body: vulnerability and threat held in equilibrium.

## Notable Passages

> "The aesthetic on the scene, in the form of one of one's fellow 'signers,' is the entire body presenting as a more or less perfect balance between self-disarmament and deterrence."

> "Art has human figures at the center, replacing the deities of mythology, and the figures initially placed at the center are unquestionably important within the human scene."

> "Each of us has to do it for others"—we must mirror back resentment in formal dress so others can see and distance themselves from their own mimetic entanglement.

## Questions Raised

1. How does the withdrawal into aesthetic judgment differ functionally from other forms of [[deferral]] or [[delay]] in human scenes? Is the aesthetic always available, or are there conditions that make it inaccessible?

2. If art's power lies in mirroring resentment back "in a barer form," what happens when that formal exhibition itself becomes an object of resentment—i.e., when critique itself becomes mimetically charged?

3. Can the aesthetic scene scale beyond small, copresent groups to mass or distributed audiences? Does [[declarative culture]] alter the conditions under which formalization can work?
