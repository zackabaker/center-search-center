## Summary

Representation is the process of operationalizing claims—making them verifiable through concrete events and institutional frameworks. Culture offers various models for operationalizing different types of claims: markets for value, experiments for nature, aesthetic attention for beauty, and institutions for justice and law. The author argues that politics and morality fundamentally involve bringing partisan claims into public view under institutional signs, thereby transcending naked egotism while renewing the institutions that must transcend partisanship.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Operationalization]]**: Making abstract claims concrete and verifiable through events, scenes, or institutional practices
- **[[Representation]]**: The deferral of violence through articulation of an event around a sign; bringing claims into accord with events
- **[[Sign]]**: The legal, normative, or cultural framework that gathers dispersed claims into meaningful form
- **[[Institutional power]]**: Concentrated, aggregated structures that display and limit power, generating enclosed scenes for assessing responsibility
- **[[Sparagmatic pie]]**: The centrifugal pull of naked self-interest threatening to dissolve into violence
- **[[Public attention]]**: The sustained focusing of collective notice required to authenticate claims about beauty, morality, or justice

## Notable Passages

> "In this way, one can pursue partisan ends in such a way as to renew the institutions that must transcend partisanship."

> "The naked egotism of 'show me the money!' is simultaneously a way putting the monetary system for determining value to work."

## Questions Raised

1. How do we distinguish between operationalizations that genuinely instantiate a claim versus those that merely simulate or perform commitment without authentic institutional embedding?

2. If representation requires institutional enclosure and rule-bound scenes, what happens to claims—particularly moral ones—that resist formalization or systematization?

3. Can partisan actors genuinely serve institutional renewal, or does the pursuit of partisan ends inevitably corrupt the very signs and norms meant to transcend partisanship?
