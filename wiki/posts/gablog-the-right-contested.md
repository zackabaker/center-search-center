## Summary

This post examines Kevin Williamson's critique of Trump-aligned economic nationalism and the "alt-right," arguing that Williamson's free-trade orthodoxy obscures rather than clarifies the real distribution of state power and subsidy. The author agrees that protectionist schemes may be impractical, but challenges the notion that the current "free trade" framework is truly non-interventionist—it simply subsidizes different groups (global corporations, politicians' pet causes) than economic nationalism would. The core tension: if all political economies redistribute resources, why is it illegitimate to redistribute toward domestic wage earners rather than abstract "market stability"?

## Key Concepts

- [[Economic nationalism]]: protectionist policies designed to benefit domestic workers and industries against global market forces
- [[Victimary logic]]: the strategic claim to victimhood as a basis for political demands and resource redistribution
- [[Declarative culture]]: the power to define what counts as legitimate vs. illegitimate discourse and policy framing
- [[Free trade orthodoxy]]: the ideological masking of particular interest distributions as universal market rationality
- [[Welfare dependency stigma]]: the cultural distinction that makes direct support shameful but indirect subsidy respectable
- [[The center]]: the authority to establish what frames of analysis are "secure ground" vs. illegitimate
- [[Resentment]]: the underlying sentiment driving both Buchanan's white working-class base and the alt-right's Trump enthusiasm

## Notable Passages

> "If the government levies a tax on your neighbors in order to fund an earned-income tax credit for your family, then you're a welfare queen; if the government levies a tax on businesses that is passed on to your neighbors in order to subsidize your earned income through higher prices, then that's economic nationalism."

> "Why not impose the interests of American wage earners upon them, and figure out the details as we go?"

> "On the simplest, or at least most cynical, level, then, why shouldn't white male wage earners get cut in on the scam?"

## Questions Raised

1. If all modern political economies involve state redistribution and subsidy, what principled grounds exist for distinguishing "legitimate" from "illegitimate" beneficiaries—or is this merely a declarative power exercised by those controlling the discourse?

2. Does Williamson's insistence on free-trade framing represent genuine economic analysis, or a way of excluding working-class resentment from the [[center]] of legitimate political conversation?

3. How does the stigmatization of certain redistributive claims (welfare, nationalism) while normalizing others (corporate subsidies, "global stability") function to maintain existing power distributions?
