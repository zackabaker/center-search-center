## Summary

This essay argues that the rise of non-state actors (particularly Islamic terrorism) signals the collapse of the modern state-based political order and the eventual dissolution of liberal democratic politics itself. The author contends that politics as we know it—parliamentary deliberation, competing parties, electoral representation—only becomes possible during periods of civilizational security. Once civilization faces existential threat from non-state actors, citizens will increasingly bypass democratic institutions and delegate power to functional experts capable of conducting the actual defense. Politics, in this account, is revealed as a leisure activity affordable only in times of peace.

## Key Concepts

- [[nomos of the earth]] – Schmitt's term for the international legal and political order governing state relations
- [[state]] – the container for modern politics, increasingly irrelevant as war moves beyond state-to-state conflict
- [[liberal democracy]] – a temporary luxury that depends on solved external threats; collapses under civilizational stress
- [[civilization]] – the historical achievement whose defense supersedes political deliberation
- [[delegated responsibility]] – the replacement of representative democracy with functional agents tasked to specific survival functions
- [[civil war]] – the frozen, perpetual party competition within democracies; masking deeper social antagonisms
- [[meta-politics]] – the level at which one questions politics' very possibility rather than competing within it

## Notable Passages

> "Politics as we know it is only possible as a leisure activity once the gains of civilization have been secured and appear beyond threat."

> "Unless states suddenly get very good at fighting Islamic terror, individual citizens will increasingly take on the task of not only defending themselves against the jihad but at carrying out reprisals and pre-emptive actions they consider appropriate."

> "The competing political parties in the democratic world have never really accepted each other's legitimacy, other than perhaps during brief periods when the parties have represented clearly interdependent social classes."

## Questions Raised

1. If politics genuinely requires civilizational security, how can societies conduct necessary internal deliberation while defending civilization—or must they accept permanent delegation to security experts?

2. Does the author's framework distinguish between states *as such* declining, versus liberal democratic *representation* becoming functionally obsolete under duress?

3. What prevents the "war of civilization against barbarism" from itself becoming the new permanent state of nature, making any return to politics impossible?
