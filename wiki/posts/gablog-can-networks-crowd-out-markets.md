## Summary

This essay explores whether advanced networked subscription systems could replace price-based markets without resorting to central planning. The author traces how market pricing emerges from the necessity of coordinating supply and demand across complex economic chains, then imagines an alternative economy where producers and consumers subscribe to cooperative networks rather than transacting through prices. The thought experiment asks whether the elimination of liberal-democratic "politics" might enable unprecedented coordination through enterprise networks—a genuinely postmarket rather than merely planned economy.

## Key Concepts

- [[Market]] — The decentralized price mechanism that solves the knowledge problem by allowing oscillation around natural cost equilibria
- [[Price]] — The signal that emerges when no one can know demand in advance; oscillates around minimum production costs
- [[Network]] — An alternative coordination system based on stable relationships, reputation, and subscription rather than transactional pricing
- [[Co-op]] — A subscriptional collective of producers and consumers organized vertically through layers of agreed provisioning
- [[Centralized planning]] — The top-down approach to coordination rejected here in favor of networked alternatives
- [[Declarative culture]] — The condition that would make network coordination possible: shared understanding of obligations and capacity
- [[Absolutism]] — Thinking "in absolutist terms" about post-liberal economic possibility rather than incremental reform
- [[Liberal democracy]] — The political system that necessitates market mechanisms; its elimination might enable new forms of coordination

## Notable Passages

> "The only way to find out is to let supermarkets sell as much bread as they can...until we end up oscillating around an average amount of bread sold per month."

> "I'm trying to imagine an advanced economy without prices and therefore without money, but also without central planning, and if that's utopian, so be it."

> "The question is whether the more advanced form of coordination that the elimination of liberalism, democracy, which is to say, 'politics,' might make possible, would in turn make cooperation through a vast network of agreements between directors of enterprises possible."

## Questions Raised

1. If subscription networks require near-total participation to function at scale, does this create a new form of dependency or vulnerability compared to the anonymity of markets?

2. What prevents subscription networks from devolving into the same knowledge and coordination problems markets solve—but now with less price feedback?

3. Does the elimination of liberal democracy necessarily precede the emergence of network economies, or is the causality reversed?
