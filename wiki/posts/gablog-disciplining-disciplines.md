## Summary

This essay argues that all disciplines—whether scientific or humanistic—operate under an external imperative to "bring something back to the center," rather than pursuing pure inquiry. Drawing on [[generative anthropology]], the author traces how disciplines originate in ritual and myth, with different practices maintaining varying distances from the sacred center. By examining how language entered productive activities to defer violence (much as it did on the [[originary scene]]), the essay proposes that even the freest inquiry operates within a civilizational constraint: the need to ultimately serve communal maintenance and [[social differentiation]].

## Key Concepts

- [[originary scene]] – The scene of collective [[sign]] use and resource distribution that grounded human language and community
- [[center]] – The sacred or communal focal point around which [[social differentiation]] radiates
- [[sign use]] – Language deployed to defer potential violence and mediate group relations
- [[social differentiation]] – The emergence of distinct roles and practices at varying distances from the center
- [[ritual]] – The original, strictly ceremonial form of language use in the community
- [[discipline]] – Any practice (scientific or humanistic) that ultimately serves civilizational needs
- [[ethics vs. morality]] – Ethics as norms specific to practice; morality grounded in equality around the center
- [[innovation at the margins]] – The tendency for new communicative and technical capacities to develop away from the center

## Notable Passages

"All disciplinary activity, then, no matter how seemingly impractical, unsupervised and free, is at bottom aware that something needs to be brought back to the center."

"In this case, though, we can assume the forms of potential violence deferred would be lower stakes than those commemorated on the originary scene: say, a challenge not so much to the leader of the expedition himself but to a particular decision."

"Innovation is more likely at the margins than at the center, and this would be a confirmation of that intuition."

## Questions Raised

1. If disciplines must ultimately serve external imperatives, how do we distinguish between genuine innovation and mere deviation that serves no civilizational function?

2. How does the margin-to-center flow of innovation interact with the center's tendency to formalize and ritualize practices once they return?

3. Can we identify contemporary disciplines that have lost sight of what they must "bring back to the center," and does this explain their current legitimacy crises?
