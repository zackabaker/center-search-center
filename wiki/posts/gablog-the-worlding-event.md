## Summary

This essay argues that [[attentionality]] precedes and grounds [[intentionality]], with attention to others' attention constituting the originary human condition. Drawing from the [[originary scene]], the author traces how [[self-reference]] becomes embedded in all human action and utterance through reciprocal attention to a shared [[center]]. Ethical action emerges as the continuous performance of this paradox—turning references outward and inward—which constructs the world as an internalized self-referential system while remaining oriented toward the [[center]]. The text proposes that institutions arise as sites where shared reference-making becomes a model of deferral.

## Key Concepts

- [[Attentionality]]: Joint attention that precedes and enables individual intention; the foundational human capacity
- [[Intentionality]]: Individualized, declarative specification emerging from shared attentional space
- [[Originary scene]]: The founding moment where all participants attend to a central object and each other's attending
- [[Center]]: The sovereign point around which attention organizes; both actual and possible, constructed by each person
- [[Self-reference]]: The paradoxical doubling where utterances simultaneously refer to external things and to oneself
- [[Sovereign imaginary]]: The implicit command structure embedded in every utterance, eliciting recognition from the center
- [[Deferral]]: Historical continuity of deferred appropriation that constructs human meaning
- [[Ethical action]]: The sustained performance of self-referential paradox; making visible the attentional structures binding community

## Notable Passages

> "The sign, as the gesture of aborted appropriation, is really nothing more than the demonstration of this reciprocal attending to their joint attention."

> "Performing the paradox of self-reference is the highest good for originary thinking. Turn every reference to something else into a reference to you and every reference to you into a reference to something else."

> "The center makes its appearance in this layering of the scene and the impossibility of determining whether new things are coming into view or we are sharing attention so thoroughly that we're not sure where your seeing begins and mine ends."

## Questions Raised

1. How does the performance of self-referential paradox in everyday action differ from explicit institutional performance—and can institutions ever truly sustain what individuals accomplish implicitly?

2. If the center is both "actual and possible" and constructed by each person, how do competing constructions of the center resolve into shared meaning without collapsing into either hierarchy or total relativism?

3. What does it mean that the attentional structure is "mostly unaware" to us—and is increased awareness of it liberating or paralyzing?
