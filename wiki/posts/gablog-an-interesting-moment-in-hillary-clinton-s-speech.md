## Summary

This post analyzes Clinton's 2016 campaign rhetoric targeting "alt-right nationalism" as a coordinated global movement led by Putin, arguing that her real target is national [[sovereignty]] itself. The author contends that Clinton's anti-Putin position is fundamentally anti-nationalist and pro-globalist, masking a deeper commitment to eroding state sovereignty. The piece questions why Russia, rather than China, became the designated enemy, and suggests Clinton's hostility reflects her investment in a post-sovereign, globalized order that threatens traditional national interests.

## Key Concepts

- [[Sovereignty]] – the capacity of a nation to govern itself independently and resist external incursions
- [[Nationalism]] – the assertion of national identity and sovereign preference, which Clinton conflates with extremism
- [[Victimocracy]] – a system where moral authority derives from victimhood claims, here deployed against "nationalism"
- [[Declarative culture]] – the power to declare enemies and identities through rhetoric and media authority
- [[Center and periphery]] – the geopolitical structure where US/West claims centrality and Russia asserts rival sovereignty
- [[Imperium]] – imperial structures of power; Clinton's vision of borderless, integrated global order
- [[Human rights discourse]] – the moral framing used to justify interventions against sovereign nations

## Notable Passages

> "To be anti-Putin is to be pro-immigration, anti-patriarchal, anti-majority, pro-EU, and uncritically bullish on NATO."

> "Russia has proven more resistant to infringements on its sovereignty than the US has, and this irks Clinton—especially if it means that the US might become more resistant to incursions into its sovereignty that she has planned."

> "Why did we never try to make Russia an ally, especially post-9/11, once we were at war with Islamic militancy?"

## Questions Raised

1. If China's human rights violations exceed Russia's, why does the liberal order consistently ally with China while treating Russia as the arch-enemy? What geopolitical logic determines this preference?

2. Does [[declarative culture]] allow actors to simply name an enemy (Putin, nationalism) into existence for partisan advantage, or does such rhetoric eventually materialize real geopolitical consequences?

3. What would a genuinely post-sovereign world look like, and who would benefit from it—is it the end-state Clinton envisions, or merely a rhetorical device to delegitimize rivals?
