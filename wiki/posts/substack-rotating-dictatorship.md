## Summary

This essay argues for reclaiming the term "dictator" as a precise descriptor for whoever occupies the [[center]] and governs without formal procedural hindrances—a role the Roman Republic already understood. Rather than avoiding the term due to 20th-century moral baggage, the author contends that conceptual clarity demands we use it. The essay locates the problem of tyranny as the defining concern of Western civilization itself, threading from Greek city-states through philosophy to modern democracy, and argues that Judaism and Christianity both contribute distinct frameworks for subordinating temporal power to higher authority.

## Key Concepts

- [[Center]] – the locus of direct governance and decision-making authority
- [[Tyranny]] – the perpetual Western concern animating philosophy and political thought
- [[Dictatorship]] – rule without formal hindrances; a neutral descriptive term historically obscured by moral opprobrium
- [[Sacral kingship]] – ritual-validated authority that depends on sacred legitimation rather than money or philosophy
- [[Money]] – historically weakens sacral order by enabling rule independent of ritual validation
- [[Meaning]] – destabilized by tyranny; the original philosophical mandate was to restore semantic stability
- [[Linear history]] – the Hebrew Bible's invention of directional time, narrating the humbling of successive arrogant kings

## Questions Raised

1. If meaning-destabilization is tyranny's signature effect, how can language itself ever stabilize enough to mount genuine resistance to arbitrary power?

2. Does the recycling of biblical linear history into progressive teleology inadvertently recreate the very sacral logic it claims to have secularized?

3. What would a "rotating dictatorship" actually look like institutionally—and is rotation itself merely another concealment of power, or a genuine structural restraint?
