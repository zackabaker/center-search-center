## Summary

The author argues that money already functions as data—encoding information about dependencies and resource mobilization. As data exchanges become increasingly explicit and technological, monetary markets can be deliberately replaced by direct data-for-services transactions. Rather than attempting to preserve privacy (which is impossible), the solution is to establish trustworthy [[governance]] structures where citizens exchange their data for genuine security, healthcare, environmental protection, and other public goods. This reconception dissolves the distinction between market and non-market exchange by revealing both as forms of promise-fulfillment mediated through some recordable medium.

## Key Concepts

- [[Market]] – A system of exchange that can be made increasingly constrained and explicit until it transforms into something else entirely
- [[Money as data]] – Currency as information about dependency relations and resource-mobilization capacity
- [[Data exchange]] – Direct substitution of explicit data transactions for monetary mediation in governance services
- [[Governance]] – Trustworthy institutional structures that receive citizen data and provide security, health, and environmental services in return
- [[Declarative culture]] – Making explicit and directly transferable what was previously implicit or "walled up in assets"
- [[Sovereignty]] – The capacity of agencies to command realms determined by access to data and resources
- [[Vendetta]] – The threat from below (popular resentment) that security agencies must defer

## Notable Passages

"Money is already data, insofar as it provides us with information regarding relations of dependency and the capacity to mobilize resources; so, that data can be made increasingly explicit in non-monetary forms, in which case money can also be replaced."

"We must make government, or at least governance, good, and assume that we can have security agencies that focus on deferring the vendetta from below and avoid getting caught up in vendettas from above."

"Any substantive change we can imagine will result from turning existing institutions inside out, so to speak—making explicit and directly transferable data that is still walled up in assets."

## Questions Raised

1. If data-exchange governance requires citizens to trust institutions fundamentally (since privacy is supposedly impossible), what prevents these agencies from reproducing the same vendetta dynamics they're meant to defer? How does trustworthiness itself become data?

2. The analogy to "scout or spy" serving an expedition suggests asymmetry—does the data-provider retain any executive capacity, or merely reportorial status? What distinguishes genuine reciprocity from sophisticated surveillance?

3. If all circulation (food, goods, security) reduces to data calculation, does the physical substrate of production become secondary? How do we prevent the abstraction from becoming totalizing?
