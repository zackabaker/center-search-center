# Flipping the Conference

## Summary

This piece proposes adapting the "flipped classroom" model to academic conferences by posting papers in advance so that conference time can be devoted to substantive discussion rather than passive listening. More importantly, it argues that [[formal representation]] and [[reciprocity]] are not separable—that shared attention itself is the foundation of ethics. Gans suggests that the primary human transformation was ethical, not cognitive, and that joint attention grounds all human equality and moral meaning.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Originary ethics]]**: Ethics as primary to human emergence, preceding representation as a tool for deferring violence
- **[[Joint attention]]**: Shared focal awareness between humans; the ground of fundamental equality and ethical meaning
- **[[Formal representation]]**: The linguistic/symbolic capacity to point to transcendent objects; inseparable from ethical reciprocity
- **[[Reciprocity]]**: The moral model of exchange that defers conflict within communities where animal hierarchy fails
- **[[Originary hypothesis]]**: The theory that language and ethics emerged simultaneously to solve the proto-human problem of deferred violence
- **[[Shared attention]]**: The capacity to direct collective focus; constitutes the "formality" of human representation

## Notable Passages

> "The primary transformation of the proto-human into the human was ethical. Language and representation emerged to defer conflict, not to provide a cognitive or ratiocinative tool."

> "In shared, or joint attention, is the fundamental equality that constitutes the human. All the resources we need for thinking about ethics lie in joint attention."

> "Formal representation is itself ethical, is indeed the origin and resource of any ethics, so that ethics cannot be thought outside of it."

## Questions Raised

1. If joint attention is truly the ground of all ethics, how do we account for ethical failures and violence that occur within communities of apparent mutual recognition?

2. Can "minimal, pared down, ethical vocabularies" grounded in attention suffice for addressing systemic or structural forms of moral harm?

3. How does the flipped conference model itself instantiate or fail to instantiate the shared attention it theorizes as foundational?
