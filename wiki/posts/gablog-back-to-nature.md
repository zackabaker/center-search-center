## Summary

This essay argues that the originary discontinuity between humans and animals must be understood alongside a parallel effort to "re-fit" ourselves back into nature through language, ritual, tools, and technology. Rather than seeing nature and culture as opposed, the author proposes that all human cultural development—from speech to digital algorithms—represents continuous attempts to interpret and integrate with our environment through evolving sign systems. The sense of alienation from nature in modern societies reflects not actual denaturization but loss of shared cultural habituation and collective meaning-making.

## Key Concepts

- [[Originary hypothesis]] — The theory that human consciousness and language emerged discontinuously through a founding gestural event
- [[Sign system]] — The structured codes through which communities interpret and represent their surroundings
- [[Retro-fitting nature]] — The continuous human effort to integrate cultural innovations back into natural patterns and rhythms
- [[Semiosis]] — The process of meaning-making through signs; human participation in signification
- [[Habituation]] — The mechanism by which cultural practices become unconscious and appear "natural"
- [[Deculturation]] — Loss of shared meaning-making frameworks and collective fit with one's community
- [[Cratylian theory]] — The view that language contains iconic, non-arbitrary dimensions reflecting object properties

## Notable Passages

> "Coinciding with the originary leap out of 'nature' must have been a very determined, even compulsive, effort at re-fitting ourselves back into nature."

> "All these devisings are, as Marshall McLuhan asserted, extensions of our senses and more broadly, our semiosis."

> "A sense of being denatured is really a sense of being decultured—of having lost a shared naturalization or 'fit' between a people and their environment."

## Questions Raised

1. If all technology represents retro-fitting into nature, what distinguishes genuinely alienating technologies from ones that maintain cultural habituation—and who determines this distinction?

2. How might recognizing the continuous nature-culture integration challenge the victimary insistence on "naturalness" as a standard for ethics or politics?

3. Can shared habituation be deliberately reconstructed in modern pluralistic societies, or does [[deculturation]] require catastrophic social reorganization?
