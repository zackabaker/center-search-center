## Summary

Problem-solving, especially intellectual and technical problem-solving, is fundamentally a [[scenic]] process that cannot be reduced to language alone. Drawing on his experience designing writing assignments, the author proposes that "figuring things out" involves extracting a [[figure]] from a non-figural background through what amounts to a catalytic revelation. Developing a "grammar" of this figuring-out process is essential because it's central to how world order organizes itself technologically. The key insight is that any utterance or solution becomes distinctive by positioning itself against a hypothetical field of alternative utterances—a practice that makes writers and thinkers producers of irreducible texts.

## Key Concepts

- [[Scenic design]] — The construction, maintenance, and modification of the scenes upon which human action unfolds
- [[Figure/Figuration]] — The emergence of a distinctive form or solution from a previously non-figural background through revelation
- [[Technics]] — Not merely tools but the organized arrangements of elements that enable problem-solving and world-ordering
- [[Assignment]] — An implicit or explicit imperative that structures how we compose utterances, solutions, and practices
- [[Hypothetical field]] — The totality of possible alternative utterances against which any singular utterance becomes distinctive
- [[Inscription]] — The internalization of source texts and prior utterances as habits embedded in our practices
- [[Literacy]] — The unified practice of reading and writing through which we learn to singularize texts and occasions

## Notable Passages

"Thinking is scenic and being on a scene cannot be reduced to its articulation of language."

"The emergence of the 'figure' involves a kind of revelation in which a solution appears, a solution irreducible to all whatever work you have done in arranging the elements."

"In this way you become a producer of irreducible, irreplaceable texts."

## Questions Raised

1. How can we make the implicit assignments and imperatives that structure our thinking more explicit without flattening the revelatory, catalytic quality that makes figuring-things-out succeed?

2. If every person is "always at least partially on some other scene" than the one we confront them on, how do we develop methods for discovering which scene they're actually on without reducing them to mere misalignment?

3. Can the grammar of figuring-out apply equally to stereotyped, "uninteresting" utterances, or does the approach depend on texts making distinctive theoretical claims?
