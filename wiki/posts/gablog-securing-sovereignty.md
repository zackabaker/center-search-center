## Summary

This essay grapples with the paradox of [[secure sovereignty]]: if sovereignty is always exercised rather than held, how can it ever be "unsecure"? Through the heuristic of five hikers rotating leadership, the author explores how [[sovereignty]] depends on both internal competence and external embeddedness within a broader [[order]]. [[Unsecure sovereignty]] emerges not when power fails to be exercised, but when the community loses trust in the sovereign's capacity to perform, or when the civilizational framework that legitimates delegated power breaks down.

## Key Concepts

- [[Sovereignty]]: Power exercised rather than held; always actualized through decision-making
- [[Secure sovereignty]]: The effective exercise of sovereign power within a stable order
- [[Unsecure sovereignty]]: The erosion of sovereign capacity or legitimacy, threatening community coherence
- [[Absolutism]]: The philosophical framework treating sovereignty as necessarily concentrated and conserved
- [[Order]]: The civilizational structure that permits delegation and nested spheres of power
- [[Center]]: The locus from which sovereignty radiates and maintains social coherence
- [[Incapacity]]: The grounds on which a sovereign may be contested or removed without formal procedure

## Notable Passages

"Insofar as sovereignty is always exercised rather than held, it is always secure—and what would it mean to 'hold' sovereignty other than to exercise it repeatedly and explicitly?"

"The more hikers forget or reject the norms of the civilization they belong to...the more likely their consensually agreed upon distribution of sovereignty will fall apart."

"A breakdown of the 'sovereignty' of the hiking group would indicate a weakening of the broader sovereignty over that group."

## Questions Raised

1. If [[unsecure sovereignty]] requires both incapacity *and* community loss-of-faith, which is primary? Can a competent sovereign lose legitimacy, or does legitimacy collapse only when performance fails?

2. How does the "broader sovereignty" that legitimates nested delegations itself remain secure? At what point does the chain of embeddedness bottom out in something that *must* perform to exist?

3. Does the return to "something like a state of nature" (when the hiking group loses civility) mean sovereignty dissolves, or only that a new, violent form of sovereignty competition begins?
