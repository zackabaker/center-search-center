## Summary

This post argues that [[nominalization]]—the conversion of verbs into nouns—is central to both the [[metalanguage of literacy]] and the generative logic of human language itself. Drawing on David Olson's work, the author traces how writing necessitates the lexical representation of relational elements (tone, intent, mimesis) that speech enacts directly, forcing the proliferation of nominal forms. The process reveals that the invisible world is "overwhelmingly populated by nominalizations," and that disciplinary thought itself emerges through the systematic stripping of determiners from nominals, transforming situated utterances into abstract objects of inquiry.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Nominalization]]**: The conversion of verbs into nouns, enabling abstract conceptualization and disciplinary thought
- **[[Metalanguage of literacy]]**: The supplementary lexical layer writing requires to represent speaker stance and relational meaning
- **[[Scene of representation]]**: The speech scene whose elements (tone, body language) cannot be directly written and must be rendered lexically
- **[[Semantic primes]]** (Wierzbicka): The irreducible verb-heavy foundation of all human language (say, want, do, happen, feel, know, etc.)
- **[[Mimesis]]**: The direct enactment of meaning through bodily presence; partially neutralized by writing
- **[[Center of gravity]]**: The generative tendency toward naming and centering inherent in nominalization

## Notable Passages

> "Most of our conceptual vocabulary is comprised of variants of 'say,' with 'think,' 'know,' 'do,' 'want' 'happen,' and 'feel' taking up the rest of the space."

> "As the word is stripped of determiners, it becomes intelligible as the 'object' of a specialized discipline."

> "The invisible world is overwhelmingly populated by nominalizations of these prime verbs and those invented to supplement them."

## Questions Raised

1. If nominalization strips away the speaker's relation to utterance, what happens to [[imperativity]] in the process—do imperatives resist nominalization, or transform it fundamentally?

2. How does the need to generate nominalizations for abstract concepts relate to the originary scene itself, where the first sign must name the deferred object?

3. Can the proliferation of nominalizations in academic discourse be understood as a flight from the [[scene of representation]], or as its most refined articulation?
