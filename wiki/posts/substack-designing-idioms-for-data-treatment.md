## Summary

Against grammaticalist notions of infinite linguistic creativity, the author proposes an [[ergodic hypothesis]] where language operates within a bounded system of [[idioms]] recombined rather than infinitely generated. Drawing on Florian Fuchs's account of modern narrative's emergence from breakdown of classical rhetoric, the piece argues that meaningful utterance works by identifying transformations across [[boundaries]] while revealing underlying sameness—a method applicable to GA's [[scene stacking]] architecture and the design of [[declarative culture]].

## Key Concepts

- [[Ergodic hypothesis]]: Language operates within finite idiomatic systems; recombination ≠ infinite originality
- [[Idiom]]: A bounded linguistic unit that structures thought and utterance around semantic priming
- [[Originary scene]]: The foundational scene of human meaning-making that persists beneath all ritual/juridical/disciplinary structures
- [[Scene stacking]]: Layering originary scenes to reveal transformations and hidden continuities
- [[Boundary]]: A linguistic and experiential threshold that simultaneously divides and unites before/after states
- [[Declarative culture]]: Culture organized around articulated events and meaning-making declarations
- [[Nomos]]: The originary distribution or law that defines justice and injustice

## Notable Passages

> "Just like we have never left the originary scene or moved beyond ritual...we have never moved outside of the oral."

> "The ergodic hypothesis...allows one to think in terms of working on language rather than 'clothing' thoughts in language."

> "One sets up a sharp distinction, as sharp as possible...then find the thread, or succession, that through the differences enables us to see the same."
