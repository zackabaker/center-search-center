## Summary

This essay argues that equality is always structured around a [[sacred center]] and is reciprocally bound to specific forms of inequality. Using Islam's Sharia as a case study, the author demonstrates how apparent inequalities (gender, status) can be framed as equality before God. Modern secular equality, by contrast, seeks to escape the sacred center entirely—making it incoherent and perpetually dissatisfied, endlessly discovering new inequalities without stable grounds for addressing them.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Sacred center]]** — the originary point around which a community structures equality and defers violence
- **[[Deferred equality]]** — equality that remains incomplete or anticipated, motivating social order rather than achieved
- **[[Firstness]]** — the anthropological connection between God (or center) and humanity that grounds reciprocal obligation
- **[[Originary scene]]** — the moment of human emergence where differential relations to the center first arise
- **[[Reciprocity]]** — the mutual constitution of equality and inequality within a centered social order
- **[[Victimary]]** — the modern stance that endlessly identifies marginalized groups and new grievances outside any stabilizing center

## Notable Passages

> "Whatever the disparities in Islamic society between men and women, or even free persons and slaves, they exist on a base of firstness-free equality."

> "The modern notion of equality, which seeks equality outside of and even against any sacred center is both incoherent and insatiable. It will always be possible to identify some new form of inequality and render it intolerable."

> "Any affirmation of equality singles out that feature which positions each one equidistant from some sacred center (with the measuring rod being a product of the center itself)."

## Questions Raised

1. If all equality must be framed around a sacred center, what happens to legitimacy when the center itself becomes contestable or secularized? Does modern democracy possess a hidden sacred center, or is it genuinely groundless?

2. Can the modern victimary's discovery of new inequalities be reframed as a kind of spiritual restlessness—an implicit search for a new center—rather than simple moral progress?

3. Does recognizing inequality's necessity within any [[reciprocal]] order mean accepting current hierarchies as inevitable, or does it open space for imagining different centers and their corresponding reframings?
