## Summary

This essay traces a genealogy of modernity through three simultaneous 17th-century English developments: capitalism (Bank of England), the two-party system, and the assumption of [[compositionality]]—the belief that any thing can be broken down into components governed by universal laws. The author argues that [[desacralization]] of the [[occupied center]] (monarchy) made possible both economic valuation by future discounting and the ritualized rotation of political figures. This foundational assumption of compositionality underwrites modern science, engineering, and social manipulation alike, emerging from state breakdown across Europe and the colonial discovery of "unlimited possibilities" for arranging peoples and resources.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Occupied center]]**: The sacred locus of political authority whose desacralization enabled modern political and economic systems
- **[[Compositionality]]**: The assumption that any thing can be infinitely decomposed and recomposed according to universal laws rather than inherent integrity
- **[[Desacralization]]**: The loss of unanimity and sacred regard directed toward the center, enabling its conceptual and actual dismantling
- **[[Technology]]**: The practical instantiation of compositionality; the rendering of masses and matter as manipulable, recombinant raw materials
- **[[Breakdown]]**: Both the violent dissolution of traditional centers and the analytical method that presumes all wholes are decomposable
- **[[Hobbes]]**: The thinker who most directly mapped desacralized compositionality; his clock metaphor for the state and human reassembly

## Notable Passages

"Anything can be taken apart and put together again, much like, say a clock...the first attempt to map out a thoroughly desacralized world in which anything, including humans as individuals and communities could...be disassembled and reassembled."

"The social sciences become possible, as the starting question, 'what are the most basic components of this thing and by what law are they held together' is suited to inquiries into trade and wealth, government, thought, learning, and anything else humans do."

"Compositionality...would have to suggest itself as a way of thinking through the possibilities and problems opened by the new situation" of colonial-era mass mobility and manipulation.

## Questions Raised

1. If compositionality requires desacralization, what happens when technological power extends so far that nothing remains sacred—does the system become self-consuming?

2. Can [[the center]] ever be truly re-sacralized once compositionality has been assumed, or does the breakdown remain permanent?

3. What distinguishes legitimate from illegitimate "recomposition" of peoples and societies, and on what grounds could such a distinction stand within a compositionalist framework?
