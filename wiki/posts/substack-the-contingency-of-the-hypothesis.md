## Summary

This essay defends Eric Gans's [[originary hypothesis]] against a critique by Willem Ernst van der Roest, who argues that the intensification of [[mimetic crisis]] needed to override natural hierarchy would destroy the group's focus on the center. The author accepts this critique as valid but argues it points not to the hypothesis's failure but to its essential logic: the originary event must be understood through [[contingency]] rather than necessity. The piece challenges GA practitioners to embrace a logic of radical unlikelihood—where something need only happen once—rather than treating all phenomena as inevitable.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Originary hypothesis]]**: Gans's account of the unique, unlikely event in which human language and culture emerged from a resolved mimetic crisis
- **[[Mimetic crisis]]**: Girardian concept of escalating mutual imitation and rivalry that threatens group stability
- **[[Contingency]]**: The quality of being dependent on chance or unlikely conditions; contrasted with logical necessity
- **[[The center]]**: The focal object around which the originary scene is organized and upon which attention converges
- **[[Animal hierarchy]]**: The natural pecking order that maintains group stability prior to human culture
- **[[Mimetic rivalry]]**: The intensification of desire and competition through mutual imitation
- **[[Weapons]]**: A posited material condition necessary to sufficiently intensify crisis beyond natural restraints

## Notable Passages

> "The breakdown of the animal hierarchy would mean the breakdown of the group into a general melee... But this only means that the constellation of the scene around the central object was highly unlikely, not that it was impossible."

> "We have perhaps not sufficiently considered that there is a particular logic built into the originary hypothesis, a logic of contingency rather than necessity, making it impossible for adherents to the far more popular logic of necessity to 'see' the hypothesis."

> "Faithfulness to the hypothesis calls for a logic of radical contingency, beginning with the contingency of the human be[ing]."

## Questions Raised

1. What logical or philosophical framework can adequately capture events that are radically unlikely yet require only a single occurrence to establish permanent cultural effects?

2. Does Gans's methodological principle of treating history "as if it were inevitable" actually undermine the originary hypothesis's own logic, or can these approaches be reconciled?

3. How should GA scholars reconstruct the specific material and social conditions (weapons, spacing, timing) that would allow the group to maintain focus on the center despite the chaos of a breaking hierarchy?
