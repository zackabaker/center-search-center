## Summary

Generative Anthropology offers a corrective to modern democratic politics by recovering what it means to think from the perspective of the [[center]]. The author argues that de-ritualization—rooted in Judaic and Christian revelation—has inverted our originary balance of love and resentment toward the center, producing contemporary "white guilt" as a governing philosophy. This guilt pathologizes the center itself as the source of all injustice, creating an impossible burden of proof. GA proposes instead that marginal challengers of power bear the same accountability for their accusations as we demand from elected officials.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Center]]** — The sacred locus around which the originary community gathers; in modern politics, paradoxically empty or invisible
- **[[Resentment]]** — One of two primordial affects (alongside love) toward the center; historically amplified by Abrahamic revelation
- **[[White Guilt]]** — A contemporary governing philosophy that inverts the center's sacrality into pollution, parasitic on asymmetries of power
- **[[Victimary]]** — Discourses that locate sacrality in the victims of the center rather than the center itself
- **[[Reciprocity]]** — Mutual obligation and responsibility; the practical antidote to victimary logic and asymmetrical power
- **[[De-ritualization]]** — Historical process of eroding ritual authority, intensifying marginal resentment toward centralized power
- **[[Originary Intuitions]]** — Primordial egalitarian instincts rooted in the human origin; exploited by victimary philosophy

## Notable Passages

> "For white guilt, the center is guilty until proven innocent; and, moreover, the standard of proof is so high that it can never be met: ultimately the center's very centrality is what is most incriminating."

> "Just as the center is rightfully obliged to bear the burden of proof in charging individuals, so those of us on the margin should bear the burden of proof in bringing charges against the center."

> "Powerlessness is just as corrupting as power; bringing charges against the center is a bid for power, it should be recognized and welcomed as such, and should be encumbered with some form of accountability."

## Questions Raised

1. How can a modern polity recover genuine respect for the [[center]] without returning to pre-Abrahamic ritual authority? What would such recovery look like institutionally?

2. If victimary discourse is parasitic on asymmetries, does establishing [[reciprocity]] in obligation necessarily resolve the asymmetries themselves, or merely obscure them behind procedural fairness?

3. Can GA's principle of marginal accountability survive contact with actual power asymmetries, or does it risk becoming a tool for protecting established centers from legitimate critique?
