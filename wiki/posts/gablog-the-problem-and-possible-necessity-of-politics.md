## Summary

This post interrogates whether politics—conceived as a competitive arena for establishing rules before a qualified audience, without violence—remains necessary or has become an obstacle. The author argues that politics serves market societies by providing a center they cannot generate themselves and by representing estranged social groups. Yet he raises sharp diagnostic questions: Does politics genuinely aggregate citizen beliefs, or does the institution itself generate the conflicts it purports to manage? He suggests that examining voluntaristic orders and their "political" analogues might clarify whether we are condemned to perpetual revolution or can transcend politics altogether.

## Key Concepts

- [[Sacred space]] — The inviolable arena of political competition (state houses, public squares) held in common
- [[Sovereignty]] — Defense of the line distinguishing qualified political actors and audiences from the excluded
- [[Center]] — The organizing principle politics provides to market societies, which generate no center of their own
- [[Resentment]] — The antagonisms among estranged groups that politics may channel as a "safety valve" or artificially maintain
- [[Representation]] — The critical function of political actors reflecting cleavages within the audience and the meta-rules governing the space itself
- [[Market economy]] — The condition of estrangement that makes politics both necessary and perpetually unstable
- [[Voluntaristic order]] — A hypothetical social arrangement without imposed political structures, whose functional analogues remain to be theorized

## Notable Passages

> "Politics provides a center for a market society that generates no center of its own—is that center a needed supplement or an obstacle to the free development of the market order?"

> "Politics is most effective when the actors represent cleavages within the audience and reflect upon the meta- or constitutive rules governing the political space itself."

> "Even if one concludes that politics should be abolished, that wouldn't tell one what to do next—for one thing, you would then have to ask whether we 'can' do what we 'should' or even 'must.'"

## Questions Raised

1. Does the political institution genuinely neutralize resentment among estranged groups, or does it perpetually reproduce the antagonisms it claims to manage?

2. What would functional analogues to the political arena look like in a truly voluntaristic order, and could they emerge without institutional scaffolding?

3. Can we identify any substantive common issues that command universal ratification across an arbitrarily delineated territory, or does the search for such issues itself generate politics?
