## Summary

This essay argues that modern political and social discourse remains trapped in a "big scenic" imaginary inherited from ritual sacrifice—we unconsciously model all social concepts (justice, liberty, democracy) as if participants share a unified center and common sacrifice. The author contends that because the social center is now permanently occupied by a non-sacrificial entity (the state/market), all attempts to speak of collective "we" without acknowledging this structural reality perpetuate a destructive mythical thinking. Until we develop new practices and modes of inquiry that genuinely account for the occupied, sacrifice-resistant center, our inherited moral and political language will remain fundamentally mystified.

## Key Concepts

- [[Originary scene]]: The foundational human gathering around a sacred central object, establishing shared intentionality through sacrifice
- [[Big scenic imaginary]]: The persistent illusion that modern social groups share a unified center and direct distribution like primitive sacrifice
- [[Sacrificial center]]: The focal point around which social order originally organized; now permanently occupied and resistant to sacrificial resolution
- [[Mythical thinking]]: Unconscious modeling of social reality on primitive scenic logic, treating groups as unified agents
- [[Imperial center]]: The permanently occupied locus of power that cannot be sacrificed away, requiring transposition of all traditional concepts
- [[Transposition]]: The impossible task of mapping primitive sacrificial models onto modern decentralized social structures
- [[Classic style]]: The literary/cognitive mode that creates illusion of shared scene between writer and reader, naturalizing mythical thinking

## Notable Passages

"It is extremely difficult to speak about politics, history or social order without modeling these constructs on a scene upon which all are present."

"That we no longer share a sacrificial center, where distribution takes place directly and intentionally; that the social center is now permanently occupied by someone who cannot be sacrificed—after all these centuries this has still not registered."

"All of our social and political concepts—justice, liberty, equality, nature, democracy, right, and so on—share the same 'big scenic' imaginary, as if we were all imagining ourselves standing around a shared central object."

## Questions Raised

1. What would social and political discourse actually sound like if we abandoned the big scenic imaginary entirely—could we even communicate about collective action without it?

2. If the occupied center cannot be sacrificed, what practices or structures could genuinely replace the scapegoating mechanisms that historically resolved social conflict?

3. Does the author's critique apply equally to all modern ideologies (market capitalism, socialism, liberalism, conservatism), or do some acknowledge the occupied center more honestly than others?
