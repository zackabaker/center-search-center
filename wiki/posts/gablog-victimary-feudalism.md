## Summary

This piece traces how competitive displays of moral superiority have created a feudal hierarchy within victimary politics, where each group must continuously expose the "barbarism" lurking in others' civilizational claims to gain moral capital. The author argues that the very act of policing civilized behavior introduces new forms of barbarism through mimetic competition—a game with no stable rules, since any civilizational gradation can be retroactively exposed as naive. The result is an endless treadmill where claiming victimhood or moral standing instantly marks one as vulnerable to being outflanked by a newer, more vigilant claimant.

## Key Concepts

- [[Victimary]] — the competitive politics of victimhood and moral standing
- [[Civilizational gradation]] — the hierarchical distinctions of propriety and enlightenment that structure social behavior
- [[Mimetic competition]] — the tendency to compete over the same objects (here, moral capital) through mutual imitation
- [[Moral exhibitionism]] — conspicuous consumption of ethical superiority for social positioning
- [[Barbarism]] — transgression of civilizational boundaries, paradoxically produced by policing those boundaries
- [[Victimage hierarchy]] — unstable ranking of which oppressed groups deserve primacy

## Notable Passages

> "The policing of civilized behavior contains the elements of new forms of barbarism, in the form of competitive displays of moral exhibitionism (a form of conspicuous consumption)."

> "In order to introduce a new civilizational gradation one must take for granted the whole mass of gradations already sedimented within our civilized way of life—we are all, at some point, like that guy who says 'We treat women pretty damn well!'"

> "What is to be avoided at all costs (what makes one a loser) is to be exposed as naively accepting some civilized norm as unproblematically present."
