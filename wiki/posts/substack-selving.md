## Summary

This post synthesizes [[practice]] and [[hypothesis]] into the concept of "selving"—the continuous work of maintaining identity across time through institutional, linguistic, and scenic engagement. Drawing on MacIntyre and Paul North's logic of likeness, the author argues that the self is minimal (merely "the same") and constituted through repetitive articulations rather than metaphysical foundations. Selving represents a shift from analyzing isolated practices to understanding how language and discourse position us imperatively, turning all utterance into a scripture-like regime where meaning is institutional enactment.

## Key Concepts

- [[Practice]] — intrinsically normative, communally embedded action continuous with ritual; elicits responses from the center
- [[Ritual]] — foundational action; paired with myth to supplement its "failures" and generate hypotheses about center response
- [[Center]] — the source from which responses emerge that enable revision and articulation of practices
- [[Selving]] — the continuous work of maintaining sameness across difference; being "the same" across contexts
- [[Self]] — minimal term meaning only "the same"; maintained through names, social markers, and ongoing institutional enactment
- [[Likeness]] — the fundamental principle holding the universe together; everything is like everything else in some respect
- [[Institutional meaning]] — the enactment of meaning through historical and communal structures under scenic conditions

## Notable Passages

"To be a self is to be the same as you were in some other situation where you were also that self."

"Maintaining ones selving requires doing things to continue constructing those forms of sameness in a sea of differences...juggling a whole set of reference points."

"Turn all of language into scripture, as if it were nothing but a giant prayerbook. Not only should we always mean what we say, but we should only say the things that we can mean."

## Questions Raised

1. How does the shift from individual "practices" to integrated "selving" change our ethical obligations when we speak—do all utterances carry the weight of scripture?

2. If selves are maintained through institutional recognition (names, numbers, addresses), what happens to selving in conditions of institutional breakdown or revolutionary rupture?

3. Can North's logic of likeness be reconciled with the specificity required for meaningful institutional enactment, or do we need a concept of *privileged* likeness that [[center]] establishes?
