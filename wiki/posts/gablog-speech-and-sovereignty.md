## Summary

Effective speech reveals and repairs failures of [[reciprocity]] between interlocutors of asymmetrical power. The speaker who points out a breach—whether a soldier to a general or a rejected writer to an editor—performs a sovereign act by naming duties and insisting on accountability. All human speech fundamentally involves [[naming]] obligations and roles, thereby constructing a mode of sovereignty where power aligns with responsibility. This originary function of speech, rooted in the naming of God as the deferred object of desire, continuously reconstructs the institutional order that distances the community from [[mimetic desire]].

## Key Concepts

- [[Reciprocity]]: The mutual fulfillment of acknowledged obligations between parties, uncodified but embedded in social relations
- [[Sovereignty]]: A mode of authority wherein the sovereign intervenes proportionally to restore breached rights and duties
- [[Speech-act]]: Language that is revelatory and transformative when it points to reparable failures and demands accountability
- [[Naming]]: The fundamental human act of assigning titles and duties, building institutions through designation
- [[Asymmetry]]: The unequal positions or obligation-fulfillments between interlocutors that give weight to grievance
- [[Accountability]]: The expectation that power-holders must account for decisions and align nominal with actual power
- [[Mimetic desire]]: The intensifying rivalry over objects of shared desirability that institutional distance prevents

1. How does the demand for accountability through speech function as a democratizing force without collapsing hierarchical structures entirely?

2. If all words name God, how do we distinguish between genuine repairs of reciprocity and mere performative or manipulative uses of speech?

3. What institutional forms best embody this proportional sovereignty that responds to breaches without lapsing into either indifference or tyranny?
