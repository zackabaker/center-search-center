## Summary

This essay traces how the disintegration of sacral kingship forced Western civilization to recover the [[originary scene]] as the basis of social legitimacy. The author argues that true "History" emerges when the [[sacred center]] moves beyond the emperor himself to an external divine standard, creating permanent resentment toward actual empires. From this Axial Age innovation flows a permanent apocalyptic structure—the expectation of a final tyrant whose fall will vindicate the [[general will]]—that shapes both Marxism and liberalism. Even today, all political legitimacy rests on an imagined unanimity about the originary scene, a condition both necessary and dangerous.

## Key Concepts

- [[Originary scene]] – the foundational gesture of deferral around a central object that establishes social order
- [[Sacred center]] – the locus of communal unity; when it collapses, societies must reinvent their centers
- [[Axial Age]] – the period (roughly 800–200 BCE) when philosophy and monotheism created trans-imperial standards of judgment
- [[General will]] – the Rousseauian fiction of unanimous popular consent on which liberal legitimacy secretly rests
- [[Divine kingship]] – the claim that imperial authority is sacral; paradoxically anti-scenic because it removes the king from the actual scene
- [[Scenicity]] – the condition of being gathered around a shared center; necessary for non-violent deferral
- [[Imperial resentment]] – the permanent grievance installed by Axial Age thought against actual emperors
- [[Apocalyptic structure]] – the narrative form in which history moves toward vindication through the fall of tyrants

## Notable Passages

> "Going back to the scene is the only response to any social crisis: if the existing institutions and the totality of gestures they organize no longer defer violence, what else could there be to do other than discover some new gesture?"

> "The only acceptable God-Emperor would be God himself, who will rule once love of Him has been implanted in all human hearts by some revelation produced by the final, cataclysmic fall of increasingly evil empires."

> "Even leftists need to reference a unanimously held originary structure… there is no other way of thinking about liberal legitimacy than according to what is still a Rousseauian notion of the 'general will.'"

## Questions Raised

1. If unanimity on the originary scene is necessary for social stability, yet impossible to achieve authentically, is liberalism doomed to oscillate between fiction and violence?

2. How do we distinguish between healthy retrieval of [[scenicity]] (gathering around shared standards) and dangerous fundamentalism that claims to have recovered the "true" originary moment?

3. Does the author's analysis suggest that all political movements—left and right—are equally trapped in the Axial Age apocalyptic structure, or is there a way to think beyond it?
