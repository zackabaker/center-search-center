## Summary

Every group requires a mythicized founding event—a [[nomos]], or originary division of property—that establishes territorial and institutional boundaries. While land remains the most fundamental form of group property, modern groups may "own" constituencies, constituencies, or institutional domains. Group identity is perpetually renewed through imagined restoration of this original settlement, with internal disorder typically blamed on betrayal or misallocation within the community. This imaginative work need not distort reality, paralleling how the [[originary hypothesis]] itself remains conceptually sound despite its speculative character.

## Key Concepts

- [[Nomos]]: The originary division of cleared property establishing a group's foundational settlement and legal order
- [[Originary event]]: The mythicized founding moment through which groups establish collective identity and territorial claims
- [[Group identity]]: Sustained through imaginative restoration of the group's imagined founding order and internal allotment
- [[Territorial sovereignty]]: The fundamental claim groups maintain over land through mutual defense covenants
- [[Internal allotment]]: The distribution of rights and privileges within a group's domain
- [[Betrayal narrative]]: The attribution of nomic disorder to community members who have misused or manipulated their allotment

## Notable Passages

> "Every group has a more or less mythicized founding event, involving a 'nomos,' in Carl Schmitt's terms: an originary division of a property cleared away for the 'settlement' of the group."

> "Attempts to reaffirm the group's identity are always restorations of the imagined nomos, including a defense of territorial boundaries and form of internal allotment."

> "Members of groups must imagine themselves in their groups in these terms, whatever violence to reality must be done—but we need not assume that the imagined allotment always does violence to reality."

## Questions Raised

- How do modern groups that lack territorial land-claims (digital communities, activist networks) establish equivalent "nomos," and what substitutes function as their "cleared property"?
- To what extent can groups accurately restore their imagined founding without inevitably distorting historical reality, and does this distortion matter morally?
- When betrayal narratives blame internal disorder on community members, how can we distinguish genuine betrayal from scapegoating that reinforces group cohesion?
