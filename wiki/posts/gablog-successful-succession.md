## Summary

The ability to create successors is the measure of all meaningful practice and social order. By examining how [[center|centers]] transfer power between occupants, we can assess the health of any social system. The author challenges the ideology of "spontaneous organization" by showing that it serves anti-imperative interests seeking to obscure hierarchies of decision-making and diffuse [[sovereignty]] across unaccountable forces—media, diplomats, crowds—rather than admitting to human [[authority]] and responsibility.

## Key Concepts

- [[succession|Succession]]: The transfer of the [[center]] from one occupant to the next; the ultimate test of a practice's viability
- [[center]]: The focal point of social order whose occupancy and transmission structure the entire social system
- [[spontaneous organization]]: The ideological claim that complex events arise from unmappable causes rather than human decision-making
- [[declarative culture]]: A system in which speech and declaration constitute reality; anti-imperative forms obscure power hierarchies
- [[practice]]: Any structured activity that can be perfected and extended through maintenance and meta-practices
- [[authority]]: Human responsibility for decision-making, obscured by spontaneity rhetoric
- [[subjectivity]]: The maintenance of one's capacity to control and distinguish what is within versus outside one's sphere of action

## Notable Passages

> "Everywhere, succession is the sign of success. Whatever you do or say is meaningful and important insofar as you create the place for and when possible install your successor. A 'childless' practice is a failure."

> "The purpose of the argument is to subvert clear lines of decision making and to give power to precisely all the spontaneous elements you cite—the media, the diplomats, crowds, and so on."

> "You can't control your dreams, but you can prevent your dreams from interfering with your management of your household."

## Questions Raised

1. How do we identify which practices have genuinely created successors versus those that merely appear to have done so through institutional mimicry?

2. If maintaining subjectivity requires distinguishing what we can and cannot control, does this framework risk becoming a justification for passivity in domains where control is difficult but morally necessary?

3. Can the succession principle apply equally to intellectual, aesthetic, and political practices, or does it function differently across these domains?
