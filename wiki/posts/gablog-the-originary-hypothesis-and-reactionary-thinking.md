## Summary

This essay argues that the [[originary hypothesis]] provides a more rigorous foundation for reactionary political thought than traditional Platonic or evolutionary frameworks. Rather than relying on vague concepts like "virtue" or "the good," the author proposes "discipline" (understood as systematized [[deferral]]) as the dynamic organizing principle of both civilization and political order. The essay contends that [[market society]] itself exemplifies discipline—the capacity to defer immediate gratification and violent impulse—and that reactionary thinking rooted in originary theory can theorize power and governance more precisely than Gans's own liberal reading of GA.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Originary hypothesis]]**: The founding myth of generative anthropology; explains human emergence through the first scene of representation.
- **[[Deferral]]**: The fundamental human capacity to delay immediate gratification or violent action; the basis of civilization.
- **[[Discipline]]**: Self-conscious, systematized deferral understood as dynamic praxis rather than static quality.
- **[[Market society]]**: A civilizational form organized around deferral of both economic gratification and violent behavior.
- **[[Power]]**: The capacity emerging from disciplined action, which can paradoxically undermine its own conditions when successfully deployed.
- **[[Reactionary thinking]]**: Political thought favoring rule by the disciplined and capable, compatible with (not opposed to) GA foundations.

## Notable Passages

> "Discipline is just an extension of deferral—it is self-conscious, systematized deferral."

> "Everything that we do, every thought and action, is a mode of deferral, and why not stick with the most fundamental concept and use it to reconstruct the more complex ones?"

> "An advanced market system encourages its own form of indiscipline insofar as success in the marketplace yields power which can then be used to intervene in the marketplace in all kinds of ways that undermines one's own discipline."

## Questions Raised

1. If discipline and deferral are the fundamental organizing principles of civilization, what mechanisms prevent advanced market societies from collapsing into the "indiscipline" that success itself generates?

2. Can reactionary thinking rooted in the originary hypothesis avoid descending into the same aristocratic platitudes it critiques, or does the shift to "discipline" as the key term genuinely provide new analytical precision?

3. How does the author's formulation account for the cultural production of what counts as "discipline" across different civilizational moments—is discipline trans-historical or historically contingent?
