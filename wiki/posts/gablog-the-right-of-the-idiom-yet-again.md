## Summary

This essay explores [[idiom]] as the articulation of group membership around a [[sacred center]], distinguished by the anthropological awareness that one's own signs could be otherwise—a reflexivity that doesn't escape the [[mimetic]] logic it observes but rather interrupts it. The author argues that idioms sharpen rather than dissolve their differences from equivalent idioms in other groups, and that idiomatic richness grows through the strategic incorporation of outside influences. The text concludes by mapping three economic modes—material division, [[gift economy]], and market exchange—as parallel systems that coexist rather than simply replace one another historically.

## Key Concepts

- [[Idiom]]: The articulation of group membership, the sharing of a sacred center, and its anthropological "surplus" or awareness of contingency
- [[Sacred center]]: The shared focus around which a community organizes its signs and identity
- [[Mimetic]] / [[mimesis]]: The uncritical investment in one's community's conflicts; the human tendency to imitate and identify
- [[Gift economy]]: A mode of exchange based on reciprocal obligation and deferral, distinct from market and material division
- [[Anthropological insight]]: The capacity to step back from one's own idiom and recognize its equivalence with other idioms
- [[Market exchange]]: The universal medium of money enabling abstract equivalence across idiomatic boundaries
- [[Individual style]]: The proliferation of distinct voices within an idiom, enriching its specificity

## Notable Passages

> "The preservation of an idiom, moreover, depends upon sharpening the differences between equivalents rather than ironing them out—the attempt to create more general signs would really just be the process of creating new groups."

> "A very good example is the copious wealth of Renaissance English, with its avaricious devouring of Latin, Italian and French influence... establishing national unity and devising a specifically Anglican form of Christianity."

> "We are never done, once and for all, with any of these economic systems"—all three modes (division, gift, market) remain active simultaneously in human life.

## Questions Raised

1. If anthropological reflexivity about one's idiom is itself a form of group-making (the anthropologists vs. the merely mimetic), does increased self-awareness necessarily protect against [[ressentiment]] or does it merely displace it?

2. How does the market's claim to transcend idiomatic difference relate to the creation of new idioms that speak "in the name of humanity"—are they the same mechanism?

3. What determines whether an idiom grows richer through incorporating outside influences versus being diluted or absorbed by them?
