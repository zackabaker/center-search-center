## Summary

This essay traces David Olson's account of how writing transformed human cognition by enabling the representation of others' speech through metalinguistic markers rather than mimetic imitation. The author extends Olson's insight to argue that oral cultures were comprehensively mimetic—members constantly inhabited multiple identities by imitating ancestral voices, the dead, and divine beings. Writing's emergence of a metalanguage paradoxically reveals that all disciplinary discourse, despite claiming objectivity, can be redescribed as scenic actions rooted in the [[originary scene]]. Mimesis, far from diminishing with language, intensifies through ritual reenactment, generating new [[mimetic crises]] that demand renewed originary resolutions.

## Key Concepts

- [[Metalanguage of literacy]]: The systematic discourse markers (claimed, exclaimed, contended) that allow writers to report others' speech while maintaining evaluative distance
- [[Originary scene]]: The primordial moment of language emergence through deferred violence and collective designation of a central object
- [[Mimesis]]: The foundational human capacity for imitation, intensified rather than reduced by language and ritual
- [[Scenic representation]]: The reduction of all intellectual discourse to observable stances and actions on a scene
- [[Oral culture]]: A social order organized as continuous theatrical performance, where identity is distributed across channeled voices
- [[Semantic primes]]: The elemental verbs (say, think, see, hear) from which all disciplinary vocabulary ultimately derives

## Notable Passages

"Each member of the group must inhabit dozens of identities daily—imagine the skill in mimicry each must develop as a result."

"The sign itself only 'works' as a kind of meta-mimetic gesture, since it's an imitation of the aggressive, appropriative advance toward the central object while also needing to be imitated itself by all members of the group."

"The emergence of language and reality would reveal the power of imitation to the new group, and quickly increase its members' mimetic capacities."

## Questions Raised

1. If all disciplinary discourse can be redescribed as scenic action, what prevents the metalanguage of literacy from collapsing back into mimetic performance? What maintains the distinction between representing a scene and enacting it?

2. How do we account for the apparent increase in [[mimetic crises]] within literate cultures if writing provides metalinguistic tools for managing representation? Does literacy intensify rather than resolve mimetic conflict?

3. What is the relationship between the "theatrical production" of oral identity-distribution and modern subjectivity? Have we truly escaped the requirement to inhabit multiple voices, or merely rendered it invisible through written abstraction?
