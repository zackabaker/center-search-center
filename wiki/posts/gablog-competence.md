## Summary

The essay traces how the Left weaponized "competence" as a political criterion, exploiting the media's selective documentation of Republican mistakes while shielding Democratic administrations from similar scrutiny. This reflects a deeper Progressive philosophy rooted in John Dewey—the application of scientific method to governance—which presumes that expert administration legitimates power. The author argues this creates an inherent asymmetry: conservatives resist expanded government, making them vulnerable to incompetence charges, while progressives gain legitimacy by appearing scientifically managed, regardless of actual results.

## Key Concepts

- [[Progressivism|Progressivism]]: Political philosophy that applies scientific method and expert administration to social problem-solving
- [[Expert Administration]]: Governance model dependent on specialized knowledge and technocratic legitimacy
- [[Declarative Culture]]: The assertion of ideological frameworks (here, "science") to establish authority without empirical proof
- [[Victimocracy]]: The political system that leverages selective narratives of harm and failure to gain power
- [[Conservative Governance]]: Political approach preferring limited government action over optimized administration
- [[Unintended Consequences]]: The gap between planned social outcomes and actual results when experiments meet complex reality
- [[Media Bias]]: The structural preference for narratives that undermine certain political actors over others

## Notable Passages

"If you see yourself, as an elected or appointed official, as akin to an engineer or doctor, then the number of serious mistakes becomes an important measure of your performance."

"Democracy, in that case, would depend upon the scientific method coming to replace traditional common sense in the public as a whole."

"The law of unintended consequences... dictates that the experiment itself will transform, in all kinds of unpredictable ways, the conditions that were to be tested in the first place."

## Questions Raised

1. If expert administration cannot be truly measured against objective standards (as the author suggests), what actually legitimates progressive governance—and is this gap itself a form of [[Declarative Culture]]?

2. How does the asymmetry between Republican and Democratic "competence" narratives reflect deeper differences in how conservative and progressive movements relate to institutional power and expansion?

3. Can the scientific method meaningfully apply to social life without collapsing into either technocratic overreach or the very "tradition and prejudice" it claims to supersede?
