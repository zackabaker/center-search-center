## Summary

This essay traces the progression from [[defilement]] through [[sin]] to [[guilt]] as a deepening of moral consciousness—from externally imposed ritual prohibitions to internalized ethical standards. The author argues that modernity's attempt to eliminate defilement discourse has failed catastrophically, evident in both totalitarian regimes and contemporary "White Guilt." Rather than dismissing defilement as irrational, the text proposes understanding it as a proper response to collective trauma, and suggests that [[error]] on the [[originary scene]] offers a framework for addressing the inexpressibility of defilement without requiring rational negotiation.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Defilement]]** – pollution of communal space through violation of prohibition, regardless of intent; requires ritual cleansing
- **[[Sin]]** – deliberate violation of divine command; marks the emergence of individual will and intentionality
- **[[Guilt]]** – internalized self-judgment according to shared but individually embodied standards of probity
- **[[Originary Scene]]** – the primordial moment of human meaning-making where all subsequent human phenomena are implicitly present
- **[[White Guilt]]** – the contemporary manifestation of defilement discourse, mysteriously contagious and resistant to rational critique
- **[[Error]]** – simultaneous emergence with [[norm]] on the originary scene; a blameless deviation that exposes the fragility of shared meaning
- **[[Compact Society]]** – primitive social formations organized around defilement; invoked by totalitarian regimes in revolt against market order

## Notable Passages

> "If everything human can be found, albeit implicitly or potentially, on the originary scene, it follows that nothing found on the originary scene is ever lost."

> "The power of White Guilt is also the power of defilement—otherwise, it would not have proven so 'contagious' so as to 'contaminate' even those (Western) countries at war with Nazism."

> "A sense of defilement...is a perfectly proper response to the crime of genocide—it is denying that sense, then, that is 'irrational.'"

## Questions Raised

1. If defilement is ineliminable from the [[originary scene]], can modernity ever successfully bracket it without producing the very violence it seeks to prevent?

2. How does recognizing [[error]] as coeval with [[norm]] transform our ethical response to collective guilt—does it dissolve responsibility or deepen it?

3. What would "public expression" of defilement that resists rational negotiation actually look like in democratic discourse?
