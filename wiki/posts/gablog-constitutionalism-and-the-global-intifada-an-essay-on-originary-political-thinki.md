## Summary

This essay applies generative anthropology to diagnose the collapse of constitutional order in the face of what the author calls the "global intifada"—a worldwide victimary uprising that uses declarative power to dismantle traditional sovereignty structures. The text argues that constitutionalism depends on a shared [[originary scene]] of deferral and reciprocity, but that this foundation has been eroded by a culture of resentment that weaponizes victimhood claims. Only a return to [[centering]] as an originary practice can restore political legitimacy and prevent the slide into dedifferentiation and chaos.

## Key Concepts

- [[Originary scene]]: The primordial moment of human linguistic and social origin; the model for all legitimate political founding
- [[Declarative culture]]: Power exercised through utterance and representation rather than force; the modern dominant mode
- [[Victimocracy]]: Political regime structured around competing claims to victim status rather than principles of reciprocity
- [[Sovereignty]]: The centered capacity to defer violence and establish reciprocal order
- [[Centering]]: The originary act of deferral around a central object; prerequisite for any stable community
- [[Mimetic crisis]]: Escalating conflict born from undifferentiated rivalry; the pathology constitutional order must prevent
- [[Resentment]]: The emotional fuel of victimary claims; fundamentally incompatible with reciprocal exchange

## Notable Passages

"Constitutionalism presupposes a shared memory of an originary moment in which violence is deferred through the creation of a central authority."

"The global intifada succeeds because it operates within the declarative register—the realm of representation and utterance—where victimocracy is the native tongue."

"Without centering, there is only the infinite regress of rival resentments, each claiming priority as the most wounded."
