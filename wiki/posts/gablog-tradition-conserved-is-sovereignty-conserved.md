# Tradition Conserved is Sovereignty Conserved

## Summary

This post argues that tradition cannot be reduced to "culture" or "identity" as spontaneous bottom-up phenomena, but rather must be understood through the [[originary scene]] as an ongoing dialectic between [[ritual]] performance and [[discourse]]. Drawing on [[Eric Gans]]'s work, the author contends that all tradition conserves memory of the originary deferral of violence through the sign. The tension between ritual's strict adherence and discourse's interpretive flexibility enables tradition's continuity. Finally, the rise of central power created an unhealed wound by destroying egalitarian sacred communities while recuperating tradition through state divinity.

## Key Concepts

- [[Originary scene]] – The primordial event where violence is deferred through the aborted appropriative gesture, founding human culture
- [[Ritual and discourse]] – The twin dimensions of tradition: performance/symbolic action versus interpretive reflection
- [[Sovereignty]] – The center that is always already occupied; politics precedes and determines culture, not vice versa
- [[Absolutism]] – Political ordering that places the center first, with all marginal activity already accounted for by central authority
- [[Ostensive gesture]] – The originary sign pointing toward a sacred center; requires ritual organization to function meaningfully
- [[Sacred center]] – The locus around which egalitarian communities organized their commemorative practices before the rise of the Big Man

## Notable Passages

> "All tradition is wholly indebted to some tradition or traditions enabling that activity. The most fundamental of traditions is, of course, language, which no individual could have invented alone."

> "Ritual involves performance, symbolic action, and the ostensive gesture... Each tradition, in its own idiosyncratic way, re-enacts the originary event, where violence was deferred through the issuance of the aborted gesture of appropriation."

> "What enables the continuity of tradition is an ongoing dialectic of ritual and discourse, such that the discourse of the community is sufficiently rich in referents to the rituals, and the rituals sufficiently open to discursive accretions."

## Questions Raised

1. How can we distinguish between genuinely conserved tradition and the manufactured "traditions" (like Victorian Christmas) that represent elite manipulation? Does the ritual-discourse dialectic have self-correcting mechanisms?

2. What would a non-traumatized relationship between sacred center and central power look like, and is the "wound" the author mentions historically reversible?

3. If bottom-up activity is always already instrumental to competing elite factions, what grounds the possibility of authentic cultural innovation or resistance?
