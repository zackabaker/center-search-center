## Summary

This essay argues that all human social orders—from primitive kingships to modern liberal democracy—organize themselves around a [[center]], regardless of whether that center is sacred, human, or diffuse. Rather than nostalgically seeking lost "golden ages" of absolute rule, we should recognize that power has never been fully secure and that all historical forms reveal different trajectories of [[resentment]] between center and periphery. The essay traces how [[individuality]] itself emerges from the capacity to imagine oneself as a potential center, and how the [[Axial Age]] attempted to distribute centrality throughout society rather than concentrate it. The fundamental human project is not recovering lost legitimacy but understanding the evolving relationship between communities and their centers.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Center]]** – The focal point of social organization around which mimetic desire, resentment, and meaning cohere; can be sacred object, ruler, or distributed across individuals
- **[[Originary Scene]]** – The hypothesized moment when mimetic fear generates the first sign, establishing the center's power to impose restraint
- **[[Resentment]]** – The ambivalent affect binding periphery to center; simultaneously constrains and enhances the center's power
- **[[Individuality]]** – The capacity to imagine oneself as a center and to direct vectors of attention toward and away from oneself
- **[[Mimetic Desire]]** – The fundamental human dynamic in which desire is modeled on another's desire, generating fear and the need for a restraining center
- **[[Axial Age]]** – The historical period that exposed the incompatibility of mass sacrifice with civilized order and attempted to distribute centrality universally
- **[[Big Man]]** – The figure who radically appropriates the center through resentment, becoming himself the object of resentment and source of meaning

## Notable Passages

"Even a horrible center is better than trying to imagine its absence. In that case, we can reverse our perspective and replace any lingering nostalgia for ideal kingdoms of the past and see ourselves as participating in the single project that encompasses all of humanity, the revelation of the commands of the center."

"The originary relation to the center is, of course, that of the hypothesized originary scene, in which mimetic desire generates the mimetic fear that issues in the issuing of the sign modeled on the central object's vulnerability and power to impose restraint."

"To be an individual is to imagine vectors of attention directed toward oneself, and to imagine oneself arranging those vectors, drawing in some, deflecting others."

## Questions Raised

1. If all centers are flawed and resentment is constitutive of the center-periphery relation, what would distinguish a "good" center from a merely functional one?

2. How does the distribution of centrality in the [[Axial Age]] and modern liberal societies actually prevent the re-concentration of power, or does it merely obscure new forms of hidden centers?

3. Does the essay's claim that individuality emerges from imagining oneself as a potential center align with or challenge contemporary experiences of atomization and the loss of shared centers?
