## Summary

Ergodism names the primacy of the event over the symbolic—the recognition that all formalizable discourse, no matter how purely symbolic, ultimately depends on originary scenes and institutional contexts that generate meaning. The author proposes a pedagogical method of unfolding entire discourses from single sentences by making explicit their nested implications, which mirrors the [[originary hypothesis]] itself. Drawing on oral poetic tradition's characteristics of modularity, iterability, and collective composition, ergodism repositions the symbolic as merely a form of deferral rather than the ground of meaning.

## Key Concepts

- [[originary hypothesis]]: The founding event-statement from which all discourse unfolds, including those claiming universalizable rules
- [[declarative sentence]]: The symbolic form that defers the ostensive and imperative, elevating abstraction over presence
- [[event-statement]]: A singular, intra-referential utterance whose implications extend infinitely when properly articulated
- [[absolute language]]: Purely symbolic language emptied of representation and reference, yet still dependent on scenes and institutions
- [[deferral]]: The mechanism by which symbolic systems postpone direct meaning, creating the appearance of autonomy
- [[modularity]]: The recombinable units of oral tradition that enable variation while maintaining recognizability
- [[iterability]]: The requirement that meaning be continuously recomposed rather than fixed, preserving it through performance

## Notable Passages

"Discourses founded on formalizable, universalizable rules are themselves only particular variants of such event-statements, those that effected a more stringent form of deferral, under specific conditions."

"The primacy of the event over the symbolic, whereby the symbolic is simply a form of deferral, is what I will call 'ergodism.'"

"Poems are combinations of performed units that can be put together variously; any two different works in a tradition will tend to have many of these units in common."

## Questions Raised

1. If all symbolic systems ultimately rest on specific scenes and institutions, what distinguishes a "transparent, self-explaining" discourse from one that merely conceals its originary dependencies?

2. How does the ergodic principle of iterability—requiring continuous recomposition—preserve coherence across performances without collapsing into pure relativism?

3. Can the teaching method proposed here actually generate knowledge, or does unfolding implications from a sentence merely make explicit what was already implicitly contained, and if so, what ontological status does such explication have?
