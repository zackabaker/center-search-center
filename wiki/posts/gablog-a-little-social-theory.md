## Summary

This post argues that social thinking must ground itself in basic [[experiential categories]]—center-margin, vertical-horizontal, sign-object, inside-outside—that are rooted in human embodiment and gesture. These categories, metaphorically extended through language, become the fundamental vocabulary for describing both the [[sacred]] realm and social hierarchies. The author suggests that all social and intellectual change can be understood as transformations in how these categories are articulated and prioritized in discourse, with [[differentiation]] creating new levels of reality through their continual involution.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Experiential categories]]** — Basic spatial and orientational pairs (center/margin, vertical/horizontal, visible/invisible) rooted in embodied human perception
- **[[Sacred]]** — The transcendental realm that can only be described through metaphorical extensions of basic experiential terms
- **[[Differentiation]]** — The continual articulation and involution of experiential categories across time, creating new levels of reality and complexity
- **[[Social hierarchy]]** — Vertical orderings (literal and metaphorical) that overlap with spiritual and ethical hierarchies
- **[[Metaphorical extension]]** — The process by which physical, bodily categories become conceptual tools for describing value, ethics, and social organization
- **[[Margin]]** — The position from which one faces and orients toward the center, fundamental to all social thinking

## Notable Passages

> "The basic experiential terms are our only source for describing the sacred and transcendental realm, and consequently the terms in which we sacralize and describe reality."

> "If external or visible elevation can conceal internal or invisible degradation, then external or visible degradation can conceal internal or invisible elevation."

> "What Eric Voegelin called 'differentiation' as opposed to 'compactness' is, I think, nothing more than this continual involution and articulation of these categories."

## Questions Raised

1. If all social thought reduces to manipulations of basic experiential categories, what determines which categories become prominent in a given historical moment—material conditions, linguistic evolution, or something else?

2. How do we distinguish between genuinely new categories emerging through differentiation versus merely rediscovering and recombining the same original set?

3. Can post-imperial societies ever escape the categories generated by imperial orders, or are they forever reactive to the hierarchies they reject?
