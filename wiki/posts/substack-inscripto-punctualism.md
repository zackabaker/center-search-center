## Summary

This essay traces "data" back to its origins in [[inscription]] and [[ostensive]] pointing, arguing that contemporary data-driven culture inherits the priest's obsession with ritual precision. Rather than treating data as "given," we must recognize it as [[capta]]—what we actively "take" from the world through our modes of attention. The author contends that in an age where deception becomes increasingly difficult to hide, the ethical imperative lies in establishing reciprocal human-machine relations that continuously verify the fidelity between what is done, seen, and said.

## Key Concepts

- [[Inscription]]: Materialized commemoration; the foundational act of marking that enables iteration and recognition
- [[Ostensive]]: The pointing gesture that establishes shared reference; basis for all subsequent linguistic orders
- [[Capta]]: Data understood not as "given" but as actively "taken" from the world through selective attention
- [[Spillage]]: Unintended consequences and revelations that leak from attempts to maintain social orders
- [[Declarative Culture]]: The imperative to establish ascertainable relations between what is done, seen, and said
- [[Chain of Custody]]: The protocols ensuring provenance and integrity of information through systems of verification
- [[Anthropomorphic Data]]: Information structured to be mutually intelligible between humans and machines

## Notable Passages

"There cannot be a human order that doesn't need to place things at the center and say what it is in such a way that can be iterated."

"It's getting harder to fake, lie and conceal...how much of that despicable activity results from the difficulty of covering up previous instances of it?"

"The optimal human-machine relation is that increasingly well-trained humans program machines to collect and automatically analyze data so as to present it back to those humans as reciprocal, anthropomorphic data collectors."

## Questions Raised

1. If [[spillage]] from social orders becomes increasingly visible and difficult to conceal, what new forms of deception emerge that exploit transparency itself as camouflage?

2. How do we distinguish between [[chain of custody]] protocols that genuinely restore ethical rigor and those that merely simulate precision while obscuring deeper questions of judgment and wisdom?

3. What happens to human responsibility and moral agency when we delegate the verification of fidelity (between doing, seeing, and saying) to automated systems trained on contaminated data?
