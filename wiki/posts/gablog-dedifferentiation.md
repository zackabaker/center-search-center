## Summary

This essay traces how the collapse of [[sacred kingship]] necessitated the differentiation of human experience into distinct disciplinary spheres—logic, ethics, aesthetics, politics—each emerging to address problems the sacred order could no longer contain. The rise of the [[tyrant]] as a destabilizing figure occupying the center demanded new frameworks of knowledge and institutional safeguards. Modernity, understood through this lens, is fundamentally organized around preventing anyone from feeling at home in the center, requiring a paradoxical population trained in both obedience and resentment toward authority.

## Key Concepts

- **[[Sacred Order]]** — A pre-modern hierarchical system where legitimacy derives from ritual conformity and divine sanction, leaving no formal space for recognized conflict.

- **[[Differentiation]]** — The modern separation of human experience into autonomous disciplinary spheres (aesthetics, ethics, logic, politics) corresponding to institutional distinctions.

- **[[Tyrant]]** — A figure who occupies the center through manipulation of class conflict rather than sacred legitimacy; a problem rather than a solution.

- **[[Center Occupation]]** — The fundamental political problem: who legitimately occupies the central authority position, and how to prevent permanent settlement there.

- **[[Revolving Center]]** — The modern institutional solution: temporary, conditional, supervised occupancy of power ensuring no ruler feels secure.

- **[[Disciplines]]** — Formal bodies of knowledge (philosophy, rhetoric, logic) that emerge to answer questions the sacred guardians can no longer address.

- **[[Rule-Governed Resentment]]** — The paradoxical psychological state modern subjects must inhabit: obedience to impersonal rules combined with romantic resentment toward those rules.

## Notable Passages

> "Once these conflictual models become the dominant ones, the ruler can no longer be the one who occupies the sacred center. The king is replaced by the tyrant, the inventor of the method of levying the 'low' against the 'middle.'"

> "Modernity is predicated not on helping the occupant of the center govern, but on ensuring he never feels at home there."

> "The vocation of the disciplines, then, is to train a population that can sustain the revolving center...an odd combination of self-reliance, insistence on self-origination, adherence to bureaucratic rules, resentment toward anyone whose life seems less strictly rule-governed."

## Questions Raised

1. If differentiation emerged to solve the "tyrant problem," what happens when the very disciplines meant to constrain center-occupiers become instruments of tyranny themselves?

2. How does the internet's flattening of institutional hierarchies challenge the "revolving center" model—does it eliminate the center or multiply centers?

3. Can the resentment built into modern rule-governance be discharged without destroying the differentiated order itself?
