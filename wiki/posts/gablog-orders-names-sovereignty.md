## Summary

The author proposes that post-sacral kingship history represents humanity's sustained attempt to reproduce the radiating centrality of the sacred ruler while eliminating the violent convergence toward the center that threatens every social order. Drawing on [[originary scene]] theory, the text argues that the fundamental problem of politics is securing power without requiring periodic sacrifice of the center itself—a problem democracy attempts to solve through ritualized rotation rather than genuine stability. The essay traces this dilemma to the minimal hierarchy necessarily present in the originary sign, suggesting that all subsequent political forms grapple with tensions between centralizing order and deferring mimetic violence.

## Key Concepts

- [[Sacral kingship]] – The pre-modern form of absolute [[sovereignty]] whose collapse defines post-sacred political history
- [[Originary scene]] – The hypothetical moment when advanced mimetic capacity created violent convergence toward a central object, requiring the [[sign]] to restore order
- [[Mimetic crisis]] – The escalating competition for a central object that threatens to dissolve social hierarchy
- [[Centrality]] – The radiating reference point that structures all social activity and political order
- [[Sign]] – The aborted gesture of appropriation that arrests mimetic violence and founds community
- [[Minimal hierarchy]] – The necessary asymmetry present even in the originary scene, preceding all institutional forms
- [[Sovereignty]] – The capacity to maintain order; persistently insecure after the fall of sacral kingship
- [[Democracy]] – Modernity's attempt to satisfy the need for a central reference point while permitting controlled elimination of the occupant

## Notable Passages

"We want a king that is a permanent reference point for all social activity without feeling compelled to lynch him, or anyone else taking on a vaguely king-like role, as a way of resolving every social crisis."

"One member would have had to have gone first without, we must assume, completely realizing what he has done until the others, successively, followed his example in a kind of arrest and reversal of mimetic rivalry."

"The minimal hierarchy is repeated, discovered, and resisted until one individual is capable of taking over the center—a pattern that defines post-sacred political history."

## Questions Raised

1. Can [[minimal hierarchy]] ever be genuinely transparent to participants, or does successful order require that the asymmetry at the scene's origin remain obscured?

2. If the [[originary scene]] required one member to gesture first "without completely realizing," does this suggest that all political innovation requires a kind of originary amnesia—founders who cannot fully account for their own founding gestures?

3. Is the problem of "unsecure power" inherent to the structure of the [[sign]] itself, or could a political form emerge that solves it by accepting [[centrality]] without demanding its periodic renewal through sacrifice?
