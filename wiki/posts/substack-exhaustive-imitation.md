## Summary

This essay argues for a rigorous, exhaustive application of [[mimetic theory]] that accounts for *all* imitative behavior, not just the crisis-laden rivalry cases Girard emphasizes. The author contends that theorists must candidly anatomize their own [[subject position]]—which models they're imitating, how they're distorting them—rather than claiming autonomous individuality. Learning and cultural difference emerge as vast fields obscured when mimetic theory focuses narrowly on imitation-leading-to-rivalry. Authenticity, paradoxically, emerges through conscious adoption and "mis-taking" of adult models tested in unexpected situations.

## Key Concepts

- [[Mimesis]]: The imitative dimension of all human action, both unconscious (discovered post-facto) and deliberate (acquiring power from models)
- [[Subject position]]: The theorist's own location within mimetic chains; what or whom they are imitating and must acknowledge
- [[Crisis]]: The convergence of mimetic rivalry that Girard emphasizes, but which the author argues accounts for only a tiny fraction of imitative acts
- [[Mis-taking]]: Productive distortion or falsification of a model in the process of learning; getting imitation "wrong" creatively
- [[Authenticity]]: Not autonomy but successful synthesis and testing of adopted models in novel situations
- [[Exhaustive]] (methodology): Accounting for all cases of mimesis, not selecting only dramatic or conflictual ones
- [[Cultural difference]]: Opened as a field of inquiry when attention shifts from crisis-mimesis to the vast background of ordinary imitations

## Notable Passages

"Much imitation, maybe, in a sense, all imitation, involves getting imitation wrong—falsifying or distorting the model. That question opens up a vast field of inquiry into learning, first of all, and also into cultural difference."

"I read to acquire language, and you acquire language through immersion which means trying out all the implications of a particular way of thinking until you've exhausted it and to some extent it has become part of your repertoire."

"Insofar as I've adopted and mis-taken adult models and apprehended their modes of operation, testing them in unanticipated situations, yes, I've got my own self and have grown up."

## Questions Raised

1. If all theorizing is imitation, how does the exhaustive anatomizing of one's models avoid infinite regress—who models the modeler?
2. What distinguishes productive "mis-taking" (learning) from mere plagiarism or intellectual dishonesty in adopting models?
3. How might mapping the ordinary, non-crisis imitations that constitute most of culture reshape our understanding of civilization's stability and change?
