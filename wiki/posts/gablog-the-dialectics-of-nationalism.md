# The Dialectics of Nationalism

## Summary

Nationalism generates internal contradictions that undermine its own stated commitments. While nationalists demand non-intervention abroad, this anti-interventionism is a luxury of the politically marginal; successful nationalist powers inevitably intervene. The comparison between nations creates hierarchies of success and failure, which spawns emulation and resentment in less successful nations, driving migration, cultural transformation, and enduring interstate conflict. National boundaries never cleanly align with ethnic or linguistic populations, creating perpetual instability that nationalism cannot resolve.

## Key Concepts

- [[nationalism]]: Political commitment to national sovereignty, coherence, and competition with other nations
- [[political imaginary]]: The necessary fictions and paradoxes that sustain any political order
- [[resentment]]: The affect generated in less successful nations that identify themselves against more successful ones
- [[sovereignty]]: The paradoxical demand that one's own nation be respected while other nations face strategic intervention
- [[ethnic homogeneity]]: An illusion nationalism pursues but never achieves, given vague and gradational ethnic boundaries
- [[imperialism]]: The strategic dominance exercised by more successful nations (MSN) over less successful ones (LSN)

## Notable Passages

> "To be a nationalist is to define your nation in comparison to and competition with other nations. It is to assume that there are more and less successful nations."

> "Anti-interventionism is also an illusion, one that can be indulged in by the politically marginalized, but will be abandoned quickly if the nationalists ever wield significant political power."

> "National boundaries are always imperfect, with people who identify more with one nation being 'trapped' within the boundaries of another nation."
