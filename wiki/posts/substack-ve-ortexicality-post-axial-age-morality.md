## Summary

This post reclaims "from each according to his abilities, to each according to his needs" as a post-Axial Age moral principle fundamentally distinct from communist ideology. Rather than egalitarian, the principle is explicitly inegalitarian and scales flexibly to match differential needs and capacities. Reconceived as "stacked reciprocity," it can function as the credit system binding a juridical order, replacing the vulnerable premise of legal equality with a more realistic acknowledgment of hierarchy. Property itself is reframed as a form of center-issued credit—a charter whose terms structure community participation.

## Key Concepts

- [[Stacked reciprocity]]: A scalable, inegalitarian principle distributing resources based on needs and abilities rather than equal shares
- [[Post-Axial Age morality]]: Ethics explicitly grounded in hierarchy rather than the universalism implicit in Axial religions
- [[Juridical order]]: A system of law and property sustained by credit relationships and deferred reciprocity
- [[Center]]: The source from which property and credit originate as distributed forms of authority
- [[Declarative culture]]: The cultural forms that materialize and tokenize shared moral obligations into assignable claims

## Notable Passages

> "Unlike more explicitly reciprocal moralities like 'do unto others,' the outflow of abilities and the inflow to meet needs can scale up as much as necessary."

> "Property is an advance, from the center, of a particular amount and form of credit, which can include the use of territory and various apparatuses. This is nothing more than a charter, the terms of which are laid out in advance."

> "I'm assuming that post-Axial Age morality will be explicitly inegalitarian, starting off by making explicit the tacit inegalitarianism of any post-Big Man order."

## Questions Raised

1. **How does "stacked reciprocity" prevent exploitation of the able?** If the center unilaterally defines both "needs" and "abilities," what constrains abusive redistribution or conscription?

2. **Can property-as-charter survive without appeal to something beyond law?** If juridical orders are purely credit systems, what prevents the dissolution of obligation when confidence breaks down?

3. **What distinguishes post-Axial explicitness about hierarchy from mere fascism?** Does naming inegalitarianism openly actually strengthen justice, or merely legitimize domination?
