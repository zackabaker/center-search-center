## Summary

This note proposes a methodological bridge between generative anthropology and social science by treating all human phenomena as [[event]]s rather than static structures. The author argues that social scientists implicitly rely on [[originary event]]s embedded in their foundational concepts—"society," "mind," "ritual"—but fail to recognize this originary dimension. By asking social scientists to hypothesize the events generating their concepts, GA practitioners can reframe quantitative inquiry within a qualitative originary structure, making disciplinary dialogue possible and mutually enriching.

## Key Concepts

- [[Originary event]] — the founding human event that generates meaning and concepts
- [[Event]] — the fundamental unit of human reality; all human phenomena are events, not static structures
- [[Concept]] — emerges from originary events and frames what we attend to and can subsequently quantify
- [[Indwelling]] — Michael Polanyi's term for inhabiting a phenomenon from within to grasp its originary reality
- [[Disciplinary power]] — the authority to name events and establish conceptual frames
- [[Ritual]] — a concept that paradoxically emerges through distinguishing "our" practices from "rituals"

## Notable Passages

> "In the human sphere there are nothing but events. Ideas, and their reproduction and dissemination, are events. There are big events and small events."

> "We attend from a concept like 'ritual' to certain practices that we distinguish from other practices—on that basis, and only on that basis, can we go on to 'quantify' ritual."

> "Knowledge comes from creating events of shared observation of those events that made the event of knowledge possible."

## Questions Raised

1. How can the originary hypothesis become genuinely testable within existing disciplinary frameworks without collapsing into mere metaphor or speculation?

2. If all human phenomena are events, what distinguishes [[originary event]] methodologically from any other historical event we might analyze?

3. Does treating social-scientific concepts as "indwelling" ritual paradoxically reinscribe the very distinctions (ritual vs. non-ritual) that the originary approach seeks to dissolve?
