## Summary

This essay reframes human social and political organization through the lens of [[credit]] and [[debt]] rather than conventional categories like morality or culture. Drawing on Carl Wennerlind's history of English finance, the author argues that liberal democracy operates as an indirect civil war where parties damage each other's credit through central bank manipulation. The framework extends to art, ethics, and sovereignty itself, positioning the [[center]] or "pointman" as the enforcer and forgiver of debts—a role now distributed across intelligence agencies and financial institutions in the post-sacral modern world.

## Key Concepts

- [[Credit]]: The monetization of debt, replacing honor-based systems of obligation; the feedback mechanism measuring credibility
- [[Debt]]: The foundational human relation, originating in our obligation to the central being on the [[originary scene]]
- [[Center]] (or "pointman"): The sovereign occupant of the central position responsible for debt enforcement and forgiveness
- [[Liberal democracy]]: A system of deferred civil war conducted indirectly through credit manipulation rather than direct conflict
- [[Originary scene]]: The primal human gathering where the first sign emerges, establishing primordial debt to the center
- [[Sovereignty]]: The authority to determine which debts are enforced or forgiven, now distributed across intelligence and banking systems
- [[Central bank]]: The mechanism replacing the general pointman, operating independently of electoral politics
- [[Credibility]]: The assessment of whether a creditor or debtor will honor obligations; the target of political and cultural warfare

## Notable Passages

> "Liberal democracy encapsulates the logic of constantly broached and deferred civil war waged indirectly by making one's opponents casualties of the credit now determined by the central bank independently of either party."

> "Credit is the monetization of debt and succeeds previous forms of expressing obligation like honor—it is only when the central bank replaces the general 'pointman' that we have a credit order."

> "Intelligence agencies have many ways of wrecking credit when doing so might harm disfavored parties."

## Questions Raised

1. How do we measure or track "credit" in non-financial cultural domains—what would a systematic accounting of artistic or intellectual credibility actually look like?

2. If sovereignty is fundamentally about debt forgiveness and enforcement, what happens to the concept when these powers fragment across distributed systems (central banks, intelligence agencies, platforms) without a single identifiable center?

3. Does the extension of credit-logic to all human discourse risk collapsing the distinction between economic calculation and ethical judgment, or does it clarify what that distinction was always obscuring?
