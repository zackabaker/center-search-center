## Summary

This essay argues that foundational concepts in political and social discourse—justice, rights, equality, liberty—are not universal but embedded in specific languages and historical contexts. Drawing on Anna Wierzbicka's Natural Semantic Metalanguage and a lineage of 20th-century thinkers (Peirce, Kuhn, Polanyi, Wittgenstein), the author develops a theory of knowledge as constructed through collaborative, institutionalized spaces of shared attention. Rather than dismissing this as relativism, the piece shows how genuine discoveries can endure across traditions while remaining marked by their conditions of emergence. Crucially, social science concepts must originate in lived community experience before becoming objects of disciplinary inquiry.

## Key Concepts

- [[Semantic metalanguage]] — a framework showing how key terms (evidence, rights, justice) are linguistically embedded rather than universal
- [[Discipline]] — a collaborative institutional space where shared attention to defined objects generates knowledge
- [[Originary scene]] — the hypothesis grounding all genuine knowledge production in human collective meaning-making
- [[Institutional space]] — the formal or informal setting in which concepts circulate and acquire legitimacy
- [[Degradation]] — the process by which concepts, severed from the questions they answered, devolve into propaganda
- [[Value-laden concepts]] — terms (like "health," "king," "justice") that cannot be purged of evaluative content without losing meaning

## Notable Passages

> "Concepts are answers to specific questions, and once they circulate free of those questions they degrade into propaganda tools."

> "All knowledge is generated through collaborative spaces in which shared attention is paid to some object defined by the space itself."

> "In human affairs, the disciplinary space must continuously be distinguished from and set up against non-disciplinary spaces."

## Questions Raised

1. If social concepts must originate in lived community experience to remain meaningful, how can a discipline study communities radically different from its own without importing its own linguistic assumptions?

2. What distinguishes a genuine rediscovery of a concept across traditions from mere circular reinforcement of inherited linguistic biases?

3. How can we identify when a concept has sufficiently degraded into propaganda to warrant disciplinary reconstruction, and who decides?
