## Summary

This essay explores the constitutive relationship between [[center]] and [[origin]] in generative anthropology, arguing that every center carries a direct lineage to the originary scene and functions as an emergent origin itself. The author distinguishes between [[power]] (drawing on the origin) and [[authority]] (extending it forward), suggesting that legitimacy resides in the capacity of central figures to maintain the link between present scenes and historical precedent. When centers fail to issue commands that connect the scene to its history, those they govern either construct alternative centers or treat the failed leader as a negative indicator—a map of what should have been said.

## Key Concepts

- [[Origin]] – The primordial scene whose semiotic and declarative force legitimates all subsequent centers
- [[Center]] – Any position from which joint attention is organized; simultaneously emergent and continuous with origin
- [[Power]] – The capacity to draw upon and preserve the originary source
- [[Authority]] – The extension and articulation of power through imperative commands
- [[Deferral]] – The uniquely human capacity to defer appropriation, anchored in central mediation
- [[Legitimacy]] – The intrinsic relation between [[ostensive]] and [[imperative]] rooted in a community's specific origin
- [[Deceleratory moment]] – The ritual settling-in of the originary scene, often mistakenly isolated from the whole

## Notable Passages

> "Any center is fit out with a link to the origin—therein lies its power. Even more, any center is itself an origin, an ever emergent origin."

> "Everyone looks to the center to determine what to do, even if what to do is to defy or ridicule the center, because the central figure is telling you, in not so many words, that he has not inhabited or impersonated the resources the center provides for exercising the power of deferral."

> "If we need to make the distinction between 'power' and 'authority' we can say that 'power' draws upon the power of the origin while 'authority' carries it forward and extends it."

## Questions Raised

1. If every center—from a president to a substitute teacher—inherits legitimacy from the originary scene, what distinguishes genuine authority from mere occupancy of a position?

2. When a center "fails" and those around it construct negative indicators pointing to unspoken commands, are they truly creating new centers or retrieving older origins that the failed center obscured?

3. How does the essay's claim that origins "cannot be illegitimate" apply to revolutionary or usurpationist origins that explicitly reject prior authority structures?
