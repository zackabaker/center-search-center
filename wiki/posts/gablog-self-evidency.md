## Summary

This essay traces how the doctrine of the [[arbitrariness of the sign]]—the claim that linguistic signs bear no natural relation to their meanings—has enabled both classical liberalism and its contemporary leftist successor. Against this tradition stemming from Locke through Kant and Hegel, the author identifies an alternative Enlightenment lineage (Reid, Peirce, Arendt, Hayek, Wittgenstein, Polanyi) grounded in [[tacit knowledge]] and [[common sense philosophy]]. The core argument is that we know far more than we can articulate; language games and idioms necessarily take vast amounts for granted, and attempting to make the foundational bedrock explicit only pushes the problem elsewhere rather than solving it.

## Key Concepts

- [[Arbitrariness of the sign]] — The claim that linguistic signs have no necessary connection to their meanings, reducible to convention or imposition
- [[Tacit knowledge]] — Knowledge we possess but cannot fully make explicit or conscious; constitutive rather than merely latent
- [[Common sense philosophy]] — The epistemological tradition beginning with Thomas Reid that treats self-evident truths as genuine starting points
- [[Language game]] — A bounded domain of meaningful discourse that necessarily takes much for granted
- [[Social contract theory]] — The liberal framework assuming rational agreement on arbitrary conventions; shadowed by tyranny
- [[Natural law]] — The medieval/Christendom assumption that social order reflects deeper metaphysical realities, opposed by nominalism
- [[Idiom]] — A particular disciplinary or contextual framework within which knowledge-making occurs
- [[Self-evidence]] — What needs no external proof because the reality itself is the best evidence

## Notable Passages

> "The arbitrariness of the sign is liberalism in linguistics and, in the end, liberalism has shown itself to share enough genetic material with the Leftism that succeeded it so as to leave it almost devoid of antibodies to fight the Leftist infection."

> "We know far more than we know we know; that is, our knowledge is to a great extent, to use Polanyi's term, tacit—and not merely because we haven't yet brought it up to consciousness, but constitutively so."

> "What would be better proof of this reality than the reality itself, as Wittgenstein liked to say?"
