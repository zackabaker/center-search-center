## Summary

This essay distinguishes between [[victimary discourse]] and [[center]] discourse by analyzing their competing relationships to [[mimesis]], representation, and the [[declarative sentence]]. Victimary thinking rejects [[mimesis]] as inherently violent and suspects all representation (including the [[originary scene]]) as occlusion of primordial violence. Centered discourse, by contrast, operates through direct declaratives that reference a common, receding horizon. The author proposes that victimary and centered statements have structurally opposite forms—one self-cancelling through obligatory negations, the other embracing substantive claims with measured acknowledgment of complexity.

## Key Concepts

- [[Victimary discourse]] – Anti-mimetic thinking that resists the forgetting of originary violence through compulsory negations and scare quotes
- [[Declarative sentence]] – The linguistic form that makes absent objects present as signs; can be structured toward center or victimary ends
- [[Mimesis]] – Representation through imitation; victimary thought views it as inherently generative of violence
- [[Originary scene]] – The founding moment of culture; victimary discourse suspects it as cover-up rather than transcendence
- [[Obligatory negation]] – Pathological negation formulas ("I don't mean to suggest…") that betray fear of the sign itself absorbing the speaker
- [[Center]] – A common, receding horizon that grounds healthy declarative statements
- [[Ostensive]] – Direct pointing; like mimesis, feared by victimary discourse as occlusion of originary violence

## Notable Passages

> "Victimary discourse is fundamentally anti-mimetic: mimesis leads inexorably to violence, and victimary discourse has faith neither in Girard's Christian transcendence of the scapegoat nor a transcendent sign."

> "A declarative of the center would include in its formulation a common and ever receding horizon, while the victimary declarative would fix our attention on a rapidly approaching, even if never quite arriving, catastrophic object."

> "The obligatory negation…makes absent an object which one fears is present–the sign itself, which would absorb one, overwhelming all resistance, into the dreaded center."

## Questions Raised

1. Can the distinction between centered and victimary declaratives be empirically verified across cultural texts, or does it remain a hermeneutic frame imposed by the analyst?

2. Is Churchill's formulation genuinely "centered," or does its admission of democracy's deficiency already concede ground to victimary logic by foregrounding failure?

3. How does one speak from the center without appearing naive or complicit to victimary consciousness—is there a viable political rhetoric of centered statements?
