## Summary

All inquiry reduces to utterances on scenes, and every utterance is fundamentally citational—it summons prior utterances while creating new scenes. The author argues that treating any thing as a "sample" opens infinite fields of likeness across scenes, and that morality/ethics derive from legal and ritual practices rather than the reverse. Rather than seeking authentic authorial intent, productive interpretation tracks chains of citations through vast databases, understanding language as a system where everyone says the same thing to ensure we don't all act identically.

## Key Concepts

- [[Citationality]]: Language as mutual citation; all utterances cite and are cited
- [[Originary scene]]: The foundational mutual citations establishing language and ensuring differentiated action
- [[Sample]]: Any utterance or thing treated as citing other similar instances across innumerable scenes
- [[Scene]]: The context on which an utterance appears; inquiry always reduces to utterances on scenes
- [[Idiom]]: Each self-citing subject as an idiomatic intelligence, a particular way of speaking/being
- [[Sincerity]]: Not a moral fact but an interpretation of legal and ritual practices
- [[Declarative culture]]: The system where utterances establish reality rather than reflect it

## Notable Passages

"The more you singularize the utterance the more you fill out the scene and filling out the scene evokes and invokes other utterances on other scenes."

"Morality and ethics are interpretations of legal and ritual practices, rather than seeing law (or even ritual) as the implementation of a moral order."

"Every utterance or sample is citing and creating a scene for the reception of the citation of some other piece of writing."

## Questions Raised

1. If morality derives from legal/ritual practices rather than preceding them, how do we justify critiques of unjust legal systems without appealing to some prior moral standard?

2. How do we distinguish between productive citation-tracking through databases and mere "plagiarism detection" that might foreclose the very openness citationality requires?

3. What determines when a field of likenesses should be "closed off" to establish same/other distinctions—and who decides?
