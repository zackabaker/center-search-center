## Summary

This essay examines Eric Gans's argument that the internet is becoming a "one medium" that subsumes all prior media forms (text, video, music, cinema) into digital convertibility. While this technological convergence erodes the integrity of distinct media, two irreducible phenomena resist total subsumption: [[live performance]] and [[art-objects]], which depend on immediate relationships to their public. Drawing on [[generative anthropology|GA]]'s understanding of the [[sacred scene]]—the central object and its peripheral human witnesses—the text argues that certain human actions require genuine presence and cannot be fully simulated, even as digital reproduction threatens to make physical and performative scarcity economically precious.

## Key Concepts

- **[[One Medium]]** – The internet's capacity to digitally convert all prior media forms into a unified platform, eroding their distinct modes of presentation
- **[[Sacred Scene]]** – GA's model of the center (sacred object) surrounded by a peripheral community that celebrates and consecrates it
- **[[Live Performance]]** – Human action requiring immediate presence and mimetic interaction, irreducible to recorded simulation
- **[[Art-Object]]** – Physical works that take up space and resist reproduction, becoming scarce and aesthetically privileged in the one-medium era
- **[[Mimesis]]** – The human capacity for imitation and desire; the ground of meaningful interaction that simulation cannot fully replicate
- **[[Presence]]** – The irreducible requirement of certain human acts (ritual, performance) for their reality and efficacy
- **[[Scarcity]]** – The economic and aesthetic premium placed on phenomena resistant to digital reproduction

## Notable Passages

> "There remain two sets of phenomena that cannot be reduced to the One Medium because they depend upon an immediate relationship to their public: performances on the one hand, and art-objects on the other."

> "Since we are mimetic beings, human interaction grounds our world in a way simulation can't—Gans uses the example of chess, pointing out that we now have computers that can defeat any human in the game, and yet we still hold human tournaments while no one would have the slightest interest in a chess match between computer programs."

> "Going to a live concert and watching it later on the internet are not the same thing, but the performer may not do anything different in the live performance than he does in a performance directly recorded to be shown on the internet."

## Questions Raised

1. If [[live performance]] remains irreducible to the one medium because it requires mimetic presence, does this suggest a *counter-algorithmic praxis*—a deliberate resistance to digitization as a form of power or freedom?

2. How does the economic scarcification of [[art-objects]] and [[performance]] reshape cultural hierarchies and access in a one-medium world? Who benefits from the premium on unreproducible presence?

3. Can we theorize *intermediate* forms that partially resist digitization—live-streamed performances, hybrid events—or does any recording already compromise the irreducibility Gans identifies?
