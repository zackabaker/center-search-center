# Tutoring Notes: Center Study with Adam Katz

*These are Zack Baker's personal notes from tutoring sessions with Adam Katz, conducted December 2020 through March 2023. They are student notes — not transcriptions — and reflect Zack's understanding at the time of the session. Some sessions are detailed reconstructions; others are brief impressions. Errors of interpretation are Zack's, not Adam's.*

*The sessions span foundational concepts in Generative Anthropology and Center Study: the originary scene, mimetic desire and its deferral, exemplary victimhood, resentment, technics, regime analysis, and the political implications of originary thinking.*

## December 2020


### First Session: Exemplary Victims

*This session introduced the concept of the exemplary victim — the figure who exposes a community's moral bad faith and draws collective violence upon himself. It moved from the Axial Age prophets through Moses, Socrates, and Christ to the modern secularization of martyrdom, ending with a discussion of how center-focused thinking might offer a way past the dead end of victimary politics.*

- When someone comes along and points out that the morality is in "bad faith," he draws upon himself all of the attention and desire in the community. He points out the "irrationality" of the morality in the community, by exposing its sacrificial nature, and translating it.
- Confucius, Buddha, Socrates, Hebrew prophets all at the same time — this is called the Axial Age.
- Any figure who can bring the wrath of the central government upon them must be an elite. The person must have first of all been elevated. Must be worthy of imitation on the terms of the empire itself.
- A justice that implicates the entire system. It's not "in" the system, it's "of" the system.
- The exemplary victim is dispossessed, "turned into" a slave, which then brings a focus onto mass slavery itself. This person stands in for them.
- This gets replayed in so many different ways — the movie Spartacus. Or the movie Gladiator.
- Moses starts off as a prince, but then identifies with the slaves.
- Abraham as well has conflicts with these monarchs.
- The Bible is "a book of exile" — everyone is on the move. A kind of "exilic thinking."
- The Jews as an exemplary victim.
- Judaism, and Christianity as well, is a kind of "anti-imperial imperialism." God as King is superseding the actual kings around them.
- The very notion of oppression itself gets constructed out of something like that. This is where the moral critique can *come from*. Before that, you may not have liked it, but oppression wouldn't have been able to be thought.
- Christ starts somewhat plebeian, but becomes elevated over the course of the gospels.
- Confucius and Buddha don't get sacrificed. But Socrates and Jesus do. There's something distinctly Western about this. It's because they rubbed against these empires in some way and so had to think about social hierarchies in a way that stood above those empires.
- Question: why didn't this happen in the east? What are the historical ways of doing this?
- In a sense it's a kind of historical accident. We know very little about the ancient Israelite republic, whatever it was. We can assume there was some nation there; they were conquered, brought into exile, they did return. Here, in this one case, a God was somehow remembered, and maintained, and used as moral and intellectual leverage. Maybe they weren't in exile for very long, maybe only a couple of generations.
- Even the idea of a "chosen people" — this notion of "choosing" — would come from sacrificing the "best of your flock." The chosen people are modeled after the flock itself. It is in some sense a non-imperial model, a nomadic model.
- China on the other hand is much more organized imperially from an early period on. The nomads were always on the outside of an empire. India similar. So these specificities would have a way of leveraging certain traditions against the empire.
- Greeks were a bunch of city-states against the empire. And so those wars against Persia were very formative. Certain understandings of freedom and democracy emerged from that.
- There's a lightning rod that gets attacked, everyone congregates around them — on the left and right. It's the only way of thinking about politics in the West.
- The problem is that it's a destructive, dead-end way of thinking. You end up commemorating and celebrating martyrs. It's always commemorating someone. There's a victim who reveals the real essence of the system.
- So then that raises the problem of how you can think outside of this. You don't want to totally discredit it because it's easy to imagine something much worse. In some sense, this is a moral acquisition that should be preserved and articulated in new ways. Even to say it's a sham or fraud would again be getting caught up in the same resentment. Even to deconstruct it, you'd have to do it in a way that doesn't just reproduce it.
- The alternative way of thinking about it is to think in terms of the center. The exemplary victim is always discrediting the center; the center is always inhibited. There's always a new exemplary victim coming down the line. So you always have this moral superiority, a kind of historical superiority. This is apocalyptic thinking. One day, history will prove you right in a cosmic course of justice.
- So the very difficult question is how you transcend this — but it springs back. Look at this guy, look at that guy, look what they're doing to us.
- It becomes an aesthetic problem: you have to represent these victimizations while removing the implicit indictment.
- There's a question of whether you're trying to derive clearer derivations from the system.
- The exemplary victim is Katz's further thinking through of the victimary politics of our day. Jesus became a model for the romantics first of all. You have a secularization of the individual ostracized and sacrificed. Through the Middle Ages it's confined within Christian doctrine and Christian ritual. Up until the Enlightenment, no individual would have represented himself as Jesus-like. Nobody would have represented themselves that way — sees all mankind as united against me. *The Catcher in the Rye*. The one who sees how phony everyone else is is the one who gets persecuted. This is a very powerful way of seeing yourself. One can see oneself as authentic as opposed to everyone else who's simply against them and acting based on convention. This is very powerful marketing. This is a classic trope. James Dean.
- The way activists and journalists talk about themselves — standing up for those who can't speak up for themselves. Very empowering. Someone who's willing to risk it all. The Nike slogan. It's very powerful marketing. You have to keep raising the stakes. Our victims have to be better than their victims. This is why the right has to find a way of representing Blacks as victims — can't let the left have a monopoly — hence "dems are the real racists." You have to compete fiercely in democratic politics and find new ways of representing people as victims. Competing for victims. Liberal politics and American politics are founded on this from the very beginning — defending natural rights from an overbearing empire that doesn't recognize them. All the heroes of the American revolution are heroes who gave up almost everything to defend those rights. Victimary thinking is playing this all the way out.
- To root out victimary thinking you'd somehow have to separate out what is genuinely, sacramentally Christian from those who can out-Christian the Christians. The Enlightenment.
- The Galileo myth is a way to turn the Christian discourses against the Christians.
- The Enlightenment says: we're following our own rationality, our own minds, and that makes us the enemies of the Christian establishment.
- All the churches have bought into this as well — the Catholics, the Protestants, the Evangelicals. None of their discourses can provide immunity.
- You can construct things in a lot of different ways if you start with a blank-slate ideology. Then every disparity is evidence of oppression. If you have an explanation that puts you on the spot, the only other explanation would immediately indict you — which immediately makes you a part of the structural system. It's a double bind. And this goes back to the logic of scapegoating. The double bind goes both ways.
- Being stereotyped is in a way being scapegoated. How do you disprove that? If you don't, the reading stands; and if you do disprove it, that itself more fully exposes that you are this other. When directed towards an individual it's a kind of scapegoating. But when you turn it around and say "you're the one stereotyping me" — once that becomes an act of violence, the double bind goes the other way. How do you prove you're not racist?
- Going back to the 1960s we begin to see all of these paradoxes. In a sense there's no way out of it.
- Katz doesn't think Girard believed Jesus literally ascended to heaven, but he also wouldn't want to debunk that. Have to be cautious about how literally you want to take it. Girard saw Christianity as the answer.
- Gans has a different approach. For Gans it's liberal democracy and the market. In a sense it disperses things, so that attention never really focuses on a single figure. Since everyone is both separated and connected through the market and a system of exchange — Girard says this in *Innovation and Repetition* — that somehow allows for peaceful rivalry and peaceful competition where nobody gets killed; things just get exchanged. You want Musk and Bezos competing for space rockets, not acting as imperial warlords.
- Reading Gans in the '90s he's pretty optimistic; he thinks the market is going to gradually erode and dissolve these victimary commitments. You can see he doesn't give up on it altogether, but maybe we need more market — he's getting more alarmed as well.
- You can do an analysis of the market and liberal democracy and see how it intensifies victimary thinking. One's support of the victimary victim of the day marks you as more saleable.
- Katz came across a book on Sacred Kingship by Francis Oakley where he makes the case that sacred kingship is the political common sense of mankind. It's the most common form of government in mankind if you take a wide enough view of things — do the math.
- The initial sacrifice.
- Girard sees scapegoating on the originary scene, but Gans rejects that. Part of Gans's argument against that is that you don't see human sacrifice among the early hunter-gatherer communities. Human sacrifice comes much later and is a feature of these larger empires. The Hebrew Bible can be read as one long argument against human sacrifice.
- But if human sacrifice comes much later, the first humans to be sacrificed must have been the kings. That's the only sacrifice that would have mattered.
- You have the Big Man, the first human to occupy the center. He's not a king yet, but you will have sacral kings. Mircea Eliade talks about every community having this center-margin relationship.
- The sacred king mediates between the community and the divine. That's at one point an extremely powerful position and also an extremely vulnerable one. This must have happened quite often. This is really the first scapegoating — the first scapegoat is the king.
- Once we see that the most powerful form of scapegoating is the central figure, you see this in Greek tragedies — it's the king, not the random guy on the street. The king brings all the antagonisms of the community onto him.
- But once you say that, you can also say that scapegoatings that seem more focused on marginal victims are just an indirect way of scapegoating the central figure as well.
- In a way, the defense of the marginal figure is a way of scapegoating the central figure itself. Once we turn that around, the person occupying the center is the person who is being scapegoated.
- There's then a moral revolution: the center is not to be scapegoated. That takes the sting out of all the liberal and democratic discourse, which is all predicated on making the center guilty of all these things. Once you say it can't be guilty — it can just be more or less coherent — then the whole thing changes.
- There can be bad presidents, bad kings, people who brutalize their people. Instead of seeing the tyrant as someone who needs to be sacrificed to have true freedom or true democracy —
- You can see that more as the center being insecure. But you can also see a center that's perfectly secure and also happens to be a sadist. You can't exclude that possibility. But that doesn't really seem to be the main problem when someone in charge behaves in a way that undermines the community. It's not really personal sadism; it's more not knowing who your enemies are. Various enemies are ideologically justified. There's always a way to find the ruler as illegitimate; you have to give one group human rights against another group. You end up ultimately ruining things one way or another.
- The real problem is making sure that all these basically arbitrary ways of delegitimating rulers are discredited, made ridiculous, refuted. This would be a revolution in thinking. The most immoral thing you can do is delegitimate a ruler. That would be how you get rid of victimary thinking.

### Second Session

*This session covered the foundations of Generative Anthropology — its debts to Girard and Derrida, the question of the origin of language, and the concept of divine agency on the originary scene. It also explored how deferral, the speech-act sequence, and the notion of the center bear on ethics, technics, and the emergence of rights discourse in modernity.*

- GA comes from Girard and Derrida.
- Deferral is the notion Gans takes from Derrida.
  - For Derrida there is no anthropological dimension to deferral. It's a kind of infra-linguistic process.
- Origin is a very old question. In a way it doesn't belong to anyone.
- In a sense, the origin of language is one of those questions that got forbidden because for them it could only really lead to crackpot answers.
- Rousseau tried to answer the origin of humanity, the origin of language. Then there are some 19th-century thinkers who try to answer this as well. There are some other French Enlightenment philosophers who tried to come up with an origin of language.
- There's a kind of built-in ethics or morality in GA. Once you say the origin of humanity — or the origin of language — is the deferral of violence, then you're saying humanity is good; it's better that it exists than that it doesn't. Deferral of violence is the first Good.
  - You have to get specific about this though. It could easily make GA sound like a pacifism, as if stopping violence all the time is the best thing you could do ethically. That's obviously not the case.
  - It's a very specific kind of violence that is the real danger. Part of ethical thinking would be distinguishing between these two forms of violence.
  - It's not just people hitting each other, but "the way the violence is framed" in some scenic way.
  - There are all kinds of violence that could play themselves out without being contained.
  - Two kids might start fighting in a playground and there'd be big kids around who'd form a circle around the fighting kids. It already keeps it contained. And they basically let them fight it out until it "goes too far." So it's not necessarily stopping the violence as much as framing or containing it.
  - Lots of cultural institutions serve this purpose.
  - Rivalries in a sense need to play themselves out.
  - There's a stuntman in LA named Derek Jacobis who communicated with Gans a few years ago. He's an interesting figure and wrote a few essays — worth checking his website.
  - He wrote some essays on historicizing staged violence.
  - He goes through all the ways violence has been staged and framed and represented and contained.
  - Even non-violence — what leads up to violence.
  - Like maybe the competitions and rivalries that would have led to violence now have people make products, and maybe there's less violence.
  - Maybe it doesn't work, but it's a good question to ask: how does this social institution serve to frame and contain violence?
- Violence
  - It's not exactly clear what should count as violence and what shouldn't.
  - Once we say violence is wrong, people have a lot of interest in coming up with convincing narratives to define violence in ingenious ways.
  - Natural Semantic Primes are important because Anna Wierzbicka makes this argument that there are all these words we think to be universal or to have a general meaning across all languages, and they really don't. This is important to her, mainly because of English.
  - She's really interested in the way that all these English terms become privileged legal terms.
  - Terms like evidence, fairness, etc. She traces a lot of them back to Locke, and the way that Locke changed the meaning of words and they became part of the English language and the liberal tradition.
  - And this in a sense offends her. She sees it as a kind of violence.
  - She's pointing out a kind of imperialism.
  - She's trying in a sense to relativize English.
  - Violence is actually a term she mentions. Physical force being used for no real reason — brute physical force. There's not a word for this in other languages. There's actually a "de-moralizing." There would always be a moral component to the way we use the word violence in English.
  - So if you want a universal theory you have to be very careful about what words you're using.
  - The discipline Katz has adopted has been to use English terms and make sure that you can translate them back.
  - In the seminar on deferral he uses the terms "same" and "other." These are primes — universal terms. On the originary scene everyone is becoming more and more the same.
  - The problem is differentiation.
  - The only way to produce difference is to force someone to do something that makes them different from you.
  - You have to use direct force against them.
  - To make someone other is to use violence.
  - It then becomes possible for there to be a need for differentiation.
  - Convergence on sameness vs. needs for differentiation.
  - Then it shifts the discussion to whether violence is necessary and how you distinguish whether it is or not.
  - For example, when you put down a riot.
  - The distinction used to be a lot clearer, but violence is used much more often now in relation to lawlessness.
  - Maybe the shift starts with Max Weber who says the state has a monopoly on violence.
  - If you talk about the police putting down a riot — usually you wouldn't really say the police used "violence"; you'd say it was "force." The rioters were using "violence."
  - The distinction used to be lawful vs. lawless. Not sure if that still stands. Could be a really interesting study.
- Originary Violence
  - Should be understood in terms of differentiation.
  - Center is the Other.
  - And then everyone can be differentiated in relation to the Center which is the Other.
- Divine Agency
  - Gans is very good on this in the early chapters of *The End of Culture*.
  - We have everyone configured around the central object.
  - Everyone puts forward the gesture of aborted appropriation (GoAA).
  - In a sense, they're being "held" in what amounts to a kind of almost paralysis. It might be momentary, but they're all deferring each other — yet for each of them, they can't run that calculation in their own mind. These hominids are not all running the calculations about who's going to beat whom to the object or why they're pausing.
  - For them — for everyone there — it has to be as if the central object is telling them to stop. It's the most minimal way to think about it. It's a prohibition or interdiction coming from the central object. If they're all thinking "I'd go for it if it wasn't for him, or him" — it wouldn't work. They wouldn't stop. They'd still be measuring themselves against each other. They wouldn't really stop.
  - To really experience this, the newly created significant object has to be "telling them." And they couldn't say that — they don't have words yet obviously — but it's telling them to stop. That's the only way to understand it.
  - The central object telling them to stop.
  - The first agency is that of the central being. All agency after that is modeled on that central being.
  - For any human to take agency would have taken a very long time. Everything they're doing is serving the center in some way.
  - Anyone who has a reason for doing something, at least in proximity to the center, would find that reason in the center telling them to do it.
  - Gans in *Originary Thinking* talks about the difference between the group being organized around the center on the one hand.
  - So what's the situation when they're going about their everyday business — like going on a hunt? Are they doing it normally and then there's a ritual scene? Or does the hunting itself become an extension of the ritual scene? Even if it doesn't happen immediately — now there's a method.
  - Any time they get into a conflict out hunting, they have the sign; they would use the sign.
  - And if they have the sign, they're referring back to the authority of the ritual scene.
  - And then however they go on the hunt — all the new leadership dynamics — that feeds back into the configuration. It's deposited into the community. The way that would be represented would be building up the ritual scene in one way or another. They might re-enact the hunt at the ritual scene. The scene gets richer. And as they come to have language, they start telling stories at the ritual scene.
  - And for Gans, this is the origin of mythology. These dramas happen at the center. Whatever happened at the hunting party — these become agents of the center telling us what to do each time we hunt.
- And this is where Katz differs from Gans. Katz doesn't think there can ever be a social order without a center.
  - If we trace that through the development of speech acts: ostensive → imperative → declarative.
  - Once there's a center that subsists beyond the first animal, we have the distinction between the signifying center and the occupying center.
  - That place is there to put the animal. There is a center even when there is nothing in it. So then the relation between the group becomes one of exchange.
  - So you bring the animal to the center. And you're giving "part of the deer" to the Deer Father. The animals you hunt are basically your ancestors.
  - These early hunter-gatherers' ancestors are always animals or human-animal mixtures.
  - Someone speaks for the center.
  - What would make that compelling? There would have to be some kind of crisis, and someone would resolve the crisis and re-enact the originary scene. And if you do that, and you resolve the crisis, then that was what the center wanted you to do.
  - Even today, this is still how we tend to think about things.
- You're doing something — but "I wanted to do it" is never a satisfactory answer to anyone else. It's never a justification. Doing something to accord with some principle or precedent or authority is drawing upon the agency of something that was there before you. Something that was backing you up. Something that might support you. Something that others would recognize. Agency is always derivative.
  - If people say "it's only fair that we do it this way" and other people accept that, it's because there's some shared model of fairness that everyone in the group accepts. And it has to pre-exist everyone; there is some precedent for it.
  - There's always some kind of event we're projecting back to.
  - This is where all notions of fairness or justice, or notions like right or even efficiency, would come back to.
  - There can't ever really be anything outside of that.
  - When you individualize things and just say "well, I wanted to do it" — on the face of things there's a kind of resentment towards the center. You're resisting by saying "I don't abide by that expectation." That has its own power.
  - That becomes a weapon in struggles over the center.
  - This is what happens in modernity.
  - The way you break up communal and collective modes of authority.
  - You make the claim that someone has legitimate interests that are not included in it.
  - And then if you sponsor the person taking that resistance —
  - That begins to support another kind of center.
  - A center that breaks up institutions. A center that supports other forms of authority.
  - But then once there's some concept like rights and a right that hasn't been met —
  - Every new extension of rights just gives the state more power over other institutions.
- Chris Bond's book *Nemesis* reads everything, including the ancient world, with this in mind and gets some very interesting results.
- There is this question of what comes after sacral kingship.
- Once you have this kind of anti-imperial imperialism — monotheism and philosophy, which do something similar — once you have a kind of authority that transcends earthly authority, that judges earthly authority —
- All of these kings, Nebuchadnezzar. They're all going to be judged by God in the end and his people will be vindicated. It's a very juridical kind of language — God is the judge who will judge these earthly judges.
- At that point you can say: this king has total earthly power, but God disapproves of it, and we know what God disapproves of. And then you can keep translating that because it opens the door — you can translate that into justice and rights and humanity and individuality.
- This is super-sovereignty.
- The Axial Age was a moral advance of enormous importance.
- The Bible puts an end to human sacrifice. And this was the only way to do it. And so you have to accept that history has to go through these crooked roads. Solving one form of violence leaves open the possibility of other forms. It's never a question of going back and trying to restore sacral kingship. The king is killed in sacral kingship and then you have to bring your firstborn to be killed — the Aztecs were just a heightened form of that. But that was prevalent in the ancient world. The Hebrew Bible is resisting that at every point along the way.
- Sodom and Gomorrah: if you can find one good man.
- The really dangerous scapegoating is against the central ruler. The point of democracy is somehow to call the president or prime minister all kinds of names and accuse them of all kinds of crimes — and this is like your basic right as a citizen, or you're some kind of drone or robot.
- The agency that counts comes from preserving the center.
- What happens in the New Testament that doesn't happen in the Old is that God gives himself up as a sacrifice. Once God comes down and becomes a human being, any human sacrifice that comes after would in a sense be sacrilegious. It would be denying that this was the sacrifice to end all sacrifices.

  - Girard said "well, we're all the Son of God."

If you're coming from a more skeptical tradition, a more Enlightenment tradition, you want to hunt down anything with supernatural implications. You want to keep secularizing the whole thing. Like Jesus appearing on the road to Damascus.

- In an oral culture, the line between a psychological and a "literal" event is much less clear.
- If you go back to a certain point, trying to draw that line is not very appropriate or useful.
- If it was something like a borderline hallucination and hearing voices — even a schizophrenic-like experience —
- It reveals something that couldn't have been proven in any other way. And if that testimony works for others and reverses certain resentments and practices they had, then that proves the truth of it to them. It's a "chain of testimony," and that's actually scientific in a way.

### But What About Patriarchy?

*This session addressed the structural logic of victimary grievance discourse — examining why complaints about systemic racism, patriarchy, and related constructs resist ordinary frameworks of remedy, and how the concept of the "sovereign imaginary" offers a more productive mode of political thinking.*

- But the very fact that you're pointing this out means something has changed. Because nobody was pointing this out during the height of this oppression.

- In a sense the answer they would give is that every member of every single group [incomplete].

  - So they draw on these categories that have reciprocity, but they remove reciprocity from them.

As soon as two people are talking, and they disagree, and there's a little bit of good faith on both sides, they're going to find a need for a reference point outside of themselves. If you can do this, the whole victimary system doesn't work anymore, because that's exactly what center-phobia is breaking down. All those words — "adjudication," "reference point" — are a center. Someone in the social order who can gather, conserve, and organize information that different people can kind of agree is connected to reality in some way.

- The desire to "move to a commune" is simply the desire to restore a center.
- If there's no central order, people will create their own orders on a local level.

#### The Sovereign Imaginary

  - You're always presupposing a center.
  - Think about everything you'd want to imagine in a society.
  - Then imagine the central power that would be necessary.
  - Then imagine if the central power capable of that would do exactly what they want.
  - Now imagine that that central power might do other things as well.
  - Once everyone accepts that, then the discussion shifts to:
  - Regardless of what people want, what is the central power that is coherent and transparent?
  - You can't say you want anything done, unless you can imagine a social order where things get done.
  - Then we can ask what exactly that would be.
  - Otherwise what are you doing when you say you want this or that?
  - And then you can ask: how can I act in such a way as to make the central authority we have right now approximate that?

#### How does one justify the minimality? Occam's razor / minimization of mystery

  - For Gans at least there is this modern scientific logic.
  - You want to test something — how does A affect B?
    - But the world is complex.
    - If you want to find the effect of A upon B you have to eliminate everything from C onward.
    - So you have to put them in a particular relationship with each other.
    - For the physical sciences this has worked really well.
    - You can do this in physics in a controlled space.
  - It's an attempt to think along those lines.
  - There's always some fundamental principle of the social order that you can take as your starting point.
  - There's always a founding event.

And so then you'd have to imagine what a *part of a meaningful sign* would be. What would be half meaningful but not completely meaningful? But you can't really think of it that way. It can't be one-fifth of a sign. It either is or it isn't. So then it has to be an event.

The more factors you introduce, the more confusion you introduce. If you say maybe there was a violent situation, but someone wanted to point out where the food was, and someone else wanted to direct everyone's attention to the sun, and you just start throwing things in like that — you're thinking in a very disorganized way and you can't arrive at a conclusion.   - It could have easily happened before they killed the victim, he realized.
  - If Gans hadn't had Girard he never would have thought about it. Girard's own hypothesis was an enormous accomplishment. Girard could only have appeared in a secular order where you could see what was at the heart of all of it.

### Fourth Session

#### Straussianism

#### The Nature of Metaphysics

  - Gans claims that metaphysics is taking declarative language for granted.
  - For Gans, the opposition to metaphysics would be scenic thinking — considering everything you say as taking place on some scene.
    - But Katz has this critique: Gans has a critique of metaphysics but he's still thinking metaphysically. You can state a model in declarative terms and the world might conform to that model.

    - And thinks of itself as the next step to get out of metaphysics.
    - Even with Locke, he thinks if we just start with empirical reality, then we'll get out of metaphysics.
    - Same with Kant, and then even with Derrida.

  - Considering what you say or write could always be taken into other possible scenes.

    - When you say something, what that means is going to be different every time it gets repeated.
    - So you have no controlling authority over the meaning of what you say.
    - That undermines metaphysics.
    - Metaphysics needs a "meta-language" — some kind of a priori agreement. But this would be impossible because if so it'd be a scene-event.
    - You need something everyone agrees on because it's obviously true. So it leads to this quest for certain statements or certain rules for making statements.
    - And once you bust through that, now you're working outside of metaphysics.
  - Philosophy always assumes an individual thinker.
    - Always seems to be thinking about thought.
    - An individual thinker, grounding himself as an individual thinker.
    - Which includes being on a scene.
    - So you're in a conversation, or in some kind of situation where a decision is being made.
  - One thinker who in a sense really got past metaphysics — Charles Sanders Peirce.
    - He made thinking collective and experimental.
    - There's always a community of inquirers.
    - Even if you're the "only one" thinking about something, you're still thinking along with all those who were thinking and speaking in ways that enabled you to think that, and those that might under different conditions pick up that thing and do something with it.
    - So you're always in some kind of disciplinary space.
    - To understand what you're doing, you need to understand what questions are being asked and what hypotheses are being posed.

    - Or anything in between.

    - And so you're now past metaphysics in a less dramatic way. This is it — finally getting to the North Pole or something.

  - Olson focuses particularly on these speech-act verbs: state, propose, assume, etc.
  - If you think about why you would say something like "I assumed he would be there" —
  - You can kind of account for someone not being there and also for me having expected him to be there. I unfortunately or incorrectly expected him to be there.
  - Discourses like philosophy try to "figure out" these metalinguistic terms. They reify and fetishize them and in a sense make them real.
  - McLuhan said that Kant was "sleepwalking through literacy."
  - Kant treats these concepts about language as things that must be controlled.
  - But this is also metaphysics.
  - We can't do away with the metalanguage of literacy, but we need to understand it.

  - So this takes out of commission so many arguments like "is there really truth?" —
    - Well, the question is settled by the fact that the word "true" is found in every language.
    - So I'm willing to skip all the philosophical debates — the word "true" means something and you can use it in a meaningful way.
  - So this wipes out all of the metaphysical questions.
  - If the declarative doesn't come first, it must be the ostensive and the imperative.
  - So what would it mean to speak as if the ostensive were the primary linguistic form? And then from the ostensive, the imperative?
    - So whenever you use the declarative, your analysis cannot be in terms of other declaratives. But that would be analyzing it philosophically or metaphysically.
    - The definition of a word is literally turning the word into a declarative sentence.
    - And so you break down a discourse into another discourse in declaratives.
  - So instead of doing it that way and getting stuck in metaphysics, the other way of analyzing a discourse would be through the imperatives and ostensives that are embedding and carrying it forward.
  - Once you say that, when you make a declarative, what kind of imperative follows? What is that statement telling me to do?
  - If there's a fact, it means that someone along the way saw something and recorded it, and you're trusting them and the person that trusted that person and so on.
    - You're putting your faith in a chain of ostensive verifications. And you yourself can't go back and authenticate it.

### Fifth Session: Technics

*This session took up the question of technics, which Gans left underdeveloped. Drawing on Mumford, Marx, and especially Stiegler, it traced technology's origins in the desacralization of communal labor and developed the argument that technics is grounded in the imperative speech act — the temporal structure that makes tools, habits, and machines possible.*

- Gans has nothing to say about it.
- Katz felt that Gans took a naive view of technics. He thought it was something about efficiency, or that technology was about making things more cheaply.
- But the whole idea that we're situated in new ways because of a world that's been remade by technology — that's what Katz has been working on.
- Mumford is the person that Katz really had in mind. Although Marx as well, and Marx's development of factory labor by capital. He traces this process of tasks being broken up into smaller and smaller tasks.
- So the more you simplify tasks the more they can be replaced by automation.
- The first machines were huge amounts of humans. Mega-machines. Humans turned into parts to send into war or to carry out irrigation work. Or to build huge temples. Whatever "public works" projects the ancient emperors — pre-Axial Age — would have carried out.
- Technology has its origins in ripping humans out of situated communal situations where the work that they do is part of a ritual setting, and instead desacralizing that and conscripting them into enormous labor armies. And in a sense the modern capitalist process replays that.
- There was a lot of technological development during the Middle Ages, and in order to get the capitalist machinery going you had to rip people from the land. Dispossess them, send them into cities, where they had no way to feed themselves, and then put them into factories where you can divide up the labor more and more precisely.
- And this transforms the intellect as well, because it opens up the possibility of analysis and synthesis that wouldn't have been conceivable before.
  - Atomism of course goes back to the ancient world. This is one of the first philosophical discoveries in a certain sense.
  - But this idea that you can take any particular practice or goal and break it down into ten parts that could ultimately be synthesized — and then break down those parts —
  - That introduces a whole new set of intellectual possibilities as well.
  - The whole world can be broken down and reconstructed again.
  - It opens up new ambitions and visions. And you couldn't turn back on that even if you wanted to.
- If we're going back to grammar — ultimately we can derive everything from this ostensive, imperative, interrogative sequence.
- Katz was reading Bernard Stiegler, who speaks about the idea of externalization.
- With animals, there is the genetic program. And then as the animals get more complex you get a kind of program of activity — operational sequences. Like a caterpillar has certain "sequences" or "instincts."
- It confronts a particular situation and there is a particular set of things it can do. And as those animals get more complex their operational sequences get more complex and varied.
- The human, for Stiegler, is the animal that "externalizes its operational sequences" — i.e., its sequences are things that outlast any specific sequence. So technics are tools, or memories, that outlast any specific sequence.
- It embeds anticipation. You construct something that you think you'll need at a later time.
- Animals might do this when they gather nuts, but they can't really vary it or develop a new version of it.
- Humans can. We can materialize our anticipation. So you also need memory. And that for Stiegler is the origin of technics.
- And he doesn't really say why that happens. Stiegler is working with Heidegger.
- What accounts for time, what accounts for memory? It's the imperative.
- When you point at something, you construct a presence. You're freezing time in a sense. The declarative also does this. It's a virtual ostensive. The object that we would eventually be pointing to is not there, but if we understand each other you know that what could ultimately be pointed to — if we were in front of it — is there. Even the simplest declarative: "James is at the store now." There is always a potential ostensive; what you're saying is true if we could immediately go to the store. So there's always that ostensive guarantee — something you could point to.
- Where the imperative is always intrinsically temporal. It could be two seconds, but it could be ten years. It could be millennia.
- So technics is going to be found in the imperative, because the imperative is what gets things done.
- There's something about technology that compels. Machines are built to force something. You're telling one material to submit itself to this process. And one part of the process is telling another part to do this.
- It's a sequence of imperatives, and you basically lay down the tracks for it.
- So you're telling the train: you're going this way and no other way. And when you're in front of a machine, it's telling you what to do. It gives you commands all the time. The whole thing is set up in an imperative manner.
- Once you get past certain notions of freedom —
  - Liberal notions of freedom are like not having to obey an imperative.
  - You can basically define freedom for liberalism in those terms.
  - So if you're not obeying an imperative, you imagine that instead you're following your own reasoning: "I do what I do because I thought it through." But if we look at that, we're going to find imperatives there as well, and all these constraints you're either taking for granted or relying upon. Or you're following some instinct or desire which is ultimately some kind of imperative — some biological imperative, for example.
- Once we set all of that aside, we realize our lives are filled with imperatives. They aren't always imposing, threatening commands.
- Every imperative is in some sense imagining or projecting a condition that that very statement wants to bring into being.
- Everything we do can be reduced to commands, requests, pleadings.
- A habit is like an imperative you give yourself over and over again until you basically automate yourself.
- A machine is basically an interlocking set of imperatives.
- What's so interesting about algorithms is that all the speech acts are there in algorithms.
- After hundreds of thousands of years of being separated, they all come back together.
- "If this is the case, do this."
- "This is the case" — declarative.
- "If" — imperative.
- If it runs, something shows up — ostensive.
